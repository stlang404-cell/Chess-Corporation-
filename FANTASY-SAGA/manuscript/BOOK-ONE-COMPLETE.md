# The Forger of Verity
*The Long Survey, Book One*
by Sage Lang


# Part One: The Walk


## Chapter 1: A Very Confident Opinion

The hedge moved at a quarter past two in the morning, and it did not want to.

Ilse Marrow felt it refuse before she heard it. A pressure gathered behind her ears, the way it did before thunder, and the ground under her knees went as taut as a drumskin. In the shuttered lantern’s single slot of light, the wet charter lay on her board with its ink still shining. The wren’s-foot flourish of her signature sat over the faint violet underline that wasn’t hers. Nine hours of work. Forty-one stations, two hundred and twelve measured lengths, every angle checked twice by sightvane and once more by spite. It was a very good map of Widow Tull’s orchard strip, accurate to a link except in one particular.

In that particular, the hawthorn hedge on the west boundary stood two rods farther west than it actually did.

“Come on,” Ilse whispered. “You know you want to.”

The hedge did not know any such thing. It had stood where it stood since Haysel, when Lord Saffery’s surveyor had walked this parish with his chainmen and his fine leather case, re-charted the whole of Wick Fallow in nine days, and filed the result with the Concordance Court. The Court had stamped it, and the hedge had moved east in a single night, taking thirty-three feet of the widow’s land and four of her apple trees with it. Corwin Fask’s charter was legal and attested, and it had been filed before anyone else’s. To the ground it was simply the truth, and the ground had obeyed it.

Ilse’s charter was a different truth. It was better made. That was the whole trick.

She laid her palm flat on the sheet, the way old Quince had taught her, and leaned her attention into it. She didn’t push. Pushing was for amateurs and drunks. She *agreed* with the map, the way you agree with a fact you have looked at for so long it has become furniture. The hedge stands here. Of course it does. Where else would it stand? The soil there is better drained. The roots have always wanted the slope. Look at the old ditch line under the grass, look at the stump where the first planting failed, and see how the land itself remembers a boundary two rods west. It isn’t a lie. It’s a very confident opinion about where a hedge ought to be.

The pressure in her ears turned into a ringing, then a single high note, and the hedge gave.

It began as a sound like a long breath drawn through teeth: roots, hundreds of them, sliding through wet earth. Then the thorn branches scraped against each other with a dry rattle, the sound of a crowd shifting its feet. A blackbird burst from the hedge-bottom shrieking. Ilse lifted the lantern shutter a finger’s width and watched forty yards of hawthorn walk.

It didn’t topple or uproot. It *slid*, the whole green wall of it sliding level through the dark, as if the field beneath were a tablecloth and someone very patient were drawing it toward them. Leaves trembled and settled. A sparrow’s nest rode along on a fork, untroubled. Somewhere along the run a stone grated and turned over. Two rods is thirty-three feet, and it took the hedge about as long to travel as it took Ilse to say the Ninefold’s Grace under her breath, which she did not believe in and recited anyway. Then it was finished, and the hedge stood still in its new place as though it had always been there. The ground let go of her ears, and a cold sweat broke over her whole body at once.

She sat back on her heels. Her head was splitting. Her right hand, the signing hand, ached from the knuckles to the wrist with the deep, grinding ache she thought of as the bill.

Behind her, at the cottage door, a voice said, “Oh, my word,” and then, more practically, “Is it done, dearie?”

“It’s done, Mrs. Tull.”

Agnes Tull came down the garden path in a nightcap and three shawls, carrying a candle in one hand and a basket in the other. She was seventy-four and walked like a woman who had stopped falling over through sheer resentment. She held the candle up toward the hedge and peered at it, and then at the four apple trees now standing on the right side of it, their leaves silver in the light.

“There’s my Pearmain,” she said. “There’s my old Pearmain back. And my Russet. Oh, you lovely girl.”

“I’m not lovely. I’m expensive.”

“Three marks and the eggs, like we said.” She pressed the basket on Ilse and then, before Ilse could stop her, pressed her dry old cheek against Ilse’s forehead as well. “Your father would have been proud.”

He wouldn’t, Ilse thought. Daun Marrow had believed in the Court and the Atlas and the Ninefold’s Grace until the day those things took his well, and then he hadn’t believed in anything at all, and three years after that he had died. But she said thank you, because Agnes Tull was seventy-four and had her Pearmain back, and that was worth a lie.

She packed in the dark by touch. The chain went into its canvas bag link by link so it wouldn’t jangle, a hundred links, each one seven inches and ninety-two hundredths. Then the sightvane and the folding rule. The levelglass went into its sock, its little bead of quicksilver swimming lazily now that the land was quiet again. Last came the inks: the common black for the visible lines, and the small corked bottle of what Quince had called *the blind*, a pale gall-ink cut with alum and a whisper of quicksilver, which dried to nothing on paper and showed only under a damp lens. With it, under her own flourish, she had traced the mark of a man who had been dead three years.

That was the part even Agnes Tull didn’t know about, and it was the part Ilse was proudest of.

A charter held land because a surveyor had signed it, and a signature had a cost. Every boundary you fixed pulled against you a little for the rest of your life, a thread from your hand to the earth, and if the land strained, so did you. Licensed surveyors carried their burden in their joints. Old ones went grey in the hands, and very old ones went grey all over and stopped. Quince had taught her the one thing the licensed men never learned, because they’d never needed to. You could trace a second mark beneath your own, line for line, in blind ink, and the weight would run down the second thread instead of the first. Quince was in the Hobbet churchyard and well past minding. He’d left her his mark the way another man might leave a watch.

*Don’t carry what you can make someone else carry*, he used to say, *and never let anyone carry you.* He had seen no contradiction in this. Neither, most days, did she.

She slung the bag over her shoulder. The hedge, in its new place, rustled once, settling, like a dog turning round before it lies down.


* * *


By eight the next morning the headache had dulled to the size of a walnut, and Ilse was carrying wet sheets through her mother’s laundry in Hobbet with the eggs in her coat pocket.

“You were out,” said Hesta Marrow, without turning from the copper.

“I was walking.”

“At two in the morning.”

“It’s a free country.” Ilse hung a petticoat across the line and pinned it with three pegs. “Within the boundaries of the Held Reach, as attested by the Ninefold and maintained by Concordance.”

“Don’t be clever at me before breakfast.” Her mother drove the paddle down into the steaming wash and hauled up a bedsheet like a drowned man. Hesta Marrow was forty-nine and built like a bollard, red to the elbows from forty years of other people’s linen, and she disapproved of her daughter with the steadiness other mothers gave to prayer. “Whose was it?”

“Whose was what?”

“Ilse.”

Ilse sighed. “Mrs. Tull’s. The orchard strip.”

The paddle stopped. Her mother looked at her through the steam for a long moment, then went back to the copper. “Well,” she said, “that one had it coming. Fask. Not Agnes. Agnes is a saint. A *nosy* saint.” She thumped the sheet. “Did you get paid?”

“Three marks.”

“Put it in the tin.”

“I always put it in the tin.”

The tin lived behind the loose brick by the chimney: a square one that had once held Lisk tea and still smelled faintly of bergamot. Ilse knew to the copper what was in it. Forty-one marks and six. She added three. Forty-four marks and six. Lord Saffery’s steward had said, two summers ago, with the condescension of a man who expected never to be held to it, that his lordship might consider selling back the north frontage of the Long Field for sixty marks. That meant the strip with the well. Sixty yards of good black soil that had been Marrow land for three generations, until a surveyor named Corwin Fask came through the parish with a new survey and a lord’s money, and the boundary moved sixty yards one autumn night in 1192.

She’d been twelve. She remembered the noise. It hadn’t been gentle like last night’s. Fask didn’t do gentle; he did *thorough*. The whole fence-line had come up out of the ground with a crack like a felled tree, and the well-head had groaned as the stones ground sideways, and her father had stood at the window in his shirt with the lamp in his hand saying *no, no, no* in a voice she’d never heard before and never wanted to hear again.

The Court had upheld it on appeal. Precedence and fidelity: Fask’s charter was newer than the old parish map, but it was better made, and better beat older. That was the law. Her father had spent his savings proving that the law was the law, then gone to work as a hired hand on his own field, and he had got smaller every year until there was nothing left for the fever to take.

Ilse put the lid back on the tin and the brick back in the wall. Sixteen marks to go, if the steward kept his word, and he wouldn’t. But she had taught herself the trade that took the field away, and she was better at it than Fask. When she had a Patent of Charting and a licensed survey, filed and stamped, she would walk the Long Field herself, chain by chain, and make it so true the Court would have to kneel to it.

She just had to stay out of prison until then.


* * *


The market at Hobbet on a Thursday was where the parish did its real business, which was talking. Ilse sold the eggs to the baker’s girl for a penny more than she’d have got from anyone else. She bought a twist of tea and a heel of cheese, and she listened, which was the other half of being a forger. You had to know where the trouble was before the trouble knew where you were.

“—never seen anything like it,” the cheesewife was saying, to anyone. “A College gentleman. Out of the Meridian, all the way out from the Cape, can you imagine. Grey gloves. Kid, I should think. Ever so polite.”

“What did he want?” said the baker’s girl.

“Looking at the parish charters. The Little Wick lane, Jem says. Sat in the vestry with the chest open half the afternoon and then thanked Jem very nicely and went off in a hired gig. Asked the way to the old milestone.”

“Why would the College care about the Little Wick lane?”

“Why does the College care about anything? To make sure we stay put, I suppose.” The cheesewife laughed. “Maybe he heard about your mother’s pastry and thought it would float off.”

Ilse turned the cheese over in her hand and didn’t look up. It might mean nothing. The Meridian sent its people out all the time: to inspect charters and renew the old benchmarks, and to stand around in good coats explaining things to magistrates. It was the College that kept the Held Reach held. Its surveyors walked the oldest boundaries and re-signed them, so that the roads stayed roads and the rivers kept to their beds, and so that nobody woke to find that their house had wandered a furlong in the night. It was a long way from the Cape to the Fallowmarch, though. They didn’t send a man in kid gloves to look at a farm lane for no reason.

There’s been the other business, too, said a voice at the back of her mind that sounded like Quince. The Concordance had sent assessors east since the spring, because of the *loosenings*: a ditch in Marlow that wandered, a gatepost at Fennock that had leaned over in a single night and then kept walking. Talk said the Margin-folk had come in from the west in their painted wagons with their peculiar songs and were unpicking the ground out of spite. Talk said a great many things. But assessors were people who looked closely at boundaries, and Ilse had moved one last night.

She paid for the cheese and walked home the long way, by the Little Wick lane. She told herself it was a nice afternoon for it.


* * *


It was a nice afternoon. That was the thing she kept coming back to, afterward. The barley was in, and the stubble fields lay gold under a high sky that was just beginning to turn green at the edge with evening. Rooks went over in a long ragged line. The Little Wick lane ran between two banks of hawthorn and dog rose, old banks, sunk deep by four hundred years of wheels, so that you walked in a kind of green trench with the fields above your head. At the bottom of the dip the lane forded the Wick brook over a bed of set cobbles. Then it climbed again past the old milestone, a lump of limestone with HOBBET II carved on one side and a benchmark arrow on the other, its notch filled with moss.

Tam Brewin was coming down the far side with his wagon and his big bay mare, a load of hurdles behind him, singing something about a girl in Lisk. Ilse lifted a hand. Tam lifted his whip.

Her ears popped.

She stopped. It was like the moment on a stair when you expect one more step and there isn’t one. The pressure that had been at the back of her head all her life, so constant she only noticed it when it changed, the faint steady hum she had learned at fourteen to call *the level*, simply dropped away. It was like a held note cut off mid-breath. The silence where it had been was so loud she put a hand out to the bank.

The bank moved under her palm.

“Tam!” she shouted.

He’d felt something too; she saw him half-rise on the box. The mare threw her head up. Then the lane began to come apart.

It started at the ford. The set cobbles, three hundred years in their places, began to drift away from each other the way the stitches of a dropped knit run away from the needle, each one loosening its neighbor. Water spread over them. The ruts in the lane were deep enough to turn a cartwheel, and they *swam*, wriggling sideways across the roadway like eels in a bucket, and the banks on either side sagged inward with a sound like a long sigh. Dog roses tore. Somewhere a root snapped. The milestone sank, slowly and with a kind of dignity, until it stood in the lane up to its shoulders like a man wading.

The wagon tipped. Its near wheel had been in a rut, and the rut was no longer there; the wheel dropped through soft earth to the hub. The load of hurdles slid with a clatter and the whole wagon heeled over toward the bank, and Tam Brewin went off the box sideways into the mud. The mare screamed and plunged, half-strangled by the traces.

Ilse was already running.

The ground was wrong beneath her. Every step went down farther than it should, or hit sooner, and the lane was shrugging under her like the deck of a boat. She went down on one knee and came up again with her hands full of mud. Tam was on his back with the edge of a hurdle across his chest and his face white. She grabbed the hurdle and heaved, and he scrabbled out from under it.

“The horse,” he gasped. “Get her loose, she’ll break her neck—”

The mare was down on her knees, thrashing, the shaft rearing up over her as the wagon settled. Ilse got her knife out. She had cut traces before; you don’t grow up in the Fallowmarch without having cut something loose from something else in a hurry. She sawed through the near trace and then the belly-band, ducking a hoof that would have taken her jaw off, and Tam got the mare’s head. Together they hauled her up the shifting slope of the bank. She went up it in great panicked surges, and they all fell together into the stubble at the top: horse, man and forger, in a heap, under the green evening sky.

Below them, the Little Wick lane went on unravelling for another minute or so. Then, slowly, it stopped. It didn’t go back; it just stopped. The cobbles lay scattered over the bed of the brook like spilled dice. The ruts had settled in new and senseless curves. The milestone stood up to its shoulders in the earth, HOBBET II looking out at a road that no longer went to Hobbet or anywhere else.

“What,” said Tam Brewin. He was lying on his back in the stubble and breathing like a bellows. “What. What in the name of the Atlas was that.”

Ilse didn’t answer. She was sitting up with her arms around her knees, looking down at the ruin of the lane, and the level was coming back into her ears, slowly, like hearing after a blow. She had felt land move many times in her life. She had *moved* land, most recently last night. And she knew how it felt when one charter pushed against another, the strain and the give and the settling. That was a kind of argument. The ground took a side.

This hadn’t been an argument. Nothing had pushed. The lane had simply been let go, as though someone had reached into the parish chest and taken out its charter and burned it, and the land, with nothing left to tell it where it was, had forgotten.

But that wasn’t possible. You couldn’t simply un-write a road. Not one this old, deep in the Reach, held by four hundred years of the Fallowmarch walking up and down it. Not without—

*A College gentleman. Grey gloves. Ever so polite. Looking at the Little Wick lane.*

“I need to go home,” she said aloud.

“You saved my life,” said Tam.

“You’d have got out.”

“I’d have got out *flat*.” He rolled over and looked at his mare, who stood trembling in the stubble with her head down and a long graze along her flank. “Look at her. Look at my wagon. Who’s going to pay for my wagon?” His voice cracked. “Ilse, what *was* it?”

“I don’t know,” she said, and it was the truest thing she’d said all day.


* * *


She should have gone home. She knew it the way you know you shouldn’t pick a scab. But there was a forger’s itch at the back of her neck, the one that had kept her out of the magistrate’s ledger for nine years, and it said that a strange day was a day to check your work. So she went the long way round in the last of the light. She came up through Fenner’s copse and over the stile behind Widow Tull’s, to look at her hedge.

That was how she found him.

There was a dark lantern set in the grass beside the hedge, shuttered to a thin gold blade of light, and in the light a man was crouching. He was young, perhaps her own age or a little less, in a long coat of the particular grey-blue that the Concordance gave its assessors, with brass buttons that caught the light. He had a notebook open on one knee and a folding rule in his hand, and he was measuring the raw edge of the soil along the hedge-bottom: the fresh earth where the roots had dragged through last night, which no rain had yet softened. He measured it, wrote something down, and measured again.

Ilse stood very still in the shadow of the stile and thought, with great clarity, *bloody hell.*

She had a choice. She could walk away; he hadn’t seen her. But if he was what she thought he was, he would be at the parish office in the morning and at Agnes Tull’s door by noon. Agnes Tull was a saint, a nosy one and a terrible liar. Better to know him now, in the dark, on her own ground.

She stepped out over the stile, letting her boot scuff the step so he would hear her coming. He looked up. The light caught his face from below: a fine, serious, well-made face, the kind that had been told it was handsome and had decided that was beside the point, with fair hair combed back with water and eyes she couldn’t see the color of. He didn’t startle. He looked at her the way she looked at a boundary stone, as if he was checking it against a record.

“Good evening,” he said.

“Evening.” She let a little of the Fallowmarch into her voice, a little farm-girl curiosity. “You’re out late, sir. Lost?”

“No.” He closed the notebook on one finger to keep his place, and with his other hand he tapped the folding rule against the raw earth at the hedge-bottom: once, twice. When he spoke again it was slowly and pleasantly, in the voice of someone who expected to be believed.

“This hedge,” he said, “has moved.”


## Chapter 2: The Assessor’s Notebook

“Has it?” said Ilse.

She came down off the stile and stood at the edge of his light, where the grass was wet and the hawthorn smelled green and bruised. She let herself look at the hedge the way a farm girl would look at it, which was to say without much interest, as a thing that kept sheep in.

“It has.” He stood, unfolding himself carefully, like a man who had been told as a boy not to slouch. “Forgive me. I should have introduced myself before accusing your hedges. Kasimir Reyne. Junior assessor, attached to the Concordance inspection of the Fallowmarch voids, under Assessor Pollard, who is at the Bell in Hobbet and has gone to bed.”

“The what voids?”

“The loosenings. The ditch at Marlow in Highsun, the gatepost at Fennock.” He paused. “The lane at Little Wick, this evening. I imagine you heard.”

“I was in it.”

That stopped him, which she enjoyed more than she should have. He looked at the mud dried to her knees, and his face rearranged itself from courtesy into attention.

“You were in the lane when it went?”

“Me and Tam Brewin and his mare. We got out.” She shrugged. “Tam’s wagon didn’t.”

He opened the notebook again, a small thing in black calf, ruled by hand. His writing was beautiful even at speed, upright and narrow, the descenders all one length, and she read it upside down out of pure professional reflex. *Little Wick lane — witness — ford first, then ruts — time?*

“What time?” he said.

“Half past six. Near enough. The rooks were going over.”

He wrote *rooks*, and then, after a moment’s thought, crossed it out. “And did you feel anything before it began? Some people describe a pressure.”

There it was: a little door, held politely open. Anyone could measure, but only the levelled felt a lane let go. If she said yes, he would write that down too, in a notebook already full of things she didn’t like.

“I felt the ground go soft under my feet,” she said. “Same as Tam. Same as the horse.”

“Of course.” He didn’t write anything, which was worse. His eyes were grey or blue, the colour of slate in rain. “And this hedge. Did you see it move?”

“Hedges don’t move.”

“That is the general view.” He crouched again and laid the folding rule flat against the raw soil at the hedge-bottom. “It is also, in this case, wrong. Look here, if you would.”

She looked; it would have been stranger not to. In the gold blade of his lantern the earth along the east side of the hawthorn lay open in a strip a little under a yard wide. It was dark and crumbed and stippled with the white ends of broken rootlets, like a bed someone had just got out of.

“Forty-one yards, nine inches, end to end,” he said. “The rootlets are broken, not cut, and the breaks are still wet. Last night’s dew lies on top of the turned soil, not under it. This hedge walked within the last day, west, two rods almost exactly.” He looked up at her. “Two rods. Thirty-three feet. That is not a number the weather chooses.”

“Maybe it was homesick.”

“Maybe it was.” He did not smile, but something near his mouth considered it. “It was re-charted in Haysel by a licensed surveyor acting for Lord Saffery, and moved east. A hedge cannot move two rods in a season against a Patent survey unless somebody has filed a later charter of better fidelity. Nobody in this parish holds a Patent.” He tilted his head. “Unless you know of someone.”

“The vicar’s uncle had one,” said Ilse. “He’s dead.”

“Then I think we may exclude him.”

He stood and brushed his knees, and she saw his boots, and they nearly undid her. Tessary boots, lasted to his own foot by someone who had done nothing else for forty years, with a welt like a line of handwriting. He had walked them to the ankle through a wet orchard and ruined them, and either he hadn’t noticed or he had decided it was a fair price for a hedge. She couldn’t decide which was worse.

He was studying her too, openly, the way he’d studied the soil. She knew what he saw, because she had arranged it: a small parish girl in a man’s old coat, hair hacked short, come over a stile to stare at a stranger. She watched him decide she was what she looked like, and watched a smaller part of him decline to decide anything at all.

Then he breathed in, and frowned. “You smell of oak-gall. Iron-gall ink, or something like it.” He said it almost apologetically, as a man might mention that your collar was turned under. “My mother’s clerks reek of it.”

The blind had a sour green edge that never washed out of the skin entirely, however much you scrubbed with lye. She had scrubbed. It was the alum, Quince used to say. Alum got into you like guilt.

“My mother’s a laundress,” she said. “We dye the mourning blacks. Oak-apples and copperas, three days in the vat. You can smell it on the whole street when someone’s died.” This was true. It had always been true. That was the beauty of it. “There’s a funeral Saturday. Old Pettifer.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be. He was a terrible man. He cheated at skittles.”

Now the smile did come, very briefly, and was put away again like a coin in a purse. “Then I am sorry for the skittles,” he said. “May I ask your name?”

She considered lying. It was a parish of thirty-one families and a mill, and any of them would tell him her name for the pleasure of telling. A lie would cost her nothing tonight and everything tomorrow, when he found it and asked himself why.

“Ilse Marrow.”

He wrote it down. *Ilse Marrow*, in that tall clean hand. It looked strange to her there, too tidy, like a borrowed dress. “Miss Marrow. And do you know whose hedge this is?”

“Mrs. Tull’s. She’s seventy-four and she’s asleep.” Ilse let her voice go sharp. “If you knock her up at this hour about her hedge, sir, you’ll get a pot of something thrown at you, and I’ll say you deserved it.”

“I am not going to knock anyone up,” he said, as though she had suggested he pick his teeth with the folding rule. “I am going to look at the parish chest in the morning, with the assessor, as is proper.” He closed the notebook. “Good night, Miss Marrow. Mind the lane, if you go home that way. It no longer goes where it went.”

She said good night and went back over the stile, not quickly. All along the field edge she felt his lantern lying flat across her back. He wasn’t watching her, she knew. He would be measuring the hedge again. That was worse.


* * *


The vestry linen went back on Fridays.

The surplices of the Ninefold chapel at Wick had to be starched until they stood up without a priest in them, and Ilse had carried them up the church hill every Friday since she was eight. She had never once been glad of it until that morning, when she saw the vestry door propped open with a hassock and heard, from inside, a voice she knew. She set the basket on the side step and took her time over the folding.

The parish chest stood open on the table: oak, iron-bound, three locks. Jem Rudd the clerk stood beside it with the keys and the stricken look of a man whose biggest event of the year had been a dispute about a pew. A stout man in the same grey-blue coat as Kasimir Reyne’s, with the buttons straining a little, sat in the vicar’s chair with his hat on his knee and a slice of seed cake on the hat.

“I’m sure it is all in order, Mr. Rudd.” Pollard ate some cake. “Mr. Reyne is very thorough. Thoroughness is what he is attached to us to learn.” He looked at his watch. “We dine at Saffery Hall at four.”

Kasimir Reyne stood at the window end of the table, where the light was best, with a charter flattened under his two hands.

She knew it by the way it lay, cockled at one corner where Agnes had held it with a damp thumb. Agnes had brought it to Jem yesterday morning: an amendment filed by the landholder, which was every landholder’s right, attested by a local surveyor, which was every hedge-man’s trade. Ilse had done everything properly. It was the improper part she had done better.

“Two charters for one boundary,” Kasimir said. “Corwin Fask’s, under Patent, filed Haysel the ninth. And this amendment, filed yesterday for Mrs. Agnes Tull. Forty-one stations. The lines are very good.” He said it the way a man might report that a bridge was well built, shortly before it fell down. “The surveyor’s mark is not registered with the Court.”

“Hedge-mark,” said Pollard, through cake. “Half the Fallowmarch is charted by hedge-men. Wheelwrights. Old women with a feel for drainage. It’s a scandal, not a crime. A freeholder may survey her own holding. Statute of Small Holdings, 1061.”

“Yes, sir. It is a crime when the survey binds the land to a state contrary to a Patent charter already in force. That is contrary charting.” Kasimir turned the sheet toward the light. Through the gap of the door Ilse saw her own wren’s foot, three lines and a spur, dark and confident in the lower corner. It looked very small from here. “To supersede a competent Patent survey, this would have to be of greater fidelity. I find that improbable for a sheet drawn by a hedge-man, at night, on a farm.”

“Improbable,” said Pollard, “is not evidence.”

“No, sir. Which is why I should like to request a drawing of the mark.”

The words went into Ilse like cold water.

She had seen it done once, at sixteen, to a Fennock wheelwright named Hobb who forged ditches. The Court’s examiner, a dry little woman with a case of lenses, had laid his charters out in the tithe barn, wetted each one and set a damp lens over the signature. A mark was a thread from the hand to the land, and an examiner could follow it home. Under a damp lens, blind ink showed. So did a second mark ghosted beneath the first. And the thread of a ghosted mark ran to whoever carried the burden: in this case to a disgraced hedge-surveyor in the Hobbet churchyard, whose mark was still on the Court’s rolls, and whose apprentice for six years had been a girl named Marrow.

They had cut a line through every one of Hobb’s marks with a silver knife. Every ditch he had ever moved had moved back in one night, and he had gone to the Tessary gaol for nine years. His wife had taken in washing. Hesta had given her the overflow.

“A drawing,” said Pollard. “Over a hedge.”

“Over a contrary charter, sir. If it is one. And there is the other matter. Mr. Rudd, you said a gentleman came to see the Little Wick lane charter on Tuesday.”

“That’s right, sir. A College gentleman. Very civil.”

“May I see it?”

Jem found it near the bottom, folded in four, the ink of the lane’s two banks faded to the colour of weak tea. Kasimir read it twice, and held it to the window and looked through it at the light.

“It is in order,” he said at last.

“It is in order,” Kasimir said again, “and the lane is not.”

There was a silence in the vestry. Pollard put down his cake.

“Old charters,” he said. “Old ground. It tires. Write it up with the others, Reyne. The Tessary office has a theory about the Margin wagons.”

“The nearest Margin wagon is a very long way from here, sir.”

“They are a mobile people,” said Pollard, with dignity. “That is rather the point of them.”

Kasimir let it go. She watched it cost him something. “And the drawing of the mark on the Tull amendment?”

Pollard sighed like a man watching his dinner recede. “The sheet would go to Tessary under seal. Correspondence, an examiner’s calendar. Three weeks at the least, Reyne, and you’ll be gone by then.”

“I will write the request myself, sir. It needs only your signature.”

“Of course it does.” Pollard held out his hand for the pen. “Seal the chest, then. Mr. Rudd, nobody opens it until the Court says so. Nobody. Not the vicar. Not the Ninefold, should they drop by.”

So that door closed too. She had been thinking, in the cool back room of her mind where Quince had taught her to think, of getting into the vestry at night and burning the amendment, or releasing it, unsigning it with her own hand. Agnes would lose her Pearmain, but there would be nothing left to draw. Either way she needed the sheet. She watched Kasimir drip a coin of grey-blue wax across the hasp and press the Concordance seal into it, neatly, and then do it twice more, once on each of the other locks.

Then he looked up and through the door and directly at her.

“Good morning, Miss Marrow,” he said.

Pollard turned round in the vicar’s chair. Jem Rudd turned round. Ilse picked up the basket of surplices and held it in front of her like a shield.

“The vicar’s linen,” she said. “I’ll leave it on the step.”

“Thank you,” said Kasimir Reyne, as if it had been his.


* * *


“You could run,” said Hesta.

It was past eleven. The copper was out and the kitchen smelled of wet ash and cooling starch. Her mother sat at the table with her red hands flat on the scrubbed wood, the way she sat when she meant to stay sitting until something was decided. The candle between them had burned down to a stub. Neither of them had moved to light another.

“Where?” said Ilse.

“Lisk. You’ve a cousin in Lisk.”

“Cousin Barnaby is a canal-warden. He’d have me in a cell by Tuesday.”

“The Court sits everywhere. That’s the point of a Court.” She heard the edge in her voice and put it away, and went to the window. The yard was black, the lane beyond it blacker.

She did what Quince had taught her to do with any trouble big enough to be frightening. She surveyed it.

Station one: Hobbet. Three weeks, a drawing of the mark, and Agnes Tull saying *I didn’t know, dearie*, which would be true and wouldn’t help. Then the Tessary gaol, and every boundary she had ever moved snapping back overnight: the Gethins’ ditch, the chapel glebe, Widow Ames’s goat-run. Every small truth she had made, unmade with a silver knife.

Station two: any town in the Reach. The Court’s rolls went everywhere the Great Meridian Road went, and the Road went everywhere.

Station three: the Margin. The Tenting wouldn’t have her, and the Margin was no place to hide. It was a place where things got lost, which was different.

The Drift wasn’t a station at all. It was the edge of the board.

So she went back and looked again, the way you looked at a traverse that wouldn’t close, and found the error. It was a thing Quince had said once in the Hare and Lantern, with gin on his breath, explaining why the College men walked around the Reach as though they owned it.

*Because they’re not ours, girl. Members of the Meridian answer to the Meridian. Charting offences, any of them, it’s their own Board tries them. Not the Court. The Court can’t touch so much as a link-boy. Privilege. Four hundred years of it. It makes Lady Muck in Tessary spit teeth.*

And how did a smallholder’s daughter become a member of the Meridian? Patent examinations, Patent fees. Or—

*Any fool with the level can try the Walk. Turn up at their gate on the equinox and walk out into the Margin and back. A good few don’t come back. I thought about it once.* He’d laughed into his gin. *Then I thought about it twice.*

“The autumn equinox,” Ilse said aloud.

Her mother looked up.

“It’s the twenty-second. Today’s the sixteenth.” Ilse sat. She found she was counting on her fingers, and stopped. “Five hundred and sixty miles to Cape Verity. The express mail goes through the Bell at dawn tomorrow, day and night, changes at every stage. Four days. I’d be at the Lantern Gate the night before the equinox. Once I’m a member, the Court can’t have me. The College tries me itself. Which takes longer. Which might take—” She stopped.

“How much,” said Hesta.

“What?”

“The coach. Don’t look at me like I’m simple. I’ve been listening to you add sums under your breath for nine years. How much.”

“Thirty-eight marks. Inside.”

Neither of them looked at the chimney.

The tin was there anyway, behind the loose brick, the way a grave is there whether you look at it or not. Forty-four marks and six. Sixteen short of a strip of black soil with a well in it. She could see the frontage in Haysel with the barley standing, and the well-head with its stones ground crooked.

She had meant to buy it back. That had been the whole of the plan since she was fourteen: buy it back, then walk it with a Patent and chain it so true the Court would kneel. She had meant to do it for him. It came to her, with the candle dying, that she had never once asked whether he would have wanted it done this way, and that she wasn’t going to ask now.

“It’s a field,” said Hesta.

“It’s *our* field.”

“It was your father’s field. And he’s dead, and it didn’t save him, and it won’t save you.” Her mother’s voice didn’t rise. It went lower and harder, like a paddle going down into the copper. “I’ll not stand at the back of a courtroom in Tessary watching them take a knife to your name so that you can buy back sixty yards of dirt. I did that once. I did it for a year. You remember what it got us.”

Ilse remembered.

She took out the brick and counted the money onto the table, though she knew to the copper what was there, because counting was a thing to do with her hands. She put six marks and six back in the tin and the rest in her purse. The purse made a sound against the table like a door shutting in another room.

“Six and six,” she said. “For the rent.”

“Keep it.”

“It’s for the rent, Mam.”

Hesta looked at her a long time. Then she lit another candle from the stub and went to the press by the stairs and took out the coat.

It was Daun Marrow’s good coat, dark brown broadcloth gone soft at the elbows, the one he had been married in. The parish lent a coat for burying, and Hesta had not let them have this one. It was too big in the shoulders for Ilse and too long in the arm. Her mother laid it on the table and turned back the lining.

“If you’ll be taking your bits,” Hesta said, not looking at her, “they’ll want to be somewhere a nosy body won’t find them.”

So they sat up until two by a single candle, as they had sat up over other people’s shirts a thousand times. Ilse unpicked the lining along the hem. In went the blind in its corked bottle, rolled in flannel; the damp-lens in its leather; a twist of alum; the fine steel pen Quince had ground for her the year before he died. Her mother stitched it closed again with laundress’s stitches, eleven to the inch, invisible. Neither of them said what it was for.

At four, Hesta wrapped half a loaf and the heel of cheese in a cloth and put it in the coat pocket where the eggs had been.

“Write,” she said, at the door. “You wretch.”


* * *


The Bell’s yard at dawn was all steam and harness and swearing.

The express mail stood under the arch with its lamps still lit, maroon and black, a crowned horn on the door and four greys stamping. The guard looked at Ilse’s thirty-eight marks as though she had made them in a back room. She had not, as it happened. Not these. She paid for an inside seat and then climbed up onto the roof among the mailbags, where the wind would be. She wanted to see.

Across the yard, on the first floor of the Bell, a window stood open.

He was in it, coat off, sleeves rolled, packing a leather case on the bed and folding each shirt into thirds. She watched him lay the black notebook on top and square it to the corners. A post-chaise waited at the side door with a trunk roped on, and the trunk had a label too far away to read.

Then he straightened and looked out of the window and saw her.

He saw her on the roof of the westbound mail with her father’s coat buttoned to the throat and her surveyor’s bag between her knees. She saw him see the bag, and the board on the yard wall chalked TESSARY — VERITY CROSS. She thought he might lift a hand. He didn’t. He only stood very still at the window with a shirt folded in thirds over his arm, the way he had stood at the hedge, checking her against a record.

The guard blew the horn. The greys leaned into the collars, and the mail lurched, and swung, and went out under the arch into the Hobbet road.

She did not look back at the window. She looked at everything else instead.

The sun came up behind them in a long red bar, and the light ran out ahead of the coach across the stubble. Every field she had ever walked went past at eight miles an hour: the Gethins’ forty acres, with the ditch she had moved four feet south in the spring; Widow Ames’s goat-run; the hedge of Saffery’s Home Farm, black and dead straight, which she had never touched. She didn’t look for the Long Field, and saw it anyway, the dark square of the well-head on the wrong side of a line.

Every hedge was a line on a sheet. She knew which ones. She knew whose. She knew Fask’s straight black work and the old parish lines in brown ink gone faint as tea, and her own, the few, the small. Every one of them was a sentence written by someone with a pen and a reason, and the land had believed them all. The hedgerows ran away from her in the low red light, dark green, unbroken, knitting the whole Fallowmarch together from horizon to horizon, and holding it still.
