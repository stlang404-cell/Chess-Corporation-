# Chapter 1
## A Very Confident Opinion

The hedge moved at a quarter past two in the morning, and it did not want to.

Ilse Marrow felt it refuse before she heard it. A pressure gathered behind her ears, the way it did before thunder, and the ground under her knees went as taut as a drumskin. In the shuttered lantern’s single slot of light, the wet charter lay on her board with its ink still shining. The wren’s-foot flourish of her signature sat over the faint violet underline that wasn’t hers. Nine hours of work. Forty-one stations, two hundred and twelve measured lengths, every angle checked twice by sightvane and once more by spite. It was a very good map of Widow Tull’s orchard strip, accurate to a link except in one particular.

In that particular, the hawthorn hedge on the west boundary stood two rods farther west than it actually did.

“Come on,” Ilse whispered. “You know you want to.”

The hedge did not know any such thing. It had stood where it stood since Haysel, when Lord Saffery’s surveyor had walked this parish with his chainmen and his fine leather case, re-charted the whole of Wick Fallow in nine days, and filed the result with the Concordance Court. The Court had stamped it, and the hedge had moved east in a single night, taking thirty-three feet of the widow’s land and four of her apple trees with it. Corwin Fask’s charter was legal and attested, and it had been filed before anyone else’s. To the ground it was simply the truth, and the ground had obeyed it.

Ilse’s charter was a different truth. It was better made. That was the whole trick.

She laid her palm flat on the sheet, the way old Quince had taught her, and leaned her attention into it. She didn’t push. Pushing was for amateurs and drunks. She *agreed* with the map, the way you agree with a fact you have looked at for so long it has become furniture. The hedge stands here. Of course it does. Where else would it stand? The soil there is better drained. The roots have always wanted the slope. Look at the old ditch line under the grass, look at the stump where the first planting failed, and see how the land itself remembers a boundary two rods west. It isn’t a lie. It’s a very confident opinion about where a hedge ought to be.

The pressure in her ears turned into a ringing, then a single high note, and the hedge gave.

It began as a sound like a long breath drawn through teeth: roots, hundreds of them, sliding through wet earth. Then the thorn branches scraped against each other with a dry rattle, the sound of a crowd shifting its feet. A blackbird burst from the hedge-bottom shrieking. Ilse lifted the lantern shutter a finger’s width and watched forty yards of hawthorn walk.

It didn’t topple or uproot. It *slid*, the whole green wall of it sliding level through the dark, as if the field beneath were a tablecloth and someone very patient were drawing it toward them. Leaves trembled and settled. A sparrow’s nest rode along on a fork, untroubled. Somewhere along the run a stone grated and turned over. Two rods is thirty-three feet, and it took the hedge about as long to travel as it took Ilse to say the Ninefold’s Grace under her breath, which she did not believe in and recited anyway. Then it was finished, and the hedge stood still in its new place as though it had always been there. The ground let go of her ears, and a cold sweat broke over her whole body at once.

She sat back on her heels. Her head was splitting. Her right hand, the signing hand, ached from the knuckles to the wrist with the deep, grinding ache she thought of as the bill.

Behind her, at the cottage door, a voice said, “Oh, my word,” and then, more practically, “Is it done, dearie?”

“It’s done, Mrs. Tull.”

Agnes Tull came down the garden path in a nightcap and three shawls, carrying a candle in one hand and a basket in the other. She was seventy-four and walked like a woman who had stopped falling over through sheer resentment. She held the candle up toward the hedge and peered at it, and then at the four apple trees now standing on the right side of it, their leaves silver in the light.

“There’s my Pearmain,” she said. “There’s my old Pearmain back. And my Russet. Oh, you lovely girl.”

“I’m not lovely. I’m expensive.”

“Three marks and the eggs, like we said.” She pressed the basket on Ilse and then, before Ilse could stop her, pressed her dry old cheek against Ilse’s forehead as well. “Your father would have been proud.”

He wouldn’t, Ilse thought. Daun Marrow had believed in the Court and the Atlas and the Ninefold’s Grace until the day those things took his well, and then he hadn’t believed in anything at all, and three years after that he had died. But she said thank you, because Agnes Tull was seventy-four and had her Pearmain back, and that was worth a lie.

She packed in the dark by touch. The chain went into its canvas bag link by link so it wouldn’t jangle, a hundred links, each one seven inches and ninety-two hundredths. Then the sightvane and the folding rule. The levelglass went into its sock, its little bead of quicksilver swimming lazily now that the land was quiet again. Last came the inks: the common black for the visible lines, and the small corked bottle of what Quince had called *the blind*, a pale gall-ink cut with alum and a whisper of quicksilver, which dried to nothing on paper and showed only under a damp lens. With it, under her own flourish, she had traced the mark of a man who had been dead three years.

That was the part even Agnes Tull didn’t know about, and it was the part Ilse was proudest of.

A charter held land because a surveyor had signed it, and a signature had a cost. Every boundary you fixed pulled against you a little for the rest of your life, a thread from your hand to the earth, and if the land strained, so did you. Licensed surveyors carried their burden in their joints. Old ones went grey in the hands, and very old ones went grey all over and stopped. Quince had taught her the one thing the licensed men never learned, because they’d never needed to. You could trace a second mark beneath your own, line for line, in blind ink, and the weight would run down the second thread instead of the first. Quince was in the Hobbet churchyard and well past minding. He’d left her his mark the way another man might leave a watch.

*Don’t carry what you can make someone else carry*, he used to say, *and never let anyone carry you.* He had seen no contradiction in this. Neither, most days, did she.

She slung the bag over her shoulder. The hedge, in its new place, rustled once, settling, like a dog turning round before it lies down.

* * *

By eight the next morning the headache had dulled to the size of a walnut, and Ilse was carrying wet sheets through her mother’s laundry in Hobbet with the eggs in her coat pocket.

“You were out,” said Hesta Marrow, without turning from the copper.

“I was walking.”

“At two in the morning.”

“It’s a free country.” Ilse hung a petticoat across the line and pinned it with three pegs. “Within the boundaries of the Held Reach, as attested by the Ninefold and maintained by Concordance.”

“Don’t be clever at me before breakfast.” Her mother drove the paddle down into the steaming wash and hauled up a bedsheet like a drowned man. Hesta Marrow was forty-nine and built like a bollard, red to the elbows from forty years of other people’s linen, and she disapproved of her daughter with the steadiness other mothers gave to prayer. “Whose was it?”

“Whose was what?”

“Ilse.”

Ilse sighed. “Mrs. Tull’s. The orchard strip.”

The paddle stopped. Her mother looked at her through the steam for a long moment, then went back to the copper. “Well,” she said, “that one had it coming. Fask. Not Agnes. Agnes is a saint. A *nosy* saint.” She thumped the sheet. “Did you get paid?”

“Three marks.”

“Put it in the tin.”

“I always put it in the tin.”

The tin lived behind the loose brick by the chimney: a square one that had once held Lisk tea and still smelled faintly of bergamot. Ilse knew to the copper what was in it. Forty-one marks and six. She added three. Forty-four marks and six. Lord Saffery’s steward had said, two summers ago, with the condescension of a man who expected never to be held to it, that his lordship might consider selling back the north frontage of the Long Field for sixty marks. That meant the strip with the well. Sixty yards of good black soil that had been Marrow land for three generations, until a surveyor named Corwin Fask came through the parish with a new survey and a lord’s money, and the boundary moved sixty yards one autumn night in 1192.

She’d been twelve. She remembered the noise. It hadn’t been gentle like last night’s. Fask didn’t do gentle; he did *thorough*. The whole fence-line had come up out of the ground with a crack like a felled tree, and the well-head had groaned as the stones ground sideways, and her father had stood at the window in his shirt with the lamp in his hand saying *no, no, no* in a voice she’d never heard before and never wanted to hear again.

The Court had upheld it on appeal. Precedence and fidelity: Fask’s charter was newer than the old parish map, but it was better made, and better beat older. That was the law. Her father had spent his savings proving that the law was the law, then gone to work as a hired hand on his own field, and he had got smaller every year until there was nothing left for the fever to take.

Ilse put the lid back on the tin and the brick back in the wall. Sixteen marks to go, if the steward kept his word, and he wouldn’t. But she had taught herself the trade that took the field away, and she was better at it than Fask. When she had a Patent of Charting and a licensed survey, filed and stamped, she would walk the Long Field herself, chain by chain, and make it so true the Court would have to kneel to it.

She just had to stay out of prison until then.

* * *

The market at Hobbet on a Thursday was where the parish did its real business, which was talking. Ilse sold the eggs to the baker’s girl for a penny more than she’d have got from anyone else. She bought a twist of tea and a heel of cheese, and she listened, which was the other half of being a forger. You had to know where the trouble was before the trouble knew where you were.

“—never seen anything like it,” the cheesewife was saying, to anyone. “A College gentleman. Out of the Meridian, all the way out from the Cape, can you imagine. Grey gloves. Kid, I should think. Ever so polite.”

“What did he want?” said the baker’s girl.

“Looking at the parish charters. The Little Wick lane, Jem says. Sat in the vestry with the chest open half the afternoon and then thanked Jem very nicely and went off in a hired gig. Asked the way to the old milestone.”

“Why would the College care about the Little Wick lane?”

“Why does the College care about anything? To make sure we stay put, I suppose.” The cheesewife laughed. “Maybe he heard about your mother’s pastry and thought it would float off.”

Ilse turned the cheese over in her hand and didn’t look up. It might mean nothing. The Meridian sent its people out all the time: to inspect charters and renew the old benchmarks, and to stand around in good coats explaining things to magistrates. It was the College that kept the Held Reach held. Its surveyors walked the oldest boundaries and re-signed them, so that the roads stayed roads and the rivers kept to their beds, and so that nobody woke to find that their house had wandered a furlong in the night. It was a long way from the Cape to the Fallowmarch, though. They didn’t send a man in kid gloves to look at a farm lane for no reason.

There’s been the other business, too, said a voice at the back of her mind that sounded like Quince. The Concordance had sent assessors east since the spring, because of the *loosenings*: a ditch in Marlow that wandered, a gatepost at Fennock that had leaned over in a single night and then kept walking. Talk said the Margin-folk had come in from the west in their painted wagons with their peculiar songs and were unpicking the ground out of spite. Talk said a great many things. But assessors were people who looked closely at boundaries, and Ilse had moved one last night.

She paid for the cheese and walked home the long way, by the Little Wick lane. She told herself it was a nice afternoon for it.

* * *

It was a nice afternoon. That was the thing she kept coming back to, afterward. The barley was in, and the stubble fields lay gold under a high sky that was just beginning to turn green at the edge with evening. Rooks went over in a long ragged line. The Little Wick lane ran between two banks of hawthorn and dog rose, old banks, sunk deep by four hundred years of wheels, so that you walked in a kind of green trench with the fields above your head. At the bottom of the dip the lane forded the Wick brook over a bed of set cobbles. Then it climbed again past the old milestone, a lump of limestone with HOBBET II carved on one side and a benchmark arrow on the other, its notch filled with moss.

Tam Brewin was coming down the far side with his wagon and his big bay mare, a load of hurdles behind him, singing something about a girl in Lisk. Ilse lifted a hand. Tam lifted his whip.

Her ears popped.

She stopped. It was like the moment on a stair when you expect one more step and there isn’t one. The pressure that had been at the back of her head all her life, so constant she only noticed it when it changed, the faint steady hum she had learned at fourteen to call *the level*, simply dropped away. It was like a held note cut off mid-breath. The silence where it had been was so loud she put a hand out to the bank.

The bank moved under her palm.

“Tam!” she shouted.

He’d felt something too; she saw him half-rise on the box. The mare threw her head up. Then the lane began to come apart.

It started at the ford. The set cobbles, three hundred years in their places, began to drift away from each other the way the stitches of a dropped knit run away from the needle, each one loosening its neighbor. Water spread over them. The ruts in the lane were deep enough to turn a cartwheel, and they *swam*, wriggling sideways across the roadway like eels in a bucket, and the banks on either side sagged inward with a sound like a long sigh. Dog roses tore. Somewhere a root snapped. The milestone sank, slowly and with a kind of dignity, until it stood in the lane up to its shoulders like a man wading.

The wagon tipped. Its near wheel had been in a rut, and the rut was no longer there; the wheel dropped through soft earth to the hub. The load of hurdles slid with a clatter and the whole wagon heeled over toward the bank, and Tam Brewin went off the box sideways into the mud. The mare screamed and plunged, half-strangled by the traces.

Ilse was already running.

The ground was wrong beneath her. Every step went down farther than it should, or hit sooner, and the lane was shrugging under her like the deck of a boat. She went down on one knee and came up again with her hands full of mud. Tam was on his back with the edge of a hurdle across his chest and his face white. She grabbed the hurdle and heaved, and he scrabbled out from under it.

“The horse,” he gasped. “Get her loose, she’ll break her neck—”

The mare was down on her knees, thrashing, the shaft rearing up over her as the wagon settled. Ilse got her knife out. She had cut traces before; you don’t grow up in the Fallowmarch without having cut something loose from something else in a hurry. She sawed through the near trace and then the belly-band, ducking a hoof that would have taken her jaw off, and Tam got the mare’s head. Together they hauled her up the shifting slope of the bank. She went up it in great panicked surges, and they all fell together into the stubble at the top: horse, man and forger, in a heap, under the green evening sky.

Below them, the Little Wick lane went on unravelling for another minute or so. Then, slowly, it stopped. It didn’t go back; it just stopped. The cobbles lay scattered over the bed of the brook like spilled dice. The ruts had settled in new and senseless curves. The milestone stood up to its shoulders in the earth, HOBBET II looking out at a road that no longer went to Hobbet or anywhere else.

“What,” said Tam Brewin. He was lying on his back in the stubble and breathing like a bellows. “What. What in the name of the Atlas was that.”

Ilse didn’t answer. She was sitting up with her arms around her knees, looking down at the ruin of the lane, and the level was coming back into her ears, slowly, like hearing after a blow. She had felt land move many times in her life. She had *moved* land, most recently last night. And she knew how it felt when one charter pushed against another, the strain and the give and the settling. That was a kind of argument. The ground took a side.

This hadn’t been an argument. Nothing had pushed. The lane had simply been let go, as though someone had reached into the parish chest and taken out its charter and burned it, and the land, with nothing left to tell it where it was, had forgotten.

But that wasn’t possible. You couldn’t simply un-write a road. Not one this old, deep in the Reach, held by four hundred years of the Fallowmarch walking up and down it. Not without—

*A College gentleman. Grey gloves. Ever so polite. Looking at the Little Wick lane.*

“I need to go home,” she said aloud.

“You saved my life,” said Tam.

“You’d have got out.”

“I’d have got out *flat*.” He rolled over and looked at his mare, who stood trembling in the stubble with her head down and a long graze along her flank. “Look at her. Look at my wagon. Who’s going to pay for my wagon?” His voice cracked. “Ilse, what *was* it?”

“I don’t know,” she said, and it was the truest thing she’d said all day.

* * *

She should have gone home. She knew it the way you know you shouldn’t pick a scab. But there was a forger’s itch at the back of her neck, the one that had kept her out of the magistrate’s ledger for nine years, and it said that a strange day was a day to check your work. So she went the long way round in the last of the light. She came up through Fenner’s copse and over the stile behind Widow Tull’s, to look at her hedge.

That was how she found him.

There was a dark lantern set in the grass beside the hedge, shuttered to a thin gold blade of light, and in the light a man was crouching. He was young, perhaps her own age or a little less, in a long coat of the particular grey-blue that the Concordance gave its assessors, with brass buttons that caught the light. He had a notebook open on one knee and a folding rule in his hand, and he was measuring the raw edge of the soil along the hedge-bottom: the fresh earth where the roots had dragged through last night, which no rain had yet softened. He measured it, wrote something down, and measured again.

Ilse stood very still in the shadow of the stile and thought, with great clarity, *bloody hell.*

She had a choice. She could walk away; he hadn’t seen her. But if he was what she thought he was, he would be at the parish office in the morning and at Agnes Tull’s door by noon. Agnes Tull was a saint, a nosy one and a terrible liar. Better to know him now, in the dark, on her own ground.

She stepped out over the stile, letting her boot scuff the step so he would hear her coming. He looked up. The light caught his face from below: a fine, serious, well-made face, the kind that had been told it was handsome and had decided that was beside the point, with fair hair combed back with water and eyes she couldn’t see the color of. He didn’t startle. He looked at her the way she looked at a boundary stone, as if he was checking it against a record.

“Good evening,” he said.

“Evening.” She let a little of the Fallowmarch into her voice, a little farm-girl curiosity. “You’re out late, sir. Lost?”

“No.” He closed the notebook on one finger to keep his place, and with his other hand he tapped the folding rule against the raw earth at the hedge-bottom: once, twice. When he spoke again it was slowly and pleasantly, in the voice of someone who expected to be believed.

“This hedge,” he said, “has moved.”
