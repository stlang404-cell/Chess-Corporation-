# Chapter 2
## The Assessor’s Notebook

“Has it?” said Ilse.

She came down off the stile and stood at the edge of his light, where the grass was wet and the hawthorn smelled green and bruised. She let herself look at the hedge the way a farm girl would look at it, which was to say without much interest, as a thing that kept sheep in.

“It has.” He stood, unfolding himself carefully, like a man who had been told as a boy not to slouch. “Forgive me. I should have introduced myself before accusing your hedges. Kasimir Reyne. Junior assessor, attached to the Concordance inspection of the Fallowmarch voids, under Assessor Pollard, who is at the Bell in Hobbet and has gone to bed.”

“The what voids?”

“The loosenings. The ditch at Marlow in Highsun, the gatepost at Fennock.” He paused. “The lane at Little Wick, this evening. I imagine you heard.”

“I was in it.”

That stopped him, which she enjoyed more than she should have. He looked at the mud dried to her knees, and his face rearranged itself from courtesy into attention.

“You were in the lane when it went?”

“Me and Tam Brewin and his mare. We got out.” She shrugged. “Tam’s wagon didn’t.”

He opened the notebook again, a small thing in black calf, ruled by hand. His writing was beautiful even at speed, upright and narrow, the descenders all one length, and she read it upside down out of pure professional reflex. *Little Wick lane — witness — ford first, then ruts — time?*

“What time?” he said.

“Half past six. Near enough. The rooks were going over.”

He wrote *rooks*, and then, after a moment’s thought, crossed it out. “And did you feel anything before it began? Some people describe a pressure.”

There it was: a little door, held politely open. Anyone could measure, but only the levelled felt a lane let go. If she said yes, he would write that down too, in a notebook already full of things she didn’t like.

“I felt the ground go soft under my feet,” she said. “Same as Tam. Same as the horse.”

“Of course.” He didn’t write anything, which was worse. His eyes were grey or blue, the colour of slate in rain. “And this hedge. Did you see it move?”

“Hedges don’t move.”

“That is the general view.” He crouched again and laid the folding rule flat against the raw soil at the hedge-bottom. “It is also, in this case, wrong. Look here, if you would.”

She looked; it would have been stranger not to. In the gold blade of his lantern the earth along the east side of the hawthorn lay open in a strip a little under a yard wide. It was dark and crumbed and stippled with the white ends of broken rootlets, like a bed someone had just got out of.

“Forty-one yards, nine inches, end to end,” he said. “The rootlets are broken, not cut, and the breaks are still wet. Last night’s dew lies on top of the turned soil, not under it. This hedge walked within the last day, west, two rods almost exactly.” He looked up at her. “Two rods. Thirty-three feet. That is not a number the weather chooses.”

“Maybe it was homesick.”

“Maybe it was.” He did not smile, but something near his mouth considered it. “It was re-charted in Haysel by a licensed surveyor acting for Lord Saffery, and moved east. A hedge cannot move two rods in a season against a Patent survey unless somebody has filed a later charter of better fidelity. Nobody in this parish holds a Patent.” He tilted his head. “Unless you know of someone.”

“The vicar’s uncle had one,” said Ilse. “He’s dead.”

“Then I think we may exclude him.”

He stood and brushed his knees, and she saw his boots, and they nearly undid her. Tessary boots, lasted to his own foot by someone who had done nothing else for forty years, with a welt like a line of handwriting. He had walked them to the ankle through a wet orchard and ruined them, and either he hadn’t noticed or he had decided it was a fair price for a hedge. She couldn’t decide which was worse.

He was studying her too, openly, the way he’d studied the soil. She knew what he saw, because she had arranged it: a small parish girl in a man’s old coat, hair hacked short, come over a stile to stare at a stranger. She watched him decide she was what she looked like, and watched a smaller part of him decline to decide anything at all.

Then he breathed in, and frowned. “You smell of oak-gall. Iron-gall ink, or something like it.” He said it almost apologetically, as a man might mention that your collar was turned under. “My mother’s clerks reek of it.”

The blind had a sour green edge that never washed out of the skin entirely, however much you scrubbed with lye. She had scrubbed. It was the alum, Quince used to say. Alum got into you like guilt.

“My mother’s a laundress,” she said. “We dye the mourning blacks. Oak-apples and copperas, three days in the vat. You can smell it on the whole street when someone’s died.” This was true. It had always been true. That was the beauty of it. “There’s a funeral Saturday. Old Pettifer.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be. He was a terrible man. He cheated at skittles.”

Now the smile did come, very briefly, and was put away again like a coin in a purse. “Then I am sorry for the skittles,” he said. “May I ask your name?”

She considered lying. It was a parish of thirty-one families and a mill, and any of them would tell him her name for the pleasure of telling. A lie would cost her nothing tonight and everything tomorrow, when he found it and asked himself why.

“Ilse Marrow.”

He wrote it down. *Ilse Marrow*, in that tall clean hand. It looked strange to her there, too tidy, like a borrowed dress. “Miss Marrow. And do you know whose hedge this is?”

“Mrs. Tull’s. She’s seventy-four and she’s asleep.” Ilse let her voice go sharp. “If you knock her up at this hour about her hedge, sir, you’ll get a pot of something thrown at you, and I’ll say you deserved it.”

“I am not going to knock anyone up,” he said, as though she had suggested he pick his teeth with the folding rule. “I am going to look at the parish chest in the morning, with the assessor, as is proper.” He closed the notebook. “Good night, Miss Marrow. Mind the lane, if you go home that way. It no longer goes where it went.”

She said good night and went back over the stile, not quickly. All along the field edge she felt his lantern lying flat across her back. He wasn’t watching her, she knew. He would be measuring the hedge again. That was worse.

* * *

The vestry linen went back on Fridays.

The surplices of the Ninefold chapel at Wick had to be starched until they stood up without a priest in them, and Ilse had carried them up the church hill every Friday since she was eight. She had never once been glad of it until that morning, when she saw the vestry door propped open with a hassock and heard, from inside, a voice she knew. She set the basket on the side step and took her time over the folding.

The parish chest stood open on the table: oak, iron-bound, three locks. Jem Rudd the clerk stood beside it with the keys and the stricken look of a man whose biggest event of the year had been a dispute about a pew. A stout man in the same grey-blue coat as Kasimir Reyne’s, with the buttons straining a little, sat in the vicar’s chair with his hat on his knee and a slice of seed cake on the hat.

“I’m sure it is all in order, Mr. Rudd.” Pollard ate some cake. “Mr. Reyne is very thorough. Thoroughness is what he is attached to us to learn.” He looked at his watch. “We dine at Saffery Hall at four.”

Kasimir Reyne stood at the window end of the table, where the light was best, with a charter flattened under his two hands.

She knew it by the way it lay, cockled at one corner where Agnes had held it with a damp thumb. Agnes had brought it to Jem yesterday morning: an amendment filed by the landholder, which was every landholder’s right, attested by a local surveyor, which was every hedge-man’s trade. Ilse had done everything properly. It was the improper part she had done better.

“Two charters for one boundary,” Kasimir said. “Corwin Fask’s, under Patent, filed Haysel the ninth. And this amendment, filed yesterday for Mrs. Agnes Tull. Forty-one stations. The lines are very good.” He said it the way a man might report that a bridge was well built, shortly before it fell down. “The surveyor’s mark is not registered with the Court.”

“Hedge-mark,” said Pollard, through cake. “Half the Fallowmarch is charted by hedge-men. Wheelwrights. Old women with a feel for drainage. It’s a scandal, not a crime. A freeholder may survey her own holding. Statute of Small Holdings, 1061.”

“Yes, sir. It is a crime when the survey binds the land to a state contrary to a Patent charter already in force. That is contrary charting.” Kasimir turned the sheet toward the light. Through the gap of the door Ilse saw her own wren’s foot, three lines and a spur, dark and confident in the lower corner. It looked very small from here. “To supersede a competent Patent survey, this would have to be of greater fidelity. I find that improbable for a sheet drawn by a hedge-man, at night, on a farm.”

“Improbable,” said Pollard, “is not evidence.”

“No, sir. Which is why I should like to request a drawing of the mark.”

The words went into Ilse like cold water.

She had seen it done once, at sixteen, to a Fennock wheelwright named Hobb who forged ditches. The Court’s examiner, a dry little woman with a case of lenses, had laid his charters out in the tithe barn, wetted each one and set a damp lens over the signature. A mark was a thread from the hand to the land, and an examiner could follow it home. Under a damp lens, blind ink showed. So did a second mark ghosted beneath the first. And the thread of a ghosted mark ran to whoever carried the burden: in this case to a disgraced hedge-surveyor in the Hobbet churchyard, whose mark was still on the Court’s rolls, and whose apprentice for six years had been a girl named Marrow.

They had cut a line through every one of Hobb’s marks with a silver knife. Every ditch he had ever moved had moved back in one night, and he had gone to the Tessary gaol for nine years. His wife had taken in washing. Hesta had given her the overflow.

“A drawing,” said Pollard. “Over a hedge.”

“Over a contrary charter, sir. If it is one. And there is the other matter. Mr. Rudd, you said a gentleman came to see the Little Wick lane charter on Tuesday.”

“That’s right, sir. A College gentleman. Very civil.”

“May I see it?”

Jem found it near the bottom, folded in four, the ink of the lane’s two banks faded to the colour of weak tea. Kasimir read it twice, and held it to the window and looked through it at the light.

“It is in order,” he said at last.

“It is in order,” Kasimir said again, “and the lane is not.”

There was a silence in the vestry. Pollard put down his cake.

“Old charters,” he said. “Old ground. It tires. Write it up with the others, Reyne. The Tessary office has a theory about the Margin wagons.”

“The nearest Margin wagon is a very long way from here, sir.”

“They are a mobile people,” said Pollard, with dignity. “That is rather the point of them.”

Kasimir let it go. She watched it cost him something. “And the drawing of the mark on the Tull amendment?”

Pollard sighed like a man watching his dinner recede. “The sheet would go to Tessary under seal. Correspondence, an examiner’s calendar. Three weeks at the least, Reyne, and you’ll be gone by then.”

“I will write the request myself, sir. It needs only your signature.”

“Of course it does.” Pollard held out his hand for the pen. “Seal the chest, then. Mr. Rudd, nobody opens it until the Court says so. Nobody. Not the vicar. Not the Ninefold, should they drop by.”

So that door closed too. She had been thinking, in the cool back room of her mind where Quince had taught her to think, of getting into the vestry at night and burning the amendment, or releasing it, unsigning it with her own hand. Agnes would lose her Pearmain, but there would be nothing left to draw. Either way she needed the sheet. She watched Kasimir drip a coin of grey-blue wax across the hasp and press the Concordance seal into it, neatly, and then do it twice more, once on each of the other locks.

Then he looked up and through the door and directly at her.

“Good morning, Miss Marrow,” he said.

Pollard turned round in the vicar’s chair. Jem Rudd turned round. Ilse picked up the basket of surplices and held it in front of her like a shield.

“The vicar’s linen,” she said. “I’ll leave it on the step.”

“Thank you,” said Kasimir Reyne, as if it had been his.

* * *

“You could run,” said Hesta.

It was past eleven. The copper was out and the kitchen smelled of wet ash and cooling starch. Her mother sat at the table with her red hands flat on the scrubbed wood, the way she sat when she meant to stay sitting until something was decided. The candle between them had burned down to a stub. Neither of them had moved to light another.

“Where?” said Ilse.

“Lisk. You’ve a cousin in Lisk.”

“Cousin Barnaby is a canal-warden. He’d have me in a cell by Tuesday.”

“The Court sits everywhere. That’s the point of a Court.” She heard the edge in her voice and put it away, and went to the window. The yard was black, the lane beyond it blacker.

She did what Quince had taught her to do with any trouble big enough to be frightening. She surveyed it.

Station one: Hobbet. Three weeks, a drawing of the mark, and Agnes Tull saying *I didn’t know, dearie*, which would be true and wouldn’t help. Then the Tessary gaol, and every boundary she had ever moved snapping back overnight: the Gethins’ ditch, the chapel glebe, Widow Ames’s goat-run. Every small truth she had made, unmade with a silver knife.

Station two: any town in the Reach. The Court’s rolls went everywhere the Great Meridian Road went, and the Road went everywhere.

Station three: the Margin. The Tenting wouldn’t have her, and the Margin was no place to hide. It was a place where things got lost, which was different.

The Drift wasn’t a station at all. It was the edge of the board.

So she went back and looked again, the way you looked at a traverse that wouldn’t close, and found the error. It was a thing Quince had said once in the Hare and Lantern, with gin on his breath, explaining why the College men walked around the Reach as though they owned it.

*Because they’re not ours, girl. Members of the Meridian answer to the Meridian. Charting offences, any of them, it’s their own Board tries them. Not the Court. The Court can’t touch so much as a link-boy. Privilege. Four hundred years of it. It makes Lady Muck in Tessary spit teeth.*

And how did a smallholder’s daughter become a member of the Meridian? Patent examinations, Patent fees. Or—

*Any fool with the level can try the Walk. Turn up at their gate on the equinox and walk out into the Margin and back. A good few don’t come back. I thought about it once.* He’d laughed into his gin. *Then I thought about it twice.*

“The autumn equinox,” Ilse said aloud.

Her mother looked up.

“It’s the twenty-second. Today’s the sixteenth.” Ilse sat. She found she was counting on her fingers, and stopped. “Five hundred and sixty miles to Cape Verity. The express mail goes through the Bell at dawn tomorrow, day and night, changes at every stage. Four days. I’d be at the Lantern Gate the night before the equinox. Once I’m a member, the Court can’t have me. The College tries me itself. Which takes longer. Which might take—” She stopped.

“How much,” said Hesta.

“What?”

“The coach. Don’t look at me like I’m simple. I’ve been listening to you add sums under your breath for nine years. How much.”

“Thirty-eight marks. Inside.”

Neither of them looked at the chimney.

The tin was there anyway, behind the loose brick, the way a grave is there whether you look at it or not. Forty-four marks and six. Sixteen short of a strip of black soil with a well in it. She could see the frontage in Haysel with the barley standing, and the well-head with its stones ground crooked.

She had meant to buy it back. That had been the whole of the plan since she was fourteen: buy it back, then walk it with a Patent and chain it so true the Court would kneel. She had meant to do it for him. It came to her, with the candle dying, that she had never once asked whether he would have wanted it done this way, and that she wasn’t going to ask now.

“It’s a field,” said Hesta.

“It’s *our* field.”

“It was your father’s field. And he’s dead, and it didn’t save him, and it won’t save you.” Her mother’s voice didn’t rise. It went lower and harder, like a paddle going down into the copper. “I’ll not stand at the back of a courtroom in Tessary watching them take a knife to your name so that you can buy back sixty yards of dirt. I did that once. I did it for a year. You remember what it got us.”

Ilse remembered.

She took out the brick and counted the money onto the table, though she knew to the copper what was there, because counting was a thing to do with her hands. She put six marks and six back in the tin and the rest in her purse. The purse made a sound against the table like a door shutting in another room.

“Six and six,” she said. “For the rent.”

“Keep it.”

“It’s for the rent, Mam.”

Hesta looked at her a long time. Then she lit another candle from the stub and went to the press by the stairs and took out the coat.

It was Daun Marrow’s good coat, dark brown broadcloth gone soft at the elbows, the one he had been married in. The parish lent a coat for burying, and Hesta had not let them have this one. It was too big in the shoulders for Ilse and too long in the arm. Her mother laid it on the table and turned back the lining.

“If you’ll be taking your bits,” Hesta said, not looking at her, “they’ll want to be somewhere a nosy body won’t find them.”

So they sat up until two by a single candle, as they had sat up over other people’s shirts a thousand times. Ilse unpicked the lining along the hem. In went the blind in its corked bottle, rolled in flannel; the damp-lens in its leather; a twist of alum; the fine steel pen Quince had ground for her the year before he died. Her mother stitched it closed again with laundress’s stitches, eleven to the inch, invisible. Neither of them said what it was for.

At four, Hesta wrapped half a loaf and the heel of cheese in a cloth and put it in the coat pocket where the eggs had been.

“Write,” she said, at the door. “You wretch.”

* * *

The Bell’s yard at dawn was all steam and harness and swearing.

The express mail stood under the arch with its lamps still lit, maroon and black, a crowned horn on the door and four greys stamping. The guard looked at Ilse’s thirty-eight marks as though she had made them in a back room. She had not, as it happened. Not these. She paid for an inside seat and then climbed up onto the roof among the mailbags, where the wind would be. She wanted to see.

Across the yard, on the first floor of the Bell, a window stood open.

He was in it, coat off, sleeves rolled, packing a leather case on the bed and folding each shirt into thirds. She watched him lay the black notebook on top and square it to the corners. A post-chaise waited at the side door with a trunk roped on, and the trunk had a label too far away to read.

Then he straightened and looked out of the window and saw her.

He saw her on the roof of the westbound mail with her father’s coat buttoned to the throat and her surveyor’s bag between her knees. She saw him see the bag, and the board on the yard wall chalked TESSARY — VERITY CROSS. She thought he might lift a hand. He didn’t. He only stood very still at the window with a shirt folded in thirds over his arm, the way he had stood at the hedge, checking her against a record.

The guard blew the horn. The greys leaned into the collars, and the mail lurched, and swung, and went out under the arch into the Hobbet road.

She did not look back at the window. She looked at everything else instead.

The sun came up behind them in a long red bar, and the light ran out ahead of the coach across the stubble. Every field she had ever walked went past at eight miles an hour: the Gethins’ forty acres, with the ditch she had moved four feet south in the spring; Widow Ames’s goat-run; the hedge of Saffery’s Home Farm, black and dead straight, which she had never touched. She didn’t look for the Long Field, and saw it anyway, the dark square of the well-head on the wrong side of a line.

Every hedge was a line on a sheet. She knew which ones. She knew whose. She knew Fask’s straight black work and the old parish lines in brown ink gone faint as tea, and her own, the few, the small. Every one of them was a sentence written by someone with a pen and a reason, and the land had believed them all. The hedgerows ran away from her in the low red light, dark green, unbroken, knitting the whole Fallowmarch together from horizon to horizon, and holding it still.
