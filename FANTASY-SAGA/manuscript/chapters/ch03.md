# Chapter 3
## Cape Verity

The Great Meridian Road was the straightest thing Ilse had ever seen, and it had been made that way on purpose.

It began at the Zero Stone, and she joined it there, at Tessary, after two days and two nights of Eastern Post lanes that went round every field as though apologizing. The Road did not apologize. It ran west out of the capital like a ruled line, paved in grey setts worn into two shallow gutters by a thousand years of wheels, with a milestone every mile and a benchmark arrow cut into every milestone. The mail took it at a gallop.

That was how the journey stayed with her afterward: a handful of sheets out of a folio, out of order.

The second passenger on the roof, from Hobbet onward, was a hedge-preacher named Brother Amos, who wore a rope belt and swore on the Atlas the way other men cleared their throats. “Fine weather, swear on the Atlas,” he said, and “Terrible pie, swear on the Atlas,” and, when the mail hit a pothole at Coldharbour and nearly threw him into a hayrick, “Swear on the Atlas and all the Ninefold standing, I will see that coachman *damned*.” He offered to pray over her bag. She told him it had been prayed over already, which was true in the sense that Quince had cursed it often.

There was the night stage, the coach-lamps swinging, and Ilse wedged between two mailbags listening to the level. In the Fallowmarch she had never thought about it, the way you never think about the floor. But as the mail ran west toward Tessary it deepened. Every mile nearer the centre of the Reach, the pressure behind her ears grew steadier and lower, until by midnight it was a note so deep she felt it in her back teeth, like the bottom string of a viol bowed very slowly and never stopping. The ground here had been held for twelve hundred years without a break. It knew exactly where it was. It was almost smug.

There was Tessary at dawn on the third day. She saw it in pieces between warehouses: copper roofs gone green, hundreds of them, stepping down in terraces, and smoke going straight up from every chimney as if even the air stayed where it was put. Then a gap in the buildings, and through the gap, for the space of a long breath, a broad paved plaza and in the middle of it a grey obelisk. It was not tall. She had expected tall. It was perhaps the height of three men, square and plain and very slightly darker than the stone around it, as if it were standing in its own shadow. People walked round it without looking at it, carrying bread.

“The Zero Stone,” said Brother Amos, and took off his hat. “Where they first set the chain down. Every boundary in the Reach is measured back to that, swear on—”

“I know,” said Ilse.

She did know. She had worked out once, with Quince, how many chains it was from the Zero Stone to the bottom of the Gethins’ ditch, and had felt for a moment a long thread running through the earth from her hand to that stone. She looked at it until the buildings closed again.

There was the afternoon west of Tessary when the land began to thin.

That was the only word for it. Nothing looked different: sheep country, then long downs with chalk showing through like knuckles. But the level grew lighter, as though someone were taking weight off the bow, until it had a reedy, uncertain edge she didn’t like. The levelglass in her bag felt it too. When the mail stopped to change horses at Garrow she took it out on the inn wall and watched the little bead of quicksilver wander off true and drift slowly back, like a cow that has seen something interesting in the next field.

And on the last day, the Road came to the Margin.

The Road ran along its edge for nearly forty miles, high on the downs, and the Margin lay on their left hand all the way to a smudged horizon: heath, brown and purple, broken by glints of standing water. Nothing on it looked wrong. That was what unsettled her. But the level dropped and steadied and dropped again as the coach went along, and she found her right hand closing on the strap of her bag without being told to.

Then she noticed the milestones.

Every one of them, along that forty miles, had a new benchmark arrow. The old arrows were there too, weathered soft and furred with lichen, but beside or beneath each one somebody had cut a fresh one. The notches were sharp and pale, the stone in them bright as a new cut in cheese. At one milestone the new arrow was so recent that the chips still lay in the grass at its foot.

“The College men,” said Brother Amos, following her look. “They come along every few weeks now. Never used to. Re-signing the Road.” He spat over the side, politely. “Afraid it’ll wander off.”

“Would it?”

“Swear on the Atlas,” said Brother Amos, “I hope not, my girl. I’ve a sister in Verity Cross.”

* * *

They came over the last of the downs at dusk on the twenty-first, and there was the sea.

She had never seen it. She had expected to be moved, the way people in songs were, and was mostly surprised by how *flat* it was. It lay out to the north and west like a sheet of beaten pewter, darkening, with a long scarf of cloud lying on it far out and lit underneath by the last of the sun. The land ran down toward it in a long tongue of granite: Cape Verity, black against the water, with the lights of the town strung along the near end of it.

And at the far end, on the headland, was the Meridian.

She saw the Held Side first, because it was meant to be seen first. It was honey-coloured stone that held the last light the way a loaf holds heat, quadrangles and towers and long slate roofs, and in the middle of its western face stood a single tall arch with a light burning on top of it. The Lantern Gate. Even from two miles off, the light did not flicker.

Beyond the Gate, the College went on, and changed.

It ran out from the arch along the spine of the headland and then down off it, onto the dark heath beyond, in a long, loose, rambling line of buildings. Halls and glass domes, stair-turrets and covered bridges, joined end to end as if added one at a time over centuries by people who didn’t speak to each other. It went on for half a mile or more, getting lower and stranger as it went, and it curved, very gently, the way a sleeping animal curves when it lies down in long grass with its tail out behind it. Windows were lit all along its length, hundreds of them, small and gold. The Drift Wing.

She was looking at one window in particular, low down near the far end, a single gold square with a dark shape moving in it, when it moved.

It slid. It went a few feet along the dark, smoothly and without hurry, the way a lamp moves when someone carries it along a corridor. But nobody was carrying the window. The whole of the building around it, the stretch of wall she could just make out by the light of its neighbours, went with it. Then it stopped, and was still, and the window beside it was a little closer than it had been.

The guard blew the horn for Verity Cross. Ilse had the level in her ears like a held breath and her right hand was aching at the knuckles, though she hadn’t signed anything for a week.

* * *

The Chain & Anchor stood at the top of Verity Cross market square, its sign a surveyor’s chain coiled round a ship’s anchor, which seemed to Ilse a joke about something. A room cost two marks. She paid it, thought about the four marks and six left in her purse, and stopped thinking about it on purpose.

The taproom was full of students.

She knew them by their rings, plain iron on the right hand, and by their voices, which were mostly Tessary and loud. She took a bowl of mutton stew to a corner settle by the fire and kept her head down over it and listened.

“—Brask’s taking it again. Fourth year running. Nobody else will.”

“Nobody else is let. Steyne would rather she was out on the heath on the equinox than in the Mess making faces at him.”

“What are the odds this year?”

“On the Walk? One in three come back with a passing sheet. One in three come back with a bad one. One in three—” A low whistle, a hand flat on the table, sliding away.

“You’re foul, Debenham.”

“I am *accurate*.” Laughter. “I’ll tell you what, though, the chains had a shocker this morning. Did you hear? Hollis’s lot. Went out at First Bell and their stair had turned clean round in the night, and they surveyed it the wrong way up and signed it. Their landing’s been facing the sea all day. Arnaud fined them six shillings each.”

“Lost your stair, chains!” said someone, in a falsetto, and the whole table roared and banged their mugs.

A girl near the fire, with her hair in a long Tessary plait, was saying to her friend in a lower voice, “—went out to the heath end of the Second Range this afternoon and the door’s opening on gorse again. Just gorse. Moy says it’ll come back by morning if the Gannet Stair lot get their sheet in—”

Ilse finished her stew and went up to bed.

She slept badly. The bed was good and the sheets were clean, which made it worse. Every time she slid toward sleep she saw the window going along the dark, smooth as a carried lamp. She saw the cobbles at the Wick ford drifting apart, and the wren’s foot under a seal of grey-blue wax. Somewhere toward three she gave up and sat on the windowsill in her shift with the casement open, and watched the light on the Lantern Gate burning two miles off, steady, small, unblinking, until the sky behind it started to go grey.

* * *

There were nineteen of them under the Gate before dawn.

The arch was bigger close to. It rose sixty feet or more, plain honey stone with no ornament but a single line of letters cut across the span, each letter as tall as a child: WE RECORD WHAT IS. The lantern sat on the keystone in a cage of black iron, burning yellow and steady. The fog had come in off the sea in the night. It lay over everything beyond the arch in a soft grey wall, and the lantern-light went into it a little way and stopped.

A sour clerk took their names. He wrote *Wick Fallow* as though it were a disease.

They stood about in the grey half-light, nineteen people not looking at each other. One boy of about eighteen had brought a chain so new it still had the chandler’s tag on it, and he kept counting the links under his breath. A gaunt, silent man of thirty or so in a quarryman’s jacket stood off by himself against the wall of the arch. His name, when the clerk called it, was Oskar Fell. He didn’t speak to anyone, then or later.

A girl about Ilse’s height but twice as wide in the shoulder stepped up beside her, grinning.

“Yara,” she said, and stuck out a hand. “Yara Penhallow. Dairy farm, out past Holm, other side of Tessary. You’re a farmer too. I can tell by your hands.”

“Laundress.”

“Same hands.” Yara beamed. She had a round red face and fair eyebrows that went up when she talked, which was often. “My da says the Walk is for people who can’t afford the Patent fees or the sense to stay home. I said that’s me, Da, both halves. Our old bull, Hector, he’s held like a church, you could survey off him—”

Something very large trod on Ilse’s foot.

“Oh, I’m sorry, I’m so sorry, I didn’t—that was your foot. That was definitely a foot. I’m so sorry.”

He was the biggest young man she had ever seen, well over six feet and broad all the way down, round-faced, with curly auburn hair and a beard that was trying hard and not quite managing. He was sweating, though the morning was cold enough to show breath. Held against his chest with both arms like a baby was a pie in a cloth, at least a foot across.

“Teo. Teodor Anselm. Lisk. I’ve brought a pie.” He looked down at it as though it had followed him there. “It’s eel. It’s my mother’s. It’s for the road, or for my wake, she said, whichever comes first, and I don’t think she was entirely joking, though it’s hard to tell with her. Would you—later, I mean. If we’re both—it’s a big pie.”

“I can see that,” said Ilse.

“Road and rain,” said someone behind her. “Paper-walls bring *pies* now?”

The girl leaning against the far pillar of the arch was brown-skinned and freckled, wiry as a whip, with black hair in dozens of long braids. Each braid was tied off with knotted cords in faded colours: red, ochre, a blue gone almost white. She wore a leather jerkin, a skinning knife at her belt and a sling looped round one wrist. She was tossing a smooth grey sling-stone up and catching it, up and catching it, without looking at it.

“It’s a very good pie,” said Teo, with some dignity.

“Smells good. Margin’ll want it too. Hey-ho.” She nodded at Ilse. “Cass. Tenter wagon. You lot ever been out there? Past your nice paper walls?”

“No,” said Ilse.

“No,” said Teo, with feeling.

“Hey-ho.” Cass caught the stone and grinned, all teeth. “Going to be a long day for somebody.”

Through the arch behind them, out of the fog, came a sound of chain.

Four students passed through the lantern-light in a bunch, walking fast, collars up, one with a coiled chain over her shoulder and one with a levelglass held out in front of him like a man carrying a full cup. One was reciting under his breath, “—three chains, twelve, three chains, thirteen—” and none of them looked at the Walkers at all. Behind them came another party, and then another, out of the dark of the Old Court and through the Gate and on into the fog toward the Drift Wing, chains clinking, lanterns bobbing, the low voices counting. Ilse heard a bell ringing somewhere behind them in the Held Side, a single heavy note, then another.

“Morning Survey,” said Yara, in a reverent whisper. “They do it every day. Every single day. Can you imagine?”

Ilse could. She watched the lanterns go out along the causeway one by one into the grey, and thought of the window sliding along the dark, and of what it would take to get up every morning of your life before dawn and walk a building back into its place.

“Walkers,” said a voice. “Here.”

* * *

Field-Surveyor Odile Brask came out of the fog on the Drift side of the arch, which was the wrong side to come from, and did not look as though she cared.

She was tall and rawboned, with white hair cropped close and a face the weather had been working on for sixty years. A black leather patch covered her right eye. Her left hand hung at her side with the fingers half-curled, and the skin of it was grey: not pale, not bloodless, but grey right through, the colour of slate, from the fingertips to somewhere under her cuff. She wore no glove on it. In her right hand she carried a brass-shod staff marked down its length in links, and leaned on it as if it had been made for her.

She looked at them. The one eye went along the line of nineteen and came back, unhurried, the way Ilse would look along a line of pins to see which one was out of true.

“This is the Long Lie,” she said, and lifted the staff an inch. “It is one rod exactly, and it is the only honest thing in the building, and it lies, because it’s held. I mention it so you’ll know where it is if I hit you with it.” She leaned on it again. “You’ve come to Walk. Fine. Here is the Walk. Go to the cairn. Come back. Bring a true survey. Don’t die. It’s paperwork.”

Somebody laughed nervously. Brask let the laugh go out into the fog and die there by itself.

“The cairn is three miles southwest, as the crow flies, which you aren’t. It is a heap of stones with a College benchmark on top and a crow, usually, who is not your friend. Survey your route there and back by chain and sight, draft it and attest it under this arch. You may carry one chain, one levelglass, one sightvane, one plane-table board and one water flask. Anything else you carry is your own affair. Food is not an instrument. Eat what you like.” Her eye rested briefly on Teo’s pie. “Within reason. No assistance from anyone who is not a Walker. No horses. No maps but your own. You have until dusk tomorrow. A sheet that comes in after the lantern’s lit is late, and a late sheet is a dead sheet, and I don’t care how pretty it is.”

She shifted her weight on the staff.

“Some of you aren’t levelled. You’ll find out when you attest. Nothing will happen, and you’ll have had a nice walk. The Margin is not trying to kill you. It is not trying anything. Ground out there is about as sure of itself as a drunk on a stair, and it will lie to your chain and your eyes and, if you let it, your feet. If you get lost, sit down. Don’t follow lights. Don’t follow voices. Don’t follow anyone who says they’ve found a shortcut. There are no shortcuts. If there were, I’d have found them.”

“And if we see a hollowing?” said the boy with the new chain, in a high voice.

Brask looked at him for a long moment.

“Then you’ll have something to tell your grandchildren, Walker,” she said, “if you’re careful. Any other questions? No. Good.”

Someone had come out of the Held Side behind them while she spoke: an old man in a master’s gown of dark green standing just inside the arch, smiling at them all as if they were his own grandchildren and had come a very long way to visit.

He was bald, with a short white beard trimmed neat, and half-moon spectacles that caught the lantern-light. His face was round and lined in all the places where people line from smiling. He wore grey kid gloves, fine as a second skin; Ilse, who looked at how things were made before she looked at anything else, noticed that the stitching at the fingers was too small to count.

“Field-Surveyor,” he said to Brask, pleasantly.

“Master Hallam.” Brask did not move. “Come to see them off?”

“I always do, Odile. You know I always do.” He came forward among them. He touched the shoulder of the boy with the new chain and said something to him that made him nod hard, twice. He came to Yara and asked her where she was from and said, “Holm! Good dairy country. Held like a church, Holm,” so that Yara went pink with delight. He came to Teo and looked at the pie and his eyes crinkled behind the spectacles. “Is that Lisk eel? Then you will certainly come back to us, my boy. The Margin could never digest it.”

Teo laughed, surprised.

Then he came to Ilse. He looked at her face for a moment, kindly and with interest, and at her bag, and at the ink stain on her right forefinger that no lye had ever shifted.

“You’re a long way from home, I think, my dear.”

“Fallowmarch, sir.”

“Ah. Good straight hedges, the Fallowmarch.” He smiled. “She’s a wide old country, the Reach, and there’s not a field of her that isn’t loved by someone. Remember that, out there. The Ninefold said it at the Holding: *we do not ask the ground to love us, only to know us.*” He laid a gloved hand on her shoulder, light, warm through the wool. “Walk true, and come home.”

“Thank you, sir,” said Ilse, and found to her surprise that she meant it.

He moved on. She looked up, without quite knowing why, at the gallery.

A stone gallery was cut into the inner face of the arch, and it was full of students. Patent first-years, she thought; they had the scrubbed, anxious, pleased look of people at the start of something. They leaned on the balustrade and looked down at the Walkers the way people at a fair look at the animals before a race.

He was at the end of the row, a little apart. His hair was combed. His coat was not grey-blue anymore but black, a student’s plain black with a white stock, and on his right hand was an iron ring. He was looking down at her already. He had perhaps been looking down at her since before she looked up.

She saw him understand. She saw it go across his face, small and exact, like a line ruled in ink. *Of course. Privilege. The Walk.* His mouth went tight at one corner. Then he did a thing she didn’t expect. He inclined his head to her, very slightly, the way one duellist acknowledges another across a field before they begin.

She didn’t return it. She looked away, and found that Brask’s one eye was on her.

It had gone from Ilse to the gallery and back again. It had seen the whole thing. Brask didn’t say anything for a moment. Then she said, “Friend of yours, Walker?”

“No.”

“Good,” said Brask. “Friends are heavy.” She turned away and lifted the Long Lie and brought it down once on the flagstones of the arch, a flat hard crack that went out into the fog and didn’t come back. “Walkers. Go.”

They went, nineteen in a ragged line, out from under the arch and down the worn steps to the path off the headland. Ilse went with them. Teo was on her right, clutching his pie. Cass was on her left, the sling-stone going up and down, up and down. Ahead of them the fog stood waiting, soft and grey and absolutely blank, like a sheet before the first line.

She walked out of the lantern’s light, and into it.
