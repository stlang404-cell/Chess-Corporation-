# Chapter 4
## The Walk

The first thing the Margin did was move her pin.

It was an iron chaining-arrow, one of the ten on a ring at the end of her chain, and she had pushed it into the turf at station two not twenty minutes before. Now she stood at station three, sixty-four links on, with her eye to the sightvane. The rag she had tied to the arrow was there in the hairline, fluttering, in the wrong place. The bearing she had taken forward from station two and the back-sight disagreed by a degree and a half.

Ilse straightened and looked back. Nothing had happened. The heath lay as it had lain, heather and tussock grass silvered with fog, a few gorse bushes hunched like men in cloaks. Far behind her, faint, the bell of the Meridian rang the hour.

She walked back and re-chained the leg. Sixty links, not sixty-four.

“Bloody hell,” she said to the heath, which didn’t care.

The fog had lifted an hour after the Gate into a white, sourceless brightness, so that the Margin lay under a lid of light like the inside of a shell. Nineteen Walkers had come down off the headland together and spread like a handful of seed. Now Ilse could see only two: Oskar Fell, walking fast and straight and alone, already small, and the giant from Lisk, labouring along a ridge to the south with his pie.

She had been doing it properly. That was the galling part. She had begun at the benchmark post at the foot of the headland steps, held as hard as a church door, oriented her board by back-sight to the lantern on the Gate, and chained the way Quince had taught her. Peg the follower end, walk out the hundred links, pin the leader, walk back, pull the follower. Sight. Plot. Every leg checked twice. It was work that held like iron in the Fallowmarch.

It was also slow, and the Margin did not hold still to be measured. It moved while she was walking back for the other end of her own chain.

She made herself think instead of swearing. The ground had not jumped. It had *crept*, in the time it took her to measure it. So the answer was not to measure better, but faster than it crept. Short legs, then. Thirty links, forty at most. Take the bearing forward and the back-sight immediately, within a minute of each other, before the ground had time to change its mind. Close a little triangle on some third mark at every second station so that she could see her error and how big it was. And write down the time. If the error grew with time, that was the ground creeping. If it didn’t, that was her.

It was hideous work, half running, the chain snaking in the heather. But at the next station the back-sight was out by a quarter of a degree, and at the one after that by less, and she plotted the stations in a tight little zigzag with the minute beside each one.

At station eleven she noticed that the ground was breathing.

She stood still and paid attention. The heath rose. It rose perhaps an inch, all of it, heather and stones and the little pool of rainwater beside her, whose water crept up one bank and down the other. Then it sank. The pause between was longer than her own breath. Rise, and hold, and sink. The level in her ears went with it, thinning and thickening, a sound she had never heard it make.

“Ground’s leaning,” said a voice. “Can’t you feel it?”

It was the Tenting girl, Cass, coming along the heath at a lope with her chain coiled on one shoulder, looking at nothing in particular, the way a dog looks at nothing when it is listening.

“I can feel it breathing,” said Ilse.

“That’s nothing. That’s just the Margin sleeping.” Cass squinted at Ilse’s board and whistled through her teeth. “Pretty. You lot draw like your mothers were looking. But you’re fighting it. It’s leaning *that* way.” She pointed southwest without looking. “Everything here’s sliding off toward the Drift, slow, like a plate on a tilted table. You chain across the lean, it fights you. Chain with it, it don’t notice you.”

“That’s not how a survey works.”

“That’s how *ground* works, paper-wall. Ground don’t care what you wrote on it.” Cass went on, stepping her distances, crouching now and then to lay a palm on the turf, and always going a little left or right of where Ilse would have gone, and always, Ilse saw with intense irritation, onto firmer ground.

She closed her eyes and leaned her attention into the ground the way she leaned it into a charter. And there, under the breathing, so faint she might have imagined it, was a lean: a slope in the level itself, the way a floor in an old house runs gently downhill toward one corner. It ran southwest.

She opened her eyes and laid out her next leg along it. The back-sight came in out by nothing at all.

“Bloody hell,” said Ilse again, in a different voice entirely, and picked up her chain.

* * *

The giant from Lisk was sitting on the bank of a creek with his head in his hands.

She came on him a little before noon, where a salt creek crossed her line between banks of grey mud fringed with samphire. It was perhaps thirty links across. The pie sat beside him on a tussock in its cloth, like a guest.

“Don’t look at it,” he said, as she came up. “I mean—sorry. Hello. You can look at it. I can’t. It gets wider.”

“What does?”

“The creek.” His face was grey under the sunburn. “When I got here it was twelve links. I chained it. Then I looked up to sight the far bank and it was eighteen. Then I looked down at my board and back up and it was twenty-two, and the far bank keeps going *away*, and the ground is going up and down, and I don’t do well with up and down. Or away. I’m sorry. I think I’d better sit down for a while.”

As she watched, the far bank went back a little further, the mud shining as the water spread to fill the gap.

The sensible thing was to leave him. She was thinking that quite clearly, in Quince’s voice, when she heard herself say, “How fast?”

Teo blinked at her. “What?”

“How fast is it widening? You chained it at twelve. When?”

“At—” He looked at the sky, then at nothing, and his face changed, like a drowning man’s who has found the bottom with his toe. “At eleven and a quarter, by my watch. It was eighteen links at eleven-twenty. Twenty-two at about eleven twenty-six. So that’s six links in five minutes and then four in six, which isn’t linear, it’s slowing, unless it isn’t, unless it comes in—oh.” He sat up straight. “It comes in breaths. Doesn’t it? It goes with the up and down. It widens on the rise and then it comes back a little on the fall, only not as much as it went, so the whole thing creeps out. Like a pastry rising. Like—sorry. Like a pastry.”

“Count it,” said Ilse. “Count me a breath.”

He counted, staring at his own knees. The ground rose under them, and held, and sank. “Forty-one seconds,” he said. “Forty-one and a bit. And the creek closes up, it closes about three links on the fall, I think, give or take.”

“Then we cross on the fall.” She was uncoiling her chain. “I take the leader end and wade. You hold the follower and count. When I’m over, I pin my end and you come across along the chain. Don’t look at the bank. Look at the links and read them off, and that’s the crossing measured by both of us, and nobody can say we didn’t.”

“I’m not sure that’s allowed,” said Teo. “No assistance from non-Walkers—”

“We’re all Walkers,” said Ilse. “A chain’s got two ends for a reason.”

He looked at her. Then he got up, hands shaking, and took the follower end of her chain in both fists.

“Road and rain,” said Cass, from behind them. “Two paper-walls drowning in a ditch.”

She was sitting on a tussock twenty yards up the bank, eating an apple. Ilse had no idea how long she had been there.

“If you’ve something useful to say,” said Ilse, “say it.”

Cass chewed, and seemed to consider whether she wanted to say it to them. Then she pointed upstream with the apple.

“You’re at the wide bit,” she said. “Course you are. Paper-walls always go where it’s *narrow*, never where it’s *narrowing*. Up there, forty yards, the ground’s leaning in from both sides. Banks are pulling together, not apart. Go cross there. You’ll get your feet wet. Won’t get your feet wet up to your neck.”

It was true. At the bend the creek was eleven links across, and on the fall of the ground’s slow breath the far bank came *toward* her, a hand’s breadth, then another.

She waded on the fall with the leader end, the water cold to her thighs, and the far bank stayed where it was. She pinned the chain and shouted, and Teo came across, eyes fixed on the links, reading them off in a high cracked voice, “—four, five, six, seven—” all the way, and when he stepped up onto the far bank he sat straight down again in the samphire and put both hands flat on the ground and laughed.

“Eleven links and nine,” he said. “Eleven and nine. I’ll never forget it. I’m going to have it carved on something.”

Cass came over last, not wading at all. She stepped from a stone to a hummock to a stone, three steps, and landed light beside them.

“Hey-ho,” she said. “Now you owe me.”

* * *

They didn’t discuss it. They simply went on together, the three of them.

Teo took the leader end because his legs were longest, and Ilse took the follower and read, and Cass ranged ahead with her nose in the wind and called back the lean. All three sighted and plotted at every station, each on their own board, in three very different hands. Ilse’s was small and exact. Teo’s numbers were beautiful and his lines a little wobbly. Cass’s board looked as though a crow had walked across it after a fight, and was, when Ilse glanced at it, broadly right.

The fog came back in the middle of the afternoon.

It came up off the salt-pools, low and thick, until they were walking in a white room twenty yards across that moved with them. Their voices came back flat. The clink of the chain seemed to come from the left.

Then Teo stopped dead, with the chain going taut in Ilse’s hand.

“Do you hear that?”

“Hear what?”

“I don’t *hear* it.” He had his head on one side. “Sorry, that’s a stupid thing to say. I feel it. In my feet. Like standing on the bridge at Lisk when a barge goes under. But lower.”

She stood still, and then she felt it too.

It came up through her boots into the long bones of her legs: a sound too low to be a sound, a vast slow thrumming that climbed and sank. It had a tune, almost. Her teeth hummed with it. The pool beside them shivered in rings.

Cass had gone very still, ahead of them in the fog. She turned her head toward the south.

“Oh,” she said, very softly. “Oh, there you are.”

And the fog parted all at once, like a curtain pulled. Two hundred yards off there was a hill.

It was an ordinary hill: a long whaleback of heather and grey stone with a stand of thorn trees near the crest. She had sighted on it before the fog came down.

The hill began to rise.

It swelled. The whole long back of it lifted, as the sea lifts under a moored boat, and the heather on its flanks rippled and parted. Then the turf began to slide. It slid off in sheets and slabs, peeling away like the skin off hot milk, and stones went rolling and bounding down in little avalanches. Underneath was grey. It was a grey like wet slate, enormous and smooth and gleaming, scarred with old pale weals and crusted along the ridge with flints and roots and the white shells of creatures she didn’t know. The thorn trees rode on top of it. They leaned, their roots tearing out in long pale threads, and toppled, and slid away down the grey slope like toys off a tilted tray.

It came up, and up, longer than the whole of Hobbet market square. Earth gone liquid sheeted off its flanks in brown falls that steamed in the cold air. It rose in a slow arc and then, just as unhurried, began to roll. As it rolled, a seam opened in the grey hide near what must have been its front.

It was an eye.

The eye was the size of a cartwheel. It was dark and wet and very deep, dark the way a well is dark, with a ring of pale like frost around it. It turned in its socket, slow as a millstone. It turned and found the three of them standing on the heath with their chain between them, and it looked at them. She had been looked at by Kasimir Reyne over a hedge and by Brask under the Lantern Gate, and she knew what being looked at was. This was that, from so far down that she felt it in the soles of her feet.

Then the eye closed, the seam sealed, and the great grey back went on rolling over and down. The hill closed over it. It closed like water over a diver, heather and stone and turf running together, folding in, smoothing over, and a long ripple went out from it across the heath in rings, one after another. The rings came toward them. The ground under Ilse’s feet rose a foot, gently, and held, and set her down again, and the next ring did the same, and the next, smaller each time, until they stopped.

The hill was a hill again. It was a little lower than it had been. The thorn trees were gone.

The song went on for a while under their feet, going away south. Then that was gone too.

Ilse realized she couldn’t hear the level.

It wasn’t like the Little Wick lane. That had been a cut, a note stopped dead. This was a silence of a different kind, the way birds go silent when a hawk goes over. The level was still there, she thought. It had simply stopped speaking, as though nothing in the world were fixed enough to register beside what had just gone by.

Cass had knelt down in the heather. She put both palms flat on the ground and bent her head and said a few words in a language Ilse didn’t know, a soft run of words with the shape of a blessing. Then, in plain Reach speech, very quietly: “Go easy, old one. Road and rain go with you.”

Teo was crying. He wasn’t making any noise about it. He stood with the chain hanging from one big hand and tears running down into his poor beard, looking at the place where the hill had opened, and he didn’t wipe them away.

“A sounder,” he said at last. “My grandfather saw one. He said it was the size of a church, and nobody believed him.” He sniffed enormously. “It was bigger than a church.”

Ilse found that her right hand was gripping the edge of her board so hard her knuckles had whitened. She made it let go. She looked down at her neat zigzag of stations, and at the place where she had plotted that hill an hour ago, carefully, in brown ink. It had been an animal all along. She had drawn it as though it would stay.

The level came back like feeling into a foot you’ve sat on, stinging.

* * *

Yara called to them half an hour later.

They had re-chained the stretch where the ripples went through, because every station had moved, and were working along the edge of a grey mere when a voice came out of the fog to their right, bright and close and laughing.

“Halloo! Is that you lot? It’s Yara! There’s a dry way round the mere, it’s firm as a floor, I’ve found a shortcut—come on, it saves a mile at least—”

“Yara!” Ilse turned toward the voice. “Wait. Wait there. Brask said—”

Nothing.

“Yara?”

The fog moved a little, and showed nothing. The water lapped at the mud. Cass had put a hand on Ilse’s arm, hard.

“Don’t,” Cass said.

“She was right there.”

“She was. Now she ain’t.” Cass’s face had gone closed and still. “You heard what the one-eyed woman said. Don’t follow voices.”

“It was *her* voice.”

“Yes,” said Cass. “That’s what makes it a voice.”

They called until their throats were raw, Teo’s great voice booming over the mere and coming back flat. Nobody answered. When at last they went on, Ilse looked back twice, and the second time she thought she saw a shape in the fog, round-shouldered and cheerful, walking away from them along a path that wasn’t there. Then it wasn’t anything.

* * *

The cairn stood on a knoll above the heath, and they reached it as the light was going.

It was a rough cone of grey stones taller than a man, with an iron post rising from it and a brass plate cut with a benchmark arrow and the words MERIDIAN — WALK CAIRN — HELD. On top sat a grey-necked crow as big as a hen, building a cairn of its own out of pebbles. It placed each one, considered it, adjusted it, and looked at the three of them with a bright, unimpressed eye.

“Evening,” said Teo to the crow, politely.

The crow put down another pebble.

Ilse closed her traverse on the brass arrow by the last of the light. A hundred and seventy-one stations, through a Margin that crept and breathed and had once stood up and looked at her, and her closing error was nine links in three miles. She checked it three times and felt something warm and fierce come up under her breastbone, and said nothing about it to anyone.

They camped in a hollow on the lee of the knoll. Other Walkers’ fires showed here and there around the cairn, small and orange. Teo built theirs with tremendous care, and then unwrapped the pie.

It had survived, battered on one side, its crust glazed brown and crimped all round like rope. He cut it with Cass’s skinning knife into exact sixths, and then cut the sixths into twelfths, so there would be more.

It was eel, and onion, and something green, and pepper, and a jelly that had set in the cold. It was the best thing Ilse had ever eaten.

“My mother’s,” said Teo, with his mouth full. “She makes forty on a market day. Forty-one, if it’s a good day. I wanted it to be a good day.”

“It was,” said Cass. She lay on her back looking up. The fog had gone, and there were more stars than Ilse had ever seen over the Fallowmarch. After a while Cass began to sing, low, more talked than sung.

*The road to Corrow goes by the high stone*
*when the moon is new,*
*and the low ford when it’s old,*
*and the white thorn when it’s neither,*
*and you’ll know the white thorn*
*by the crow that sits on it*
*and the crow by the thorn.*

It went on like that for some time, verse after verse, a road that went one way in one season and another way in the next. Teo lay back and listened with his hands folded on his belly. Ilse sat with her arms round her knees.

A map that moved. A route written down not as where a thing was, but as what it did. She turned the idea over once, as she would a stone the plough had brought up, and put it away. Roads didn’t move. Held roads didn’t, anyway. That was what a road *was*.

“What’s that for?” she said, when Cass stopped.

“For? For getting to Corrow.” Cass turned her head and looked at her. “Why’d you come, Fallowmarch?”

“Why did you?”

“Nan told me to. My grandmother. She said, go and learn what the paper-walls know, and then come home and tell us.” Cass grinned at the stars. “I think she wanted to know if they knew anything at all.”

“I failed the Patent,” said Teo, to the fire. “Twice. I know the whole Mensuration syllabus backward, but they put you in a hall with two hundred desks and a very loud clock, and my hand—” He held it up and looked at it. “So I thought, well. There aren’t any desks on the Walk. My mother said I was the stubbornest fool in Lisk. I think she was pleased.”

They both looked at Ilse.

“I wanted to be a surveyor,” she said.

Cass laughed. It was a short, warm, delighted laugh, and it went up into the dark like a spark.

“Liar,” she said. “Nice one, though.”

Ilse didn’t answer. After a while Teo began to snore, softly, like a kettle a long way off. Cass turned on her side with her braids across her face. The fire burned down. The crow on the cairn above them had gone to sleep, or was pretending to.

Ilse lay awake on the hard ground with her father’s coat over her and her board under her head. She listened to the Margin. Under her back, very slowly, it rose, and held, and sank. It rose again. It was not trying to do anything, Brask had said. It was only breathing, as a sleeper breathes. Ilse lay and listened to it and did not sleep for a long time, and she could not have said, if anyone had asked her, whether what she felt was fear.
