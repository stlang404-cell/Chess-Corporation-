# Chapter 5
## A True Survey

In the morning the way home was somewhere else.

Ilse sat up under her father’s coat in the grey first light with dew on her face and looked down from the knoll the way they had come, and the land was wrong. There was the heath, and the long pale gleam of the mere, and the low whaleback hill where the sounder had gone down. But the mere lay further west than it had. The hill was on the wrong side of it. And the salt creek, which should have been a brown thread winding in from the left, wasn’t anywhere.

“Hey-ho,” said Cass, sitting up beside her with her braids full of heather. She looked at the view and yawned enormously. “Ground’s been walking.”

“All of it?”

“All of it that wanted to.” Cass stretched. “Told you. It leans. It leaned all night.”

Teo woke last and saw it and lay back down again and put his arm over his eyes.

On the cairn the crow stood on its finished tower of pebbles, looking pleased with itself. The cairn had not moved at all. When Ilse laid her levelglass on its base the bead of quicksilver sat dead centre, as steady as a seed pearl. Held. The College had put its mark on this one heap of stone out in the half-world and the Margin had flowed round it all night like water round a pier.

So there was that, at least. Her traverse closed on something that stayed put.

They ate the last of the pie for breakfast, three twelfths, cold. Then they sat in a row on the cairn’s lee side with their boards on their knees and looked at what they had.

“The first mile’s all right,” said Teo, comparing their boards with his big careful finger. “Near the Held ground it hardly moves. We can check it and annotate the creep. But from the creek on—” He made a small helpless gesture at the landscape. “That’s all gone somewhere. We’ll have to do the whole back half again. Coming home.”

“A hundred and ten stations,” said Ilse.

“A hundred and eleven. And we’ve until dusk. And I don’t want to be a nuisance,” said Teo, “but I would point out that we’ve also got no pie.”

* * *

The way back was worse than the way out.

It was the same heath, the same slow lean toward the southwest that Ilse could feel now without trying, like a draught along the floor. But every leg they chained was a leg they had chained the day before, lying in a slightly different place, so that the morning had the maddening quality of copying out a letter you had already written, from memory, with the words rearranged.

Short legs, quick sights, triangles closed every second station. They found the salt creek again an hour before noon, nearly a furlong east of where it had been, wide and lazy and only knee-deep now, as if it had exhausted itself. They found the mere where Yara had called out of the fog. Nobody said anything about that. Ilse looked for footprints in the mud along its edge and found plenty, and none of them meant anything.

The bell of the Meridian rang noon, faint across the heath, and then one o’clock, and two.

At a quarter past two they came to a stream.

It was nothing much: a peaty brook a yard wide between cut banks of black earth, with a stone in the middle you could step on. She had chained across it the day before without a second thought. Today it was a few rods west of where it had been, like everything else, and they chained across it again, and Ilse plotted, and closed the triangle.

The triangle didn’t close.

She re-took the sight. She made Teo re-chain the leg. She made Cass stand on the stream stone with the ranging-rod while she sighted from both sides. The numbers came back the same each time, and wrong. Between the station on this bank and the station on that, the stream bank was fourteen links out from where every other measurement said it should be. Not four links, which she could have called creep. Fourteen. Nine feet and a quarter, as though the ground there simply hadn’t agreed with itself about where it was.

“Huh,” said Cass. She crouched by the water with her palm on the earth, frowning. “It’s—huh. It’s leaning both ways here. That’s queer.”

“It can’t lean both ways.”

“Well, it is.” Cass stood and wiped her hand on her jerkin. “Ground does what ground does.”

Teo was writing in his field-book, steadily, in his round clear numbers. He wrote the fourteen links down. He drew a little box round it. Beside it he wrote *misclose — stream — cause unknown — ground leaning (Cass) — re-chained x3*, and then a careful line underneath, and he looked up at Ilse and said, “That’s all right. We’ll just record it. It’ll be a bit ugly, but—”

“Yes,” said Ilse.

She looked at her board.

It was beautiful. She could admit that, on her heels on the wet bank with her eyes gritty from two days of squinting through a sightvane. Two hundred and thirty stations plotted in fine brown ink at ten chains to the inch, the route crossing the Margin in one long confident line that bent where the ground had made it bend. It was the best survey she had ever drawn, of the strangest country she had ever seen.

And there, across the middle of it, sat the stream, with its banks fourteen links apart from themselves.

She knew what it would look like drawn. Two lines that didn’t meet, a stutter in the long clean run, and a note saying, in effect, *I don’t know.* She had seen surveys like that in the Hobbet parish chest, made by honest men with shaking hands, with notes that said *uncertain* and *as near as may be*. They were the weakest charters in the chest. The ground hardly bothered to believe them.

The bank was *there*. She could see it was there. Any fool could see it ran straight, on from the bank above and the bank below, a little curve of black peat that ought to sit, by every station on either side of it, just so. The fourteen links weren’t the bank. They were the ground having a bad moment, the way the Long Field’s well-head had ground its stones crooked in the night. They were an error. And an error, unrecorded, was only a gap; but an error *corrected* was a truth told better.

*A good lie is ninety-nine parts truth*, Quince said, in the back room of her head. *The hundredth part’s just tidying.*

She took out her little knife and scraped the ink of the bank-line gently off the paper, along an inch of its length, so lightly the surface didn’t roughen. She blew the shavings off. She dipped her pen. She drew the bank where it ought to be, in a single unbroken stroke, and she drew the stream between its banks running clean and narrow, and she joined the stations on either side of it to the new line without a seam. She did it in less than a minute. Her hand didn’t shake once. When she sat back the board was whole and lovely and the route ran across the Margin without a stutter, and the stream lay in it like a vein in a leaf.

It felt like the right thing. It felt like making the sheet tell the truth.

“There,” she said, and stood up, and rolled her shoulders. “Let’s go. We’ve two hours.”

Teo glanced at her board as she shouldered it. He looked at the stream, and then at his own sheet with the fourteen links in its careful box, and opened his mouth.

“I’ve drawn it as near as may be,” said Ilse.

“Oh,” said Teo. “Yes. Right.” He closed his book.

Cass, already ranging ahead, didn’t look back at all.

* * *

They came up the steps from the heath at four o’clock in the afternoon, with an hour and a half of daylight to spare, and the Lantern Gate was full of people.

She had pictured coming in to a clerk and a ledger. Instead the arch was crowded with students, and someone had brought out an urn of something steaming, and when the three of them came up over the top of the steps, filthy to the waist, a ragged cheer went up from the gallery. It was the kind of cheer people give for anyone who comes in, because they have been waiting and counting. But it went into Ilse like the first mouthful of hot tea, and she found she had to stop on the top step for a moment and look at her boots.

“Oh, *well done*. Well done, all three of you.”

Master Hallam came through the crowd toward them with both hands out. He took Teo’s hand first, in both his gloved ones, and shook it, and then Cass’s, though Cass looked at her hand afterward as if he might have put something in it. Then he took Ilse’s, and held it a moment, warm and light.

“Home again,” he said, “and the Margin no worse for you, and you no worse for her. Get some tea into you. Field-Surveyor Brask is in the Hall with the sheets that have come in; you’ll attest here first.”

“How many,” said Ilse. “Sir. How many have come in?”

“You make nine, my dear. Nine of nineteen, and the day not done. That’s a good year.” He smiled, and the smile was not quite as easy as before. “We’ll hope for a better one by dusk.”

Teo was staring at Hallam’s hands. He had been since the handshake, Ilse realized, with the open, worried look he gave to anything he thought might be broken. He turned pink when Hallam noticed.

“I’m sorry, sir. It’s just—your gloves, sir. I wondered. Are your hands cold? Only my grandmother has the joint-ache and she says kid’s no good for it, she says you want lambswool—”

Hallam laughed. He looked down at his own hands, the fine grey kid, and flexed the fingers once, slowly, as a man does when he is reminded of an old ache.

“Old Sallow frostbite, my boy,” he said. “Twenty years ago, and more, and they still complain when the wind’s off the sea. Your grandmother is quite right about lambswool. I shall tell my glover.” He patted Teo’s arm. “Go on. Attest.”

They attested under the arch, one at a time, at a stone lectern set in the middle of the passage where the light from the lantern fell. Teo went first and came away shaking. Cass went second and signed her sheet with a quick fierce scrawl like a bird’s track in mud and came away looking at nothing.

Then Ilse laid her board on the lectern.

She took up the College pen they gave her. And there, with the nib over the corner of the sheet, she stopped.

Her hand knew what it wanted to do. It wanted to make the wren’s foot, three lines and a spur, as it had on forty charters in three years. It wanted to reach for the blind afterward and lay the dead man’s mark underneath, line for line, and let him carry it. The bottle was sewn into the hem of the coat she was wearing. She could feel its little weight against the back of her knee.

She signed with neither. She wrote *I. Marrow* in plain capitals, the way a child signs a slate, and set her intent on it, and leaned.

The thread went out.

She felt it go from her hand down through the lectern and the flags of the arch and out across the heath, a long fine line running southwest the way they had come, through a hundred and seventy-one stations to a cairn with a crow on top. It was not like the Tull hedge. There was no strain and no give, no hedge to move. The Margin did not fight her. It merely let her lie on it, lightly, the way a sleeper lets a blanket lie. Only in one place, two-thirds of the way along, the thread caught, like a stitch on a splinter. It caught, and pulled, and settled.

The ache came up into her knuckles, faint, and stayed.

* * *

The Hall of Instruments was long and high and full of brass.

Sightvanes as long as oars hung on the walls. Glass cases held chains that had belonged to the Ninefold, their links worn thin as wire. The floor was laid in tiles each painted with a fragment of map, so that you crossed a jumbled Reach of coastlines and rivers to reach the long table under the windows where Brask sat with the sheets.

She had a lens screwed into her one good eye like a jeweller, and she was bent so low over the sheet in front of her that her cropped white head nearly touched it. The Long Lie leaned against the table beside her. Her grey hand lay curled on the corner of the sheet, holding it flat.

Hallam stood a little behind her with his hands folded. The other Walkers sat on benches along the wall in various states of collapse; the boy with the new chain had fallen asleep sitting up.

They put the three sheets down in front of her side by side: Teo’s, Cass’s, Ilse’s.

Brask looked at Teo’s first. She went down it slowly with the lens, station by station, and made small marks with a pencil on a slip of paper beside it. She stopped at the box Teo had drawn round the fourteen links and read his note. Her mouth moved slightly.

“Ground leaning,” she said. “Cass. Who’s Cass?”

“Me,” said Cass.

Brask grunted. She went on. At the end she sat back and took the lens out and rubbed her eye with the back of her good hand.

“Pass,” she said. “Narrowly. Your lines wander, Lisk. Your numbers don’t. Next time make them agree.”

Teo made a sound like a kettle coming off the boil, and sat down very suddenly on the end of the bench.

Cass’s sheet was ugly; there was no getting away from it. The stations were stabbed into the paper rather than drawn, and in one place she had run out of ink and finished in pencil. But Brask went down it with the lens and her pencil and at the end she grunted again, a different grunt.

“True,” she said. “Hideous. True. Pass.”

Cass grinned all over her face and didn’t say anything at all.

Then Brask drew Ilse’s sheet in front of her and screwed the lens back in.

She went down it as she had the others, station by station. Ilse stood with her hands behind her back and watched the cropped white head move along the line. Out from the headland. The first creek. The long stretch past the mere, the sounder’s hill. The cairn, and the turn. Back. The lens moved steadily, the pencil made its small marks. Then it reached the stream, and stopped.

It stayed stopped.

Brask did not move. She did not make a mark. She sat bent over the sheet with the lens to her eye and her grey hand flat beside it, and the Hall of Instruments was very quiet. The boy on the bench snored once. A clock on the wall ticked. Outside the long windows the light was going amber over the heath. Brask reached out with her good hand, without looking, and drew Teo’s sheet across beside Ilse’s, and laid them edge to edge, so that the two streams sat side by side. One had its banks fourteen links apart from themselves, boxed and annotated. The other was whole and clean and drawn in a single unbroken line.

She looked from one to the other. She looked for a long time.

Ilse kept her hands behind her back. She kept her face still. In the back room of her head she was running, as she had run all her life, through every answer to every question that might come next. None came. Hallam, behind Brask, glanced at Ilse over his half-moon spectacles and smiled at her, encouragingly, as if to say *she’s always like this*.

At last Brask took the lens out of her eye. She did not look up.

“True enough,” she said.

She made a mark on the slip, and pushed the sheet across to the clerk, and drew the next sheet toward her. That was all.

* * *

The last Walkers came in with the dusk.

Two more passed: a quiet girl from the Salt Steps and a man of about thirty from the Ammonite Hills with a broken nose. Several failed. One had a survey so short it was clear he had turned back at the first creek and walked in circles. Another had a lovely sheet that, when Brask held it up to the window, simply wasn’t attested. There was no hold in it at all, and the boy who had drawn it looked at it as though it had died in his hands.

At half past six the clerk lit the lamps in the Hall, and out at the Gate someone tended the great lantern on the keystone, though it had never gone out. The sheet that came in after that would be late, and a late sheet was a dead sheet.

Eleven passed. Six failed.

Two had not come back. The clerk read the names off the ledger in a flat voice. Oskar Fell. Yara Penhallow.

Brask stood up.

She did it without hurry, using the table and the Long Lie, and she took her coat from the back of the chair and put it on, one-handed. Hallam made a small movement toward her.

“Odile. The wardens can go out at first light. It’s full dark in an hour. You’re—” He stopped, and changed what he had been going to say. “You’ve done this day already.”

“I’ve done this day nineteen times,” said Brask. “It doesn’t get shorter.” She took a lantern from the clerk’s table. “Fell walked straight. He’ll have gone straight on past the cairn, the fool, he’ll be in the Rushes by now. Penhallow—” She stopped. Her face did nothing at all. “Penhallow, I’ll look.”

She went out. Ilse went to the long windows and watched her go.

The heath was blue-black under a sky going green at the edges. Brask came out of the Gate below with the lantern in her good hand and the Long Lie in the crook of her stilled arm. She went down the steps to the heath and out across it, alone, the small yellow light swinging, getting smaller. She didn’t look back. After a while the light went over a rise and was gone, and there was nothing out there but the dark and the Margin breathing.

* * *

The steward found her in the Old Court an hour later.

He was a thin old man in a black gown, and he put the ring on her right hand himself. It was iron, plain and heavy and a little too big, with a single fine line inlaid across its face in brass: the meridian, Tessary to the Cape. He said, “Ilse Marrow, Member of the Meridian, Walk of twelve hundred and three,” in the voice of a man who had said it many hundreds of times, and wrote her name in a book, and went away.

She stood in the Old Court with the ring on her hand.

The quadrangle was lit by lamps on brackets, the honey-coloured stone gone gold in their light. The brass studs of the meridian line ran across the flags at her feet, catching the light, one every yard, from the far gate to the Lantern Gate and on into the dark. She looked down at the line in the stone and the line on the ring.

Member. They couldn’t touch her now. Not Pollard, not the Concordance, not the examiner with her lenses. The Court could seal every chest in the Fallowmarch and it wouldn’t matter. She was the College’s. The relief came up through her slowly, from the feet, warm, like sitting down at last. She put her other hand over the ring and held it.

“Congratulations, Miss Marrow.”

He had come across the court from the direction of the Library. He wore the student’s black coat and white stock, and his hair was combed, and on his right hand was the same iron ring with the same brass line. He stopped a careful two yards from her, the way he had stopped at the edge of his lantern-light at the hedge.

“Mr. Reyne,” said Ilse.

“That was a very fine survey. I saw it on the table.” He said it plainly, as a fact. “Brask does not say *true enough* to many people.”

“She said it to eleven.”

“Yes.” He inclined his head. “I wanted to tell you something myself, rather than have you hear it from the Provost’s office. I thought it was only fair.” His grey-blue eyes were steady on her face. “This afternoon I wrote to the parish of Hobbet. I have asked that the Court’s inquiry into the Tull amendment be withdrawn from Tessary and forwarded instead to the Board of Errata here, as the proper authority over a Member of the College. The parish clerk will need to be told where to send the sheet.”

Ilse said nothing.

“The Board sits quarterly,” Kasimir said. “It is very thorough. I understand it is also very slow.” He paused. “Good night, Miss Marrow. And congratulations. I mean it.”

He went away across the court, between the lamps, over the brass line without stepping on it.

Ilse stood where she was. She looked down at the ring on her hand, iron and brass, too big for her finger. She had spent the Long Field on it. She had walked out into the Margin and come back and lied on the best sheet she had ever drawn. And it had bought her exactly what she had paid for, no more: not a pardon, not a door out, only a different courtroom and a longer road to it. A slower trial. She turned the ring once on her finger, and then again, and found that it was already warm.
