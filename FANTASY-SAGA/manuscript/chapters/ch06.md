# Chapter 6
## Benchmarking

The Mess had stars on the ceiling and nobody looked at them.

Some long-dead artist had laid out the whole night sky across its blue vault in gold leaf, every constellation labelled, and two hundred students ate under it twice a day without raising their eyes. On the second morning after the Walk, the business was parties.

“It’s like a cattle fair,” said Teo unhappily. “My uncle sells heifers at Ambry Market and this is exactly how they look at them.”

He was right. The Patent first-years had spent their week sorting themselves, and sat in fours along the upper tables, heads together. Now and again one of them looked down the hall to where the eleven Walkers sat, and looked away.

“We need a fourth,” said Cass. She had her feet on the bench opposite and was eating porridge out of the bowl with her fingers, which was causing comment. “Proctor says. Party of four or they split you up and give you out like spare boots.”

“Then we’ll be given out,” said Ilse.

“I don’t want to be given out,” said Teo. “I want to be in this one. I’ve got used to this one.” He went pink. “If that’s—sorry. If that’s all right.”

In three days he had trodden on her, fed her, cried in front of her and walked across a widening creek on her chain, and Ilse found she didn’t want to be given out either. She didn’t say so. She looked at her porridge.

A tray came down on the table beside her with a crash that made the bowls jump.

“Right,” said the girl who had put it there, and sat. She was smaller even than Ilse, freckled all over, with fair hair scraped back so tight it looked painful. “Henrike Solberg. Hen. Salt Steps. I’m Patent but I’m scholarship, so they don’t count me, so I’ve been sitting up there for a week being not-counted, and frankly, the tide’s out on that one.” She pointed at Ilse with a spoon. “I watched you come in off the Walk. You looked like you knew what you were doing. And the others—” She jerked her head toward the upper tables. “The others looked like they wanted to marry each other. I’d rather be with you.”

“Hey-ho,” said Cass, delighted.

“I’m very good at Mensuration,” Hen added. “Ask anyone. Don’t ask anyone, they’ll only be rude. But I am.”

Then she laughed, a tremendous laugh much bigger than she was, the laugh of a salt-pan foreman’s daughter who had to be heard across a mile of flats in a gale. Heads turned up and down the Mess. Teo stared at her as though she had pulled a sounder out of her pocket.

“Teo,” he said. “Anselm. I’m very good at Mensuration too.”

“I know. You did the mean in your head at the Gate when the clerk got the Walk times wrong. I heard you.” Hen beamed at him. He went the colour of a brick.

The senior proctor, a tall fifth-year called Arnaud, read their rooms off a board without looking up. “Marrow, Anselm, Tenter, Solberg. Second Range, Wren Stair, top landing. Morning Survey stretch, the Wren Stair and the Long Gallery, three chains forty-one links. First Bell is an hour before dawn. If you lose your stair you pay the fine, and you will lose your stair.”

From the next table, where four Patent boys were sitting, there was a snort.

“The *Wren Stair*,” said one of them. “Oh, bad luck.”

“Why bad luck?” said Teo.

“Because it hops, link. Why do you think they call it that? It turned clean round twice last year.”

Ilse said nothing. She was thinking of three lines and a spur in the corner of a sheet in a sealed parish chest five hundred and sixty miles away. It was only a name.

The rooms were four small bedrooms off a sitting room at the top of a tall grey building that leaned, with a hearth, four mismatched chairs, a window over the heath and a wardrobe of black oak that had clearly been there since the Lost Wing. That night Ilse lay and listened to the building. It creaked. Once, very faintly, it groaned, like a ship’s timbers when the tide turns under them.

In the morning the wardrobe was a foot from the wall.

It stood with a clean rectangle of unfaded boards behind it, looking innocent. Teo stood in his nightshirt and considered it.

“Well,” he said. “That’s not ideal.”

* * *

The First Bell rang in the dark like someone dropping a cauldron down a well.

They had been up an hour already, because Hen insisted, with the instruments laid out by lamplight beside the charter of record for their stretch. It showed the stair coming down to the Gallery’s east door in a tight right-hand spiral of forty-one steps, and the Long Gallery running a chain and sixty links west to the double doors at the end.

“So,” said Hen, bright-eyed. “We measure it as it is. Then we draw it as it should be, and we sign, and if our numbers are good enough the building agrees with us and goes back.”

Ilse looked at the sheet. She looked at it for longer than she meant to.

“That’s—” she said.

“That’s what you lot call *holding*,” said Cass, pulling on her boots.

In Wick Fallow they had another word for it. Ilse kept it to herself.

They went down the stair with a lantern, counting, and at the bottom they stopped.

“It turns left,” said Teo.

It did, as neat and plain as if it always had. The stones of the newel were worn smooth on the wrong side.

“Oh, *no*,” said Teo. “It’s gone round in the night. My stomach knew. My stomach’s been saying so all the way down and I didn’t listen.”

“It’s still forty-one steps,” said Hen, who had counted. “That’s something.”

“It’s forty-one steps in the wrong *direction*—”

“Gallery,” said Ilse. “Gallery first. Stair after. The stair’s a nightmare; the gallery’s just long.”

The Long Gallery had windows on one side over the black heath and portraits on the other: dead Masters holding dividers, all looking faintly disappointed. Teo walked out the leader end while Ilse held the follower, and Hen held the lantern and the watch, and Cass trailed one hand along the wall.

“A chain,” called Teo from the dark. “And sixty. And—sixty-three. A chain and sixty-three links.”

“It should be sixty.”

“I know it should be sixty, but it’s sixty-three. It’s grown two feet in the night. Like bread.”

They did it again and got the same. Then the four of them stood in the middle of the Long Gallery at a quarter to six in the morning and argued.

Teo wanted to chain everything twice more and take the mean. Hen wanted to triangulate off the windows. Ilse wanted to start drafting before the building changed its mind again. Cass leaned against a dead Master and said nothing for a while. Then she said, “Ground’s leaning.”

“The ground’s *always* leaning,” said Ilse.

“Yeah. And right now it’s leaning west, toward the end doors. Whole thing’s sliding off toward the heath like a drawer coming out, and the stair’s got twisted coming with it.” Cass pushed off the wall. “Fix the gallery, the stair’ll come round by itself.”

They all looked at her.

“Twelve minutes to the Dawn Bell,” said Hen, looking at her watch, “and the Antechamber’s eight minutes away if we run, which we will.”

So Ilse drafted, standing, on the board against the wall, with Teo reading her the bearings and Hen the minutes. It was the work she knew best in the world: to measure a thing that was wrong and draw it the way it ought to be, so well that the world gave in. She drew from the numbers and from the lean under her boots, the gallery at a chain and sixty, the stair turning right. Teo checked her sums in his head and said “yes” and “yes,” and Hen said “four minutes,” and then they ran: along the causeway in the grey beginning of the light, through the Lantern Gate, across the Old Court over the brass meridian studs, and down a flight of stone steps into the cape itself.

The Register Antechamber was a long, low vault cut into the rock, lit by brass lamps on chains and smelling of old paper and ink. Folios bound in faded calf, thick as paving stones, ran back into the shadows. At a high desk by the door sat a thin, anxious man with ink stains up both cuffs to the wrist. “Merrin Coyle,” Hen breathed. “Registrar-Adjunct.” He had the Second Range folio open in front of him.

“Wren Stair,” he said, before they could speak. “Last in. You’ve—” he glanced at the clock above the door “—three minutes. Links sign with initials until Benchmarking. Here, here and here.” He turned the great book round and pointed with an inky finger. “Here, not in the Ranges. Master Hallam likes every sheet signed here. He’s very particular.”

“Why?” said Ilse.

The clerk blinked at her. “It’s the Register,” he said, as if that answered everything, and turned the book a little further toward her.

They signed, four sets of initials one under another. As Ilse set her intent on hers, she felt the faint familiar pull in her knuckles, and then, very far away, at the other end of the long causeway, something settle. It was like a door closing in another house.

The Dawn Bell rang.

Coyle closed the folio. “On time,” he said, and for the first time the beginnings of a smile crossed his anxious face. “Well. Nearly.”

They walked back in the first real light, with the sun coming up red behind the Held Side. Not everyone had been so lucky. A party of Lisk boys from the Third Range stood in a miserable huddle at the foot of a stair that went up eight feet and stopped at a blank wall. Further along, a door that should have opened onto a corridor stood wide on nothing but bright yellow gorse and a grazing wayhorn, which lifted its great branched head and looked at them in mild surprise.

The Wren Stair turned to the right. The Long Gallery was a chain and sixty links; Hen chained it again to be sure. Teo sat down on the bottom step and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

* * *

The Ossel Fair was held at the Verity Cross town gate on market days, and Hen took them, because nobody’s first week was complete without it.

The Tenting wagons stood in a line along the town wall, tall-wheeled, painted from axle to roof-ridge with route-diagrams in red and ochre and white that wound and branched and doubled back. Some had been repainted in a different direction, the old route showing faint beneath the new. The stalls sold glasswrack, coiled and ringing faintly in the wind like a wet finger round a glass, and wayhorn velvet, and pale curved plates of sounder-ivory that Teo stood before a long time without speaking.

And there were the ossels.

The sellers held them up to passing students and described them, loudly and confidently, and as they described them, they *changed*. “A fox!” cried a woman with a pipe, holding up a squirming handful. “A little red fox with a white tip to his tail!” And it was a little red fox, all at once, with a white tip to its tail. “A rabbit!” shouted a boy at the next stall, and his ossel went long-eared and brown in his hands. A Patent girl was bargaining for a marten while her friend said “No, a stoat, a white stoat,” and the poor thing blurred between the two like a reflection in disturbed water.

“That’s called flicker,” said Cass, watching it with a closed face. “Two of you saying two things. It hurts them.”

“Does it?” said Ilse.

“Course it does. How’d you like it? Ossels got no hold to speak of, so they go to whatever’s said about them loudest. They’ve their own shape, left alone. Nobody leaves them alone. You lot—” she said it without heat, as a fact “—you lot describe things into whatever you want, and then you call it buying.”

Ilse was looking back over her shoulder at the flickering marten-stoat when she stepped on something.

It was small and soft and it squeaked. She leapt back. “Sorry—*sorry*—”

At her feet, on the cobbles, was a creature.

That was all she could call it. It was the size of a teapot, grey-brown and indeterminate, mostly ears, like a hare drawn by someone who had heard of hares but never seen one. The rest was a vague furred shape that hadn’t decided on legs. And two eyes, enormous, the clear pale amber of honey held to the light, looking up at her.

“Oh,” said Ilse. “What *are* you?”

The eyes looked at her.

“It’s a—” She found herself crouching. She found herself, absurdly, answering her own question, out loud, in the voice she used for telling a hedge where it lived. “It’s a—a cat? A small grey cat. With ridiculous ears.”

And it was.

It happened without fuss, the way a drop of ink takes in water. The blur drew itself together: four white-tipped paws, a thin tail, a narrow face and a pink nose, and on top of its head two ears far too large for it, which turned toward her like a pair of sails. A small grey cat sat on the cobbles. Its eyes were still that clear amber.

It blinked at her, slowly.

“Oh,” said Hen. “Oh, *look* at it.”

“That’s one of Mab’s,” said Cass. “Got out of the basket. Hey, Mab!”

A Tenting girl of about twelve came running from a wagon and stopped dead. She looked at Ilse, and at the cat, and her face went from dismay to calculation.

“A penny,” she said.

“I don’t want—”

“It wants you.” The girl pointed. The cat had stood up, and walked to Ilse’s boot, and sat down again on it, firmly, the way a cat sits on a letter you are trying to write. “See? A penny. Can’t sell it to no one else now. It’s been said.”

Ilse paid a penny. She hadn’t meant to.

It followed her two steps behind her boots all the way back to the College, its ears swivelling at every sound. At the foot of the Wren Stair, Teo, who had been looking at it with growing fondness, crouched and held out a finger and said warmly, “Look at him. He’s just like a little ferret, isn’t he? A grey ferret.”

The cat flickered.

It went long and low and sinuous, and then it was a cat again, and then a ferret, and then neither, a smear of grey between the two. It made a thin small sound, a sound of real distress, and pressed itself flat against Ilse’s boot.

“Teo,” said Cass, sharply.

“I’m sorry! It’s a cat. You’re a cat, little one, a cat.” Ilse said it at the same time, firmly, “A small grey cat,” and Hen said “Cat!” as though calling a dog. Between them it settled, shuddering, into the cat again, and looked up at Teo reproachfully.

“Cat,” said Teo, humbly. “Agreed. Cat. I’ll never say the other word again.”

They tried to name it that evening by the hearth, with the wardrobe watching. Hen suggested Pebble. Teo suggested Pie. Cass suggested a Tenting word she wouldn’t translate. The cat washed one enormous ear and ignored them all. Then Ilse, getting up to put the kettle on, stepped too near its tail, and said “Sorry,” without thinking, and the cat looked up at once, and trotted over, and wound itself round her ankles.

“Oh, *no*,” said Hen, and began to laugh. “Oh, that’s it. That’s its name. That’s all it answers to.”

Ilse tried it three more times, to be sure. Pebble, nothing. Cat, nothing. *Sorry*, and the ears swung round at once.

So it was Sorry.

* * *

Benchmarking was on the thirtieth, in the Old Court, under a high white sky.

The Old Wall was the north wall of the court, the oldest stone in the College: honey stone two hundred feet long, and every inch of its lower half cut with witness-marks in rows under brass benchmark arrows. There were thousands of them. Crosses, circles, single bars; knots, keys, little flowers. Some were sharp and fresh and some so worn by rain they were faint as a signature on blotting paper, older and older as the rows went down toward the ground, each with a name and a year. In the lowest row, near the corner, worn nearly away, a little mark like a bird in flight had *4 H.* beside it.

Master Halloway presided, a thin, twitchy man with three watches on three chains across his waistcoat, who stammered so badly that Ilse winced for him.

“W-welcome. To. Um. To Benchmarking. Which is. Ah. An old—a very old—” He gave up and gestured at the wall, helplessly. Then a link at the front asked how deep to cut, and his whole face cleared like a sky.

“An eighth of an inch. Exactly. At sixty degrees to the face. Shallower and the weather will have it off you in two hundred years. Deeper, and it spalls in the first frost. The mallet is eleven ounces. Strike from the wrist.” He beamed. Then he lost the thread again and pointed to the clerk with the great Register of Marks. “Y-your names. There. And then. Um. Cut.”

They went up one by one, showed the clerk their mark, and cut it under the newest arrow while Halloway watched the angle. Some of the Patent links had been planning theirs for years: family devices, crowns. Hen cut a gull’s wing over a line, very neat, very fast. Teo cut a looped T like a pothook, for Teodor and for the hook his mother hung the eel-pot on, and apologized to the stone for the loop. Cass looked at the wall for a long while and then cut a quick Tenting zigzag, like a road going down a hill, and stood back from it, and said “Hey-ho,” quietly, to the stone.

Then it was Ilse.

She stood with the chisel in her hand. The clerk waited with his pen.

She had thought about it all week. She could choose anything: a clean new mark, a circle and a bar, with no history at all. Nobody would ever look at it twice. That was the sensible thing.

She thought of the wren’s foot.

She had drawn it thirty-one times in three years. She had drawn it on Agnes Tull’s amendment, and Kasimir Reyne had turned that sheet to the light in the Wick vestry and sealed it in a chest. And every time, underneath it, in the blind, she had drawn Quince’s, line for line, so the weight would go to him and not to her. The wren’s foot had never carried anything. A dead man had been carrying it for three years in the Hobbet churchyard, and she had thought it clever.

And yet it was the only mark that had ever felt like hers. She had made it at sixteen, watching a wren hop along the Gethins’ hedge: a small brown bird that was nobody’s and went where it liked.

“Miss?” said the clerk.

“A wren’s foot,” said Ilse. She drew it for him on the margin of the Register. Three lines and a spur. “Like that.”

She set the chisel at sixty degrees and cut it, an eighth of an inch deep, from the wrist. The stone was soft and sweet under the steel, and the dust ran down in a fine honey-coloured stream. Halloway, watching, said, “G-good. *Good* angle,” and moved on.

When she stepped back and looked up, Kasimir Reyne was standing three yards along the wall.

His own mark was fresh in the stone beside him: a capital R with a level line struck through it, precise as a proof. He was looking at her wren’s foot. He looked at it a moment longer than a man looks at a thing that means nothing to him, and his lips moved once, as if he were counting strokes. Then he looked at her, and his face did nothing at all, and he turned away to his party.

Ilse found that she didn’t care. She was surprised by how little.

“Oh, that’s lovely.”

Master Hallam had come along the line behind them, gloved hands folded behind his back, and stood looking at the wren’s foot with his head on one side.

“A clean mark,” he said. “Not fussy. Half the Patent links want to cut their whole family tree.” He smiled at her over his spectacles. “You have a draftswoman’s hand, my dear. I noticed it on your Walk sheet. A beautiful line. We don’t see enough of them.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“Keep it,” he said, kindly, as if it were a thing that could be lost, and went on along the wall.

The last part of Benchmarking was the stone.

Each link drew a paving stone of the Old Court by lot, to survey, chart and sign with their new mark, and keep in place for a year through frost and feet and the slow lean of the cape. Ilse’s was a grey sandstone flag near the southeast corner, two feet by three, cracked at one corner, worn hollow in the middle by centuries of boots.

She measured it on her knees with Quince’s folding rule: four sides, two diagonals, and its offset from the nearest brass stud of the meridian, eleven links and four-tenths. She measured the hollow in the middle, which she didn’t have to do. All around her the other links knelt over their own stones like people at prayer. It was the smallest charter she had ever made, and the plainest. It held a single stone where it already was.

Then she signed it.

She set the wren’s foot in the lower corner, three lines and a spur, in plain black ink. She set her intent on it, and leaned.

And there was nothing under it.

She waited, out of habit, for the second thread: the cool sideways slide of the burden running off down the ghosted line toward a churchyard in Hobbet. It didn’t come. There was only her mark and the stone and a single thread between them, short and clean, and the stone took it as a dry field takes rain.

The ache came into her knuckles, small and exact. It was hers.

She knelt there with the sheet on her knee, and the thing she would remember was how light it was. She had always thought that carrying your own weight would be heavy. It wasn’t. It was a flagstone, two feet by three, a little cracked at one corner. She could have carried it for a year with one hand.

Across the court, on the warm stones by the Mess door, a small grey cat with ridiculous ears sat and washed one paw and watched her with eyes the colour of honey.
