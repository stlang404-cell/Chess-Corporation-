# Chapter 6
## Benchmarking

The Mess had stars on the ceiling and nobody looked at them.

It was a long, high hall on the north side of the Old Court, with tables running its length and a painted ceiling of deep blue on which some long-dead artist had laid out the whole night sky in gold leaf, every constellation labelled in a fine Ninefold hand. Two hundred students ate under it every morning and evening without once raising their eyes. They were busy. On the second morning after the Walk, the business was parties.

“It’s like a cattle fair,” said Teo, in a low, unhappy voice. “It’s exactly like a cattle fair. My uncle sells heifers at Ambry Market and this is exactly how they look at them.”

He was right. The Patent first-years had been at the College a week already. They had spent it, Ilse saw, sorting themselves. All along the upper tables they sat in fours, already paired off and squared away, their heads together, and now and again one of them would look down the hall to the lower end where the eleven Walkers sat, and look away.

“We need a fourth,” said Cass. She had her feet on the bench opposite and was eating porridge out of the bowl with her fingers, which was causing comment. “Proctor says. Party of four or they split you up and give you out like spare boots.”

“Then we’ll be given out,” said Ilse.

“I don’t want to be given out,” said Teo. “I want to be in this one. I’ve got used to this one.” He went pink. “If that’s—sorry. If that’s all right.”

Ilse looked at him. She had known him for three days. In three days he had trodden on her, fed her and cried in front of her, and walked across a widening creek on her chain, and she found that she didn’t want to be given out either. She didn’t say so. She looked at her porridge.

A tray came down on the table beside her with a crash that made the bowls jump.

“Right,” said the girl who had put it there. She sat down beside Ilse without being asked. She was small, smaller even than Ilse, and freckled all over, with fair hair scraped back so tight it looked painful and a round, cheerful, sharp-eyed face. “Henrike Solberg. Hen. Salt Steps. I’m Patent but I’m scholarship, so they don’t count me, so I’ve been sitting up there for a week being not-counted, and frankly, the tide’s out on that one.” She pointed at Ilse with a spoon. “I watched you come in off the Walk. You looked like you knew what you were doing. And the others—” She jerked her head toward the upper tables. “The others looked like they wanted to marry each other. I’d rather be with you.”

There was a short silence.

“Hey-ho,” said Cass, delighted.

“I’m very good at Mensuration,” Hen added. “Ask anyone. Don’t ask anyone, they’ll only be rude. But I am.”

Then she laughed. It was a tremendous laugh, much bigger than she was, the laugh of someone whose father ran a salt-pan crew and had to be heard across a mile of flats in a gale, and heads turned up and down the Mess. Teo stared at her as though she had just pulled a sounder out of her pocket.

“Teo,” he said. “Anselm. I’m very good at Mensuration too.”

“I know. You did the mean in your head at the Gate when the clerk got the Walk times wrong. I heard you.” Hen beamed at him. He went the colour of a brick.

The senior proctor, a tall fifth-year called Arnaud with a list on a board, gave them their rooms after breakfast. She read it off without looking up. “Marrow, Anselm, Tenter, Solberg. Second Range, Wren Stair, top landing. Morning Survey stretch, the Wren Stair and the Long Gallery, three chains forty-one links. First Bell is an hour before dawn. If you lose your stair you pay the fine, and you will lose your stair.”

From the next table, where four Patent boys were sitting, there was a snort.

“The *Wren Stair*,” said one of them. “Oh, bad luck.”

“Why bad luck?” said Teo.

“Because it hops, link.” The boy grinned. “Why do you think they call it that? Every party that’s ever had it has been fined by Fogmonth. It turned clean round twice last year. They found the Chainmen’s Club president’s boots on the roof.”

Ilse said nothing. She was thinking of three lines and a spur, inked small in the corner of a sheet in a sealed parish chest five hundred and sixty miles away. It was only a name. A small brown bird that lived in hedges was nothing to be afraid of.

The rooms were at the top of a narrow stair in a tall grey building near the middle of the Second Range, which leaned. There were four small bedrooms, like boxes, off a shared sitting room with a window that looked out over the heath, a hearth, a table, four mismatched chairs, and a wardrobe of black oak that had clearly been there since the Lost Wing. That night Ilse lay in her narrow bed and listened to the building. It creaked. It ticked. Once, very faintly, it groaned, the way a ship’s timbers groan when the tide turns under it.

In the morning the wardrobe was a foot from the wall.

Nobody had touched it. It stood in the middle of the sitting-room floor with a clean rectangle of unfaded boards behind it, looking innocent. Teo stood in his nightshirt and looked at it for a long time.

“Well,” he said. “That’s not ideal.”

* * *

The First Bell rang in the dark like someone dropping a cauldron down a well.

They had been up for an hour already, because Hen had insisted. They had the chain and the levelglass and the sightvane laid out on the sitting-room table in the lamplight, and the folio sheet that the proctor had given them: the charter of record for the Wren Stair and the Long Gallery, drawn by last year’s party and attested by every party since. It showed the stair going up from the Gallery’s east door in a tight right-hand spiral of forty-one steps. It showed the Long Gallery running a chain and sixty links west from the stair-foot to the double doors at the end. It was a plain, competent drawing in a dozen different hands.

“So,” said Hen, bright-eyed. “We measure it as it is. Then we draw it as it should be, and we sign, and if our numbers are good enough the building agrees with us and goes back.”

Ilse looked at the sheet. She looked at it for longer than she meant to.

“That’s—” she said.

“That’s what you lot call *holding*,” said Cass, pulling on her boots.

In Wick Fallow they had another word for it. Ilse kept it to herself.

They went down the stair with a lantern, counting, and at the bottom they stopped.

“It turns left,” said Teo.

It did. The stair that the sheet said spiralled to the right went down to the left, as neat and plain as if it had always done so. The stones of the newel were worn smooth on the wrong side.

“Oh, *no*,” said Teo. “Oh, no, no. It’s gone round. It’s gone round in the night. We’ve been walking down it the wrong way. My stomach knew. My stomach’s been saying so all the way down and I didn’t listen.”

“It’s still forty-one steps,” said Hen, who had counted. “That’s something.”

“It’s forty-one steps in the wrong *direction*—”

“Gallery,” said Ilse. “Gallery first. Stair after. The stair’s a nightmare; the gallery’s just long.”

The Long Gallery was a stone corridor with windows on one side looking out over the black heath and portraits on the other: dead Masters in dark coats, holding dividers, all looking faintly disappointed. Teo took the leader end and walked it out while Ilse held the follower at the stair-foot, and Hen held the lantern and the watch, and Cass walked along beside the chain with one hand trailing on the wall.

“A chain,” called Teo from the dark. “And sixty. And—sixty-three. A chain and sixty-three links.”

“It should be sixty.”

“I know it should be sixty, I can read, but it’s sixty-three. It’s grown. It’s grown two feet in the night. Like bread.”

“Again,” said Ilse.

They did it again. A chain and sixty-three links and a half. Hen wrote it down. Then the four of them stood in the middle of the Long Gallery at a quarter to six in the morning, while the portraits looked disappointed at them, and argued.

Teo wanted to chain everything twice more and take the mean. Hen wanted to triangulate off the windows. Ilse wanted to stop measuring and start drafting, because she could already see how the sheet had to go and she didn’t trust the building to wait. Cass leaned against a dead Master and said nothing for a while. Then she said, “Ground’s leaning.”

“The ground’s *always* leaning,” said Ilse.

“Yeah. And right now it’s leaning west, down the gallery, toward the end doors. That’s why it’s longer. Whole thing’s sliding off toward the heath like a drawer coming out. And the stair’s turned because the stair’s the top of the drawer and it’s got twisted coming with it.” Cass pushed off the wall. “You fix the gallery first, the stair’ll come round by itself. Drawer goes back, top goes back.”

They all looked at her.

“Twelve minutes to the Dawn Bell,” said Hen, looking at her watch, “and the Antechamber’s eight minutes away if we run, which we will.”

So Ilse drafted the Long Gallery at a chain and sixty links. She drew it standing up, on the board against the wall, by lantern-light, with Teo reading her the bearings and Hen reading her the minutes and Cass watching the end doors. It was exactly the work she knew best in the world: to measure a thing that was wrong, and to draw it the way it ought to be, so well that the world gave in. She did not look at the sheet of record. She drew from the numbers and from the feel of the floor under her boots, the lean that she could feel now, a little, sliding west like a drawer. She drew the stair turning right, forty-one steps. Teo checked her sums in his head and said “yes” and “yes” and “yes,” and Hen said “four minutes,” and then they ran.

They ran down the Long Gallery and along the causeway in the grey beginning of the light, with the chain clanking in its bag and the folio flapping under Ilse’s arm. They passed through the Lantern Gate under the burning lantern and across the Old Court, over the brass meridian studs without stepping on them, and down a flight of stone steps into the cape itself.

The Register Antechamber was a long, low, vaulted room cut into the rock, lit by brass lamps on chains, and it smelled of old paper and lamp-oil and ink. Shelves of folios ran into the shadows at the back, row on row, bound in faded calf, thick as paving stones. At a high desk by the door sat a thin, anxious man of about thirty-five with ink stains up both cuffs of his shirt to the wrist. He had the folio for the Second Range open in front of him.

“Wren Stair,” he said, before they could speak. “Last in. You’ve—” he glanced at the clock above the door “—three minutes. Links sign with initials until Benchmarking. Here, here and here.” He turned the great book round and pointed with an inky finger. “Here, not in the Ranges. Master Hallam likes every sheet signed here. He’s very particular.”

“Why?” said Ilse.

The clerk blinked at her. “It’s the Register,” he said, as if that answered everything, and turned the book a little further toward her.

They signed. Hen first, then Teo, then Cass, then Ilse, four sets of initials one under another on the fresh sheet she had drawn standing in the gallery. As she set her intent on it, Ilse felt the faint familiar pull in her knuckles, and then, very far away, at the other end of the long causeway, something settle. It was like a door closing in another house.

The Dawn Bell rang.

Coyle closed the folio. “On time,” he said, and for the first time something like a smile crossed his anxious face. “Well. Nearly.”

They walked back along the causeway in the first real light, not running now, with the sun coming up red behind the Held Side and the heath going from black to brown to gold. When they reached the Wren Stair it turned to the right. The Long Gallery was a chain and sixty links, end to end; Hen chained it again to be sure. Teo sat down on the bottom step and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

Not everyone had been so lucky. On the way back they passed a party of Lisk boys from the Third Range standing in a miserable huddle at the foot of a stair that went up perhaps eight feet and stopped, ending in a blank wall. Further along, a door that should have opened onto a corridor of the Second Range stood wide on nothing at all but gorse, bright yellow, and a wayhorn grazing, which lifted its great branched head and looked at them in mild surprise.

“Lost your stair, links!” said Cass to the Lisk boys, with deep satisfaction.

* * *

The Ossel Fair was held at the Verity Cross town gate on market days, and on the first market day after the Walk Hen took them to see it, because, she said, nobody’s first week was complete without it.

The Tenting wagons stood in a long line along the outside of the town wall. They were tall-wheeled and high-sided and painted from axle to roof-ridge with route-diagrams in red and ochre and white. The lines on them wound and branched and doubled back on themselves, and some of them, Ilse saw, had been painted over and repainted in a different direction, the old route showing faint beneath the new. The stalls between the wagons sold glasswrack, coiled and ringing faintly in the wind like a wet finger round the rim of a glass, and wayhorn velvet in soft brown rolls, and salt, and pale curved plates of sounder-ivory that Teo stood in front of for a long time without speaking.

And there were the ossels.

They were in wicker baskets, mostly, or on cords. The Tenting sellers held them up to passing students and described them, loudly and confidently, and as they described them, they *changed*. “A fox!” cried a woman with a pipe, holding up a squirming handful. “A little red fox with a white tip to his tail!” And it was a little red fox, all at once, with a white tip to its tail. “A rabbit!” shouted a boy at the next stall, and his ossel went long-eared and brown in his hands. A Patent girl from the upper tables of the Mess was bargaining for one described as a marten. Her friend was saying “No, a stoat, a white stoat,” at the same time, and the poor thing in the seller’s arms blurred and wavered between the two like a reflection in disturbed water.

“That’s called flicker,” said Cass, watching it with a closed face. “Two of you saying two things. It hurts them.”

“Does it?” said Ilse.

“Course it does. How’d you like it?” Cass jerked her chin at the stalls. “Ossels are Margin things. Got no hold to speak of, so they go to whatever’s said about them loudest. They’ve their own shape, left alone. Nobody leaves them alone. You lot—” she said it without heat, as a fact “—you lot describe things into whatever you want, and then you call it buying.”

Ilse was looking back over her shoulder at the flickering marten-stoat when she stepped on something.

It was small and soft and it squeaked. She leapt back. “Sorry—*sorry*—”

At her feet, on the cobbles, was a creature.

That was all she could call it. It was about the size of a loaf, and grey-brown, and indeterminate. It was mostly ears, two great soft ears like a hare’s, if a hare were drawn by someone who had heard of hares but never seen one. The rest of it was a vague furred shape that didn’t seem to have decided on legs. And two eyes, enormous, the clear pale amber of honey held to the light, looking up at her.

“Oh,” said Ilse. “What *are* you?”

The eyes looked at her.

“It’s a—” She found herself crouching. She found herself, absurdly, answering her own question, out loud, in the voice she used for telling a hedge where it lived. “It’s a—a cat? A small grey cat. With ridiculous ears.”

And it was.

It happened all at once, without fuss, the way a drop of ink takes in water. The grey-brown blur drew itself together. There were four paws, white-tipped. There was a tail, thin and long. There was a small grey cat sitting on the cobbles of the Verity Cross gate, with a narrow face and a pink nose and, on top of its head, two ears far too large for it, which turned toward her like a pair of sails. Its eyes were still that pale, clear amber.

It looked at her and blinked, slowly.

“Oh,” said Hen. “Oh, *look* at it.”

“That’s one of Mab’s,” said Cass. “Got out of the basket. Hey, Mab!”

A Tenting girl of about twelve came running from a wagon, braids flying, and stopped dead at the sight of the small grey cat on the cobbles. She looked at Ilse. She looked at the cat. Her face went, very quickly, from dismay to calculation.

“A penny,” she said.

“I don’t want—”

“It wants you.” The girl pointed. The cat had stood up, and walked to Ilse’s boot, and sat down again on it, firmly, the way a cat sits on a letter you are trying to write. “See? A penny. Can’t sell it to no one else now. It’s been said.”

Ilse paid a penny. She hadn’t meant to. It was the first thing she had bought since the coach.

It followed her round the rest of the fair. It followed her up the hill road to the College and through the Lantern Gate and along the causeway, two steps behind her boots, its ridiculous ears swivelling at every sound. At the foot of the Wren Stair, Teo, who had been looking at it with growing fondness for half an hour, crouched and held out a finger and said warmly, “Look at him. He’s just like a little ferret, isn’t he? A grey ferret.”

The cat flickered.

It went long and low and sinuous, and then it was a cat again, and then a ferret, and then neither, a smear of grey between the two. It made a thin small sound, a sound of real distress, and pressed itself flat against Ilse’s boot.

“Teo,” said Cass, sharply.

“I’m sorry! I’m so sorry! I didn’t—it’s a cat. It’s a cat. You’re a cat, little one, I’m sorry, a cat.” He said it hurriedly, contritely, and not very confidently. Ilse said it at the same time, firmly, “A small grey cat,” and Hen said “Cat!” as though calling a dog. Between the three of them it settled, shuddering, into the cat again, and stayed. It sat on Ilse’s boot and looked up at Teo reproachfully out of its amber eyes.

“Cat,” said Teo, humbly. “Agreed. Cat. I’ll never say the other word again.”

They tried to name it that evening, round the sitting-room hearth, with the wardrobe watching. Hen suggested Pebble. Teo suggested Pie. Cass suggested a Tenting word that she wouldn’t translate. The cat ignored all of them. It sat on the hearth-rug and washed one enormous ear and showed no sign of having heard. Then Ilse, getting up to put the kettle on, stepped too near its tail, and said “Sorry,” without thinking, and the cat looked up at once, and trotted over, and wound itself round her ankles.

“Oh, *no*,” said Hen, and began to laugh. “Oh, that’s it. That’s its name. That’s all it answers to.”

Ilse tried it three more times, to be sure. Pebble, nothing. Cat, nothing. *Sorry*, and the ears swung round like sails into the wind.

So it was Sorry.

* * *

Benchmarking was on the thirtieth, in the Old Court, under a high white sky.

The Old Wall was the north wall of the court, the oldest stone in the College, and it was covered in marks. Ilse had walked past it every day for a week and never stopped to look properly. Now she stood in front of it with the other links and looked, and could not stop.

It was a sheet of honey stone thirty feet high and two hundred long, and every inch of its lower half was cut with witness-marks. They were in rows, roughly, under brass benchmark arrows sunk into the stone at intervals, and there were thousands of them. There were simple marks, a cross, a circle, a single bar. There were elaborate ones, like knots, like keys, like little flowers. Some were sharp and fresh and some were so old the rain had worn them almost smooth, faint as the ghost of a signature on blotting paper. They went back and back, row on row, older and older as they went down toward the ground. Every one had a name beside it in small letters and a year. In the lowest row, near the corner, worn nearly away, a little mark like a bird in flight had *4 H.* beside it.

Master Halloway presided. He was a thin, twitchy man of forty in an untidy gown, with three watches on three chains across his waistcoat. When he spoke to them as a group he stammered so badly that Ilse winced for him.

“W-welcome. To. Um. To Benchmarking. Which is. Ah. An old—a very old—” He gave up and gestured at the wall, helplessly. Then a link at the front asked how deep to cut, and his whole face cleared like a sky.

“An eighth of an inch. Exactly. At sixty degrees to the face, both sides of the stroke. Shallower than an eighth and the weather will have it off you in two hundred years. Deeper, and you weaken the arris and it spalls in the first frost. The mallet is eleven ounces. Strike from the wrist, not the elbow. Fourteen strokes to the inch is about right for this stone.” He beamed. Then he lost the thread again, and stammered, and pointed to the clerk with the great Register of Marks open on a stand. “Y-your names. There. And then. Um. Cut.”

They went up one by one. Each link chose a mark, showed it to the clerk, who drew it in the Register beside their name, and then cut it into the stone under the newest arrow with chisel and mallet while Halloway watched the angle. Some of the Patent links had clearly been planning their marks for years. They were complicated, beautiful things, family devices, crowns. Hen cut a little wave over a line, very neat, very fast. Teo cut a ring with a dot in its centre, for a pie, he said, seen from above. Cass looked at the wall for a long while and then cut three short slanting strokes like a bird’s track in mud, and stood back from it, and said “Hey-ho,” quietly, to the stone.

Then it was Ilse.

She stood with the chisel in her hand. The clerk waited with his pen.

She had thought about it all week. She could choose anything. That was the point. She could make up a mark this morning, a clean one, a circle and a bar, and cut it and register it and it would be hers from now on, and it would have no history at all. Nobody would ever look at it twice. That was the sensible thing.

She thought of the wren’s foot.

She had drawn it forty times in three years. She had drawn it on Agnes Tull’s amendment, and Kasimir Reyne had turned that sheet to the light in the Wick vestry and looked at it, and sealed it in a chest. She had drawn it on every forgery she had ever made since Quince died, small and confident in the lower corner. And every time, underneath it, in the blind, she had drawn his. Line for line. A dead man’s mark under her own, so the weight would go to him and not to her. The wren’s foot had never carried anything. Quince had carried it all. He had been carrying it for three years in the Hobbet churchyard, and she had thought it clever.

And yet it was the only mark that had ever felt like hers. She had made it at sixteen, idly, watching a wren hop along the top of the Gethins’ hedge. Three lines and a spur. It was the shape of a small brown bird that lived in hedges and was nobody’s and went where it liked.

“Miss?” said the clerk.

“A wren’s foot,” said Ilse. She drew it for him on the margin of the Register. Three lines and a spur. “Like that.”

He copied it beside her name. She went to the wall and set the chisel at sixty degrees and cut it, an eighth of an inch deep, fourteen strokes to the inch, from the wrist. The stone was soft and sweet under the steel, and the dust ran down in a fine honey-coloured stream. Halloway, watching, said, “G-good. *Good* angle,” and moved on.

When she stepped back and looked up, Kasimir Reyne was standing three yards along the wall.

He had cut his own mark already. She saw it over his shoulder, fresh and sharp: a square, and a single exact diagonal through it, corner to corner, precise as a proof. He was holding his chisel loosely at his side. He was looking at her wren’s foot.

He looked at it a moment longer than a man looks at a thing that means nothing to him. Then he looked at her. His face did nothing at all. He turned away and handed his chisel to the next link and went to stand with his party.

Ilse found that she didn’t care. She was surprised by how little.

“Oh, that’s lovely.”

Master Hallam had come along the line behind them, as he seemed to come along every line, his gloved hands folded behind his back and his spectacles catching the white sky. He stopped beside her and looked at the wren’s foot with his head a little on one side, the way a man looks at a picture in a gallery.

“That’s a *very* clean cut,” he said. “And a clean mark. Not fussy. I can’t abide fussy marks, I never could; half the Patent links want to cut their whole family tree.” He smiled at her over his half-moons. “You have a draftswoman’s hand, my dear. I noticed it on your Walk sheet. A beautiful line. We don’t see enough of them.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“Keep it,” he said, kindly, as if it were a thing that could be lost, and went on along the wall.

The last part of Benchmarking was the stone.

Each link was given a single paving stone in the Old Court, chosen by lot from a bag of numbered pebbles, and made their first attestation as a witnessed Member on it: survey it, chart it and sign it. Then they must keep it in place for a year, so that it sat in its bed exactly as charted, not a hair out, through frost and feet and the slow lean of the cape. Ilse’s was a grey sandstone flag near the southeast corner of the court, about two feet by three, a little cracked at one corner, worn hollow in the middle by four hundred years of boots.

She measured it on her knees with Quince’s folding rule. She measured its four sides and its two diagonals and its offset from the nearest brass stud of the meridian line, which was eleven links and four-tenths. She measured the depth of the wear, the hollow in the middle, which she didn’t have to do and did anyway. She drew it on a small clean folio sheet, alone, in the middle of the court, with the other links all around her kneeling over their own stones like people at prayer. It took her half an hour. It was the smallest charter she had ever made, and the plainest. It held a single stone exactly where it already was.

Then she signed it.

She set the wren’s foot in the lower corner, three lines and a spur, in plain black ink. She set her intent on it, and leaned.

And there was nothing under it.

That was the thing. She waited, out of habit, for the second thread: the cool sideways slide of the burden running off down the ghosted line, away from her, toward a churchyard in Hobbet. It didn’t come. There was no blind. There was no Quince. There was only her mark and the stone and a single thread between them, short and clean, from her right hand down through the paper into the sandstone and its bed of sand, and the stone settled into it the way a cat settles into a lap.

The ache came into her knuckles, small and exact. It was hers.

She knelt there in the Old Court with the sheet on her knee and the pen in her hand, and the thing that surprised her, the thing she would remember, was how light it was. She had thought, always, that carrying your own weight would be heavy. It wasn’t. It was a flagstone. It was two feet by three, a little cracked at one corner. It was nothing at all. She could have carried it for a year with one hand.

Across the court, on the warm stones by the Mess door, a small grey cat with ridiculous ears sat and washed one paw and watched her with eyes the colour of honey.
