# Chapter 7
## Fieldwork

The Fieldwork shed had been built, as far as Ilse could tell, by someone who had heard of roofs but never seen one close up.

It stood at the far end of the First Range, where the causeway gave out and the heath began. Its back wall was the College; its front wall was mostly door. Under a roof of tarred planks that had shrunk apart in four places sat fifteen first-years on two benches, and under each leak someone had set a bucket: one copper, one a coal scuttle, and one a chamber pot that nobody wanted to look at closely. The rain came through in four thin strings and struck four different notes. Teo had been tapping his knee in time with them for ten minutes.

“It’s nearly a scale,” he whispered. “If the coal scuttle were a bit fuller it’d be a scale.”

“Don’t fill the coal scuttle.”

“I wasn’t going to. I was only saying.”

Odile Brask came in out of the weather without hurrying. Rain ran off her oilskin and off the brass shoe of the Long Lie, and she stood in the doorway with her back to the heath, looking at them with her one eye as if she had been sent a parcel of the wrong size. Her left hand hung at her side in its grey half-curl, bare to the wet. It was hard not to look at, like a stopped clock.

“Fieldwork,” Brask said. “Compulsory. You’ll hate it. Good. Come to the door. Don’t push, Grice, you’ll go through the wall and then we’ll have to survey it.”

They crowded the doorway. Outside, the Margin lay low and brown under the rain: heather, bracken gone the colour of rust, a gleam of standing water, and gorse in dark humped clumps like sheep lying down to wait out the weather. The nearest clump was a big dense bush with a few late yellow flowers on it, very bright against the grey.

“That gorse,” said Brask. “How far?”

“Two chains,” said Absalom Grice, who was the size of a small barn and seemed to regard this as an accomplishment.

“How might you be wrong?”

Grice looked at the bush, and then at Brask, like a man who suspects a trap but cannot see its shape. “I’m not, ma’am.”

“Then you haven’t measured anything. Next.”

A thin Patent boy said two and a half chains, or perhaps two, or three, and Brask told him that was being wrong in three directions at once and hoping one took. Piety Mercer said three chains with great firmness. Cass didn’t look at the bush at all, only at the ground in front of the door, and said, “Bit over three. Ground’s leaning off toward that pool,” which got a long look from Brask and no comment. Hen closed one eye, held up her thumb and said, “Three chains, eighteen links, give or take a chain, because I’ve only ever done this with boats.”

Then the eye came to Ilse and stopped.

“Marrow.”

She had been working at it since Grice’s two chains. She hadn’t wanted to, and then she couldn’t stop; it was like being handed a knot. She had stepped the distance out in doorframes and caught herself reading the bush closer because it was large.

“Three chains, ten links,” she said. “Give or take six.”

“How might you be wrong?”

“Short, most likely. There’s a fold about a chain and a half out, where the heather goes dark, and I can’t see the bottom of it, and measuring over a dip you can’t see into always reads short. The bush is big, which makes it look nearer; I corrected for that, but I might have corrected too much. The light’s flat, so there’s no shadow to show the ground, and flat light makes things look farther, so that might cancel some of it, or it might not.” She hesitated. “And it’s the Margin. It’s possible the bush isn’t where it was when I started talking.”

Somebody laughed, not unkindly. Brask didn’t.

“Anselm. Solberg. Take the chain to that bush.”

Teo and Hen went out into the rain calling the tallies to each other across the heather. Ilse watched them go down into the fold she had seen, waist-deep, and felt a small hard satisfaction she was careful not to show. Hen planted the arrow at the gorse and shouted back through cupped hands.

“Three chains, twenty-two links!”

“You said give or take six,” said Brask. “You were out by twelve. Wrong by twice what you said you might be. That’s the worst sort of wrong there is, Marrow, because it’s the sort that thinks it knows.” The class went back to looking at the rain. “The rest of you were wronger, but at least Grice is honest about being an idiot. Sit down.”

She talked for an hour without sitting, the Long Lie upright in her good hand: light and fold and ground-lean, and what heather did with water under it. Ilse wrote down every word and was angrier with each one. She had been so pleased with her six links.

At the end Brask said, “Go away. Not you, Marrow.”

Ilse turned in the doorway.

“The chains in the box by the wall are rusting. There’s tallow and a rag. Every link. Then the levelglasses, and don’t drop them, they cost more than you do. Tomorrow you’ll carry the instruments out for the Second Range class, and the day after for the Third, and you’ll do it until I say otherwise.”

“Is this a punishment?”

“It’s not paid, and it’s not an honour. It’s chains, and chains rust.” Brask was working one-handed at the toggles of her oilskin, and Ilse saw for the first time how tired she was: grey under the brown of her face, mud dried and cracked on her boots to the knee, as if she had walked a long way in it and then sat a long while without taking them off. “You were wrong by twelve links. But you knew there was a fold. None of the others saw the fold. Solberg saw boats.”

“I heard they found Oskar Fell,” Ilse said.

“I found Oskar Fell. Three days out, sitting in a salt-pool up to his chest, counting his fingers. He’d got to nine and was having trouble with the tenth.” Brask took her clay pipe out of her coat, looked at it, and put it back unlit. “Orle says he’ll mend, mostly.”

“And Yara?”

The rain played its four notes into the buckets.

“The tallow’s by the door,” said Brask. “Every link, Marrow.”

* * *

Charting was taught in a high, cold room halfway along the First Range, with benches stepped up in tiers above a long table, so that the students sat looking down on their work like gulls on a harbour. The Master of the Register did not teach first-years in their first term. They had his adjunct instead, Master Wystan Pell, a narrow, pink-handed man who handled every sheet as though it might bruise, and who began each class by reminding them that Master Hallam would see their work at the end of term and had very high standards and a very kind heart, and they should try not to disappoint either.

Today they were charting cobbles. Each of them had a tray of fine sand and a cobble the size of a hen’s egg set loose on it. A cobble on sand had a latitude of a few inches, Pell explained, so it could not go far wrong and could not do much harm when it did. They were to measure it, draw it, and attest, and then Pell would come along the table with a wooden mallet and tap each tray.

“The s-simplest charter you will ever make,” he said. “And in it is the whole of the art.”

Ilse’s cobble was a dull brown thing with a white vein of quartz across one end. She measured it from the left and near edges and from the far corner as a check, and she drew the white vein exactly, because it was the cobble’s one distinguishing feature and the sand would have trouble arguing with it. When she set her wren’s foot to the quarter-sheet she felt the small tightening in her ears that meant the charter had taken, like a string drawn up to pitch. It was light. That still surprised her. She had expected honest marks to be heavier, with no one else to carry them.

Pell came down the table tapping. Each cobble jumped and settled. Grice’s rolled a full inch and lay on its side looking guilty. Piety Mercer’s slid a quarter-inch, which she said was the sand’s fault. Ilse’s moved a hair and a half; she saw it blur and steady, like a plucked string.

Pell tapped Kasimir Reyne’s tray. The sand jumped. The cobble did not.

He struck it harder, so hard that a little fountain of sand went up and pattered down over the table, and the cobble sat in the middle of it as if it had been cast in iron and bolted through the tray into the bench.

Pell held the quarter-sheet up for them all to see. It had the beauty of a well-cut key, the beauty of nothing extra. Every line had been put down once and had stayed.

“That,” said Pell, “is what fidelity looks like.”

“It’s a stone,” said Orla Devane from the bench above, leaning back on her elbows, pleased with the world. “He’s been drawing stones since he could hold a pen. His nurse gave him gravel instead of sweets.”

“Thank you, Miss Devane.”

“He cried when they took it away.”

“*Thank* you, Miss Devane.”

Kasimir didn’t look round. He wiped his pen.

“Some of us were taught to draw before we came,” said Piety Mercer, not quite quietly. “I suppose on the Walk they give you a stick and point.”

“They give you a chain and a cairn three miles off,” said Ilse, without turning, “and the ground moves while you work. I suppose in Tessary they give you sand that holds still.”

Piety’s face closed like a purse. Orla laughed out loud. Pell said “Ladies” in a hurt voice, as though they had disappointed Master Hallam personally.

She knew how she could have made her cobble hold like his. She had known it the moment she lifted the pen. You leaned the drawing a hair toward where the sand wanted the stone, so the land had less to argue with, and signed it as the plain truth. Quince would have done it in his sleep. It wouldn’t even have been much of a lie.

She hadn’t, and her cobble had moved a hair and a half. She found she minded less than she would have expected. She minded a little.

* * *

The Library of Record had a hammerbeam roof dark with age, long tables under green-shaded lamps, and a tiled floor that made a map of the Reach, so that to reach the legal commentaries you had to walk across Tessary. Ilse had found what she wanted in the region of the Salt Steps: *Concordance Procedure, Practice of the Board*, bound in calf and smelling of mice. The Board of Errata sat four times a year, took evidence on oath, and could expel, fine, or put a Member to such use as the College saw fit. She had read that clause twice. It didn’t say what use.

She felt him come down the bay before she saw him. A quiet room went quieter.

“Miss Marrow.”

“Mr. Reyne.”

He sat down across from her without asking and laid his notebook on the table, squared to the edge. Small, black, with a clasp: *errors observed*. She had watched him write in it at Benchmarking, after he had looked at her mark on the Old Wall for a breath longer than a man looks at a thing he is not thinking about.

“I have had a reply from Hobbet,” he said.

“That was quick.”

“The express mail. Four days there, four days back, and the clerk is a prompt man.”

“Jem likes letters. He doesn’t get many.” She was doing the sum without wanting to: Reaping 23, when he wrote; eight days on the road; a day for Jem to write back in his best hand with his tongue between his teeth. It came out at today. He had read it this morning, gone and drawn his iron cobble, and come here afterwards to tell her. Courtesy or cruelty; with him they might be the same thing.

“The parish informs me that the amendment to the Tull charter is in order,” Kasimir said. “Properly filed, properly attested, in its proper place in the chest. The clerk sees no reason for further inquiry. He did not enclose a copy.” He paused. “I had hoped he would enclose a tracing of the mark. It would have saved us both a good deal of time.”

She kept her eyes on his face and her right hand flat on the book. Whether he had looked at a flourish an inch high at the foot of a parish amendment closely enough to know it again on the Old Wall, she didn’t know. She suspected he looked at everything that closely.

“I’m sorry he disappointed you,” she said.

“He followed procedure. A parish clerk has no authority to forward a charter to the Board on the request of a first-year, however politely made. I knew that, and I wrote anyway.” He opened the notebook, wrote a single line, and closed it. She would have given a mark to read the line. “The inquiry must be refiled with the Board directly. It sits in Frost. If it accepts, it sends to the Court, the Court sends to the parish, and in due course the charter comes here and someone draws the mark. That may take until spring.”

“So you have no proof.”

“I have no proof.”

“Then why tell me?”

He looked at her properly then, and she saw that his eyes were grey with blue in them, like slate in rain, and wished she hadn’t noticed.

“Because I would not like you to think I had given up. I will be patient, Miss Marrow. I am very good at being patient. I have had a great deal of practice.”

“What a thing to be good at.”

“It is a useful thing to be good at, in law. My mother is the Chancellor of the Concordance Court.” He said it the way you might mention that your mother kept bees. “I have lived in the law since I was born. It is like a house. Its walls keep out the weather.”

“Its walls belong to whoever built the house.”

“They belong to everyone who lives inside it.”

“Have you ever been outside it?” She heard her voice rise and pulled it down; an old man was asleep two bays off with his spectacles on his forehead. “Have you ever watched a man with a licence and a lord’s money draw a line through a field, and then gone to the Court and been told the line is true because it was drawn better? The law doesn’t protect the weak from the powerful. The law is what the powerful call it when they win.”

“And what do the weak call it when they win?”

She opened her mouth and shut it again.

“Justice,” he said. “Or forgery. It depends, I suppose, on whether they are caught.” He said it gently, which was the worst of it. “The law is not a good wall. It has holes in it, and the rich know where all of them are. But if everyone who disliked where it ran were free to move it in the night, there would be no wall at all. Who do you suppose suffers most then?”

She thought of the Long Field: sixty yards, the well, an appeal that cost every penny they had, her father at the window with the lamp. It was right there. It would have been easy to lay it on the table between them like a card and watch his clean face take it in.

She didn’t. It was the only thing she owned outright, and she was not going to spend it on him.

“The people outside the wall,” she said. “Same as always.”

He turned the notebook a quarter-turn on the table and turned it back.

“I do not dislike you, Miss Marrow,” he said at last. “I want you to understand that. I dislike that you are good at something you ought not to be able to do.”

“Which thing?”

“I think you know which thing.”

He stood, inclined his head with perfect, maddening courtesy, and went away across the tiled Salt Steps. Ilse read the clause about such use as the College saw fit a third time and still didn’t know what it meant.

* * *

“The trick,” said Teo, “is you don’t let it know you’re frightened.”

He was crouched over the spirit-stove on the hearthstone, the only part of the room certain to be stone. The stove was a squat brass thing the size of a fist with a blue flame that smelled like a chemist’s, forbidden in the Ranges on pain of a two-mark fine. Teo had three. He had brought them from Lisk in a hatbox along with a jar of dripping, a string of onions, a hard sausage and a tin he called *emergency mustard* and would not open for anyone.

“Don’t let what know?” said Hen.

“The pan. If you hesitate, it sticks. My grandmother says that about pans and horses and the tax-assessor.”

Hen laughed. It came all the way up from her boots and seemed to surprise her as much as anyone, and she put her hand over her mouth and then took it away again as though she’d remembered she was allowed.

Their rooms on the Wren Stair were two narrow bedrooms and a sitting room between, up three crooked flights: a hearth, a table, four chairs that didn’t match, a window over the Margin, and a wardrobe that would not stay put. On the first night it had stood flat to the wall. By the second it had taken a step forward, as though to hear better. Every morning since, Hen had measured the gap and pencilled it on the inside of the wardrobe door. Eleven inches now. Cass said it was getting brave.

Cass had her boots off and her feet on the table, which Teo said was worse than boots, and her braids hung over the chair-back with two route-cords come loose and trailing on the floor. She was telling them about a man in Verity Cross selling charms against hollowings: a knot of string and a holed pebble.

“I said, hey-ho, where’d you get it? He said, Tenting make ’em. I said, I’m Tenting. He said, then you know how good they are. Sold three to a pair of furlongs while I stood there.”

“Are they any good?” said Hen.

“It’s string and a pebble.”

“But are they any good?”

Cass considered. “Pebble’s good. You can throw it.”

Teo dished out slices of the hard sausage fried with onion in a great deal of dripping, on four chipped plates borrowed from the Mess that he fully intended to return, and they ate with their fingers. It was the best thing Ilse had eaten since Hobbet. When she said so he went red up to his curls and said the sausage did all the work, really; he only encouraged it.

“Where’s Sorry?” said Hen.

It had been asleep on the end of Ilse’s bed when they came in, a tight grey knot with its ears folded down over its face like a pair of hands. It was not there now. It was on Teo’s desk, sitting very upright beside the inkstand with its ears pricked and its amber eyes half shut, and its whole small grey face from the whiskers to the chin was black.

“Oh no,” said Teo.

The ink bottle lay on its side, empty. Not a drop had been spilled. Sorry regarded them with mild, contented inquiry and licked its lips with a small tongue that was also black, and a blue-black bead ran down one whisker and dropped off.

“That was my good ink,” said Teo. “Lisk ink, my uncle sent it, it’s oak gall from the Ambry woods, it doesn’t fade, you could write a will with it—”

“You could write a cat with it, apparently,” said Hen, and set herself off, and Cass laughed until she had to hold on to the chair-back, and Ilse found she was laughing too, helplessly, with her greasy hands held up away from her coat. Sorry watched them with its ears turning one way and then the other. Then it walked across the desk, lay down on Teo’s Mensuration notes, and began to wash its black face with a black paw.

“It’s going to be sick,” said Teo.

“Ossels eat ink,” said Cass, wiping her eyes. “Ink, flies, seeds, letters from your mother. It’ll be smug, but it’ll be fine.”

“It’s already smug,” said Teo in despair.

They stayed up later than they should have. Hen told them how the brine in the salt-pans went pink at high summer, so the pans looked like a field of roses laid flat, and how her father could taste a pan on one fingertip and tell you to the day when it would crust. Teo told them about Anselm’s pie shop, which had been Anselm’s for a hundred and nine years, and his grandmother, who had once thrown a tax-assessor into the canal. Cass didn’t tell them anything about the Tenting. She sang instead, very low, something without words that went up and down like a road over hills, and when Hen asked what it was Cass said, “Song,” and wouldn’t say more.

Ilse didn’t tell them anything either. Nobody seemed to mind. She sat against the wall with her knees drawn up and listened to the three of them, and to the Wren Stair settling round them for the night, a joist creaking, the window-frame easing a hair in its stone. She had not sat in a room like this, warm and late and full of other people’s voices, since she was a child. She didn’t know what to do with it. She held it carefully, as you hold an egg you’ve found in the grass.

It was nearly midnight when Hen said, “There’s something under the door.”

It was a square of paper pushed through from the landing, though nobody had heard a step on the stair: a page torn from a field-book, the kind with a faint blue grid, and on it, in a cramped, square hand that pressed so hard it had dented through the grid, eight words.

*Walk with me tomorrow, Marrow. Before the bell.*

There was no signature. There didn’t need to be.

“Who’s it from?” said Hen.

Ilse folded it along its crease and put it in the pocket of her father’s coat, where it lay against her ribs.

“My employer,” she said.
