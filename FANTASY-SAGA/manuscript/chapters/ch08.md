# Chapter 8
## The Quiet

Brask was waiting at the door of the Fieldwork shed at four in the morning with a shuttered lantern in her good hand and the Long Lie crooked in the elbow of the other. She looked Ilse up and down once, the coat, the boots, the scarf Hesta had knitted in a fit of temper two winters ago, and said, “You’ll do,” and walked off into the dark.

Ilse followed. There was no path. There was the causeway’s end, and a gap in the bracken, and then the Margin, black and breathing.

They walked for half an hour without a word. The lantern made a small gold room around them that moved when they moved, and outside it the heath went on in every direction, felt rather than seen: the wet slap of bracken against Ilse’s knees, the give of peat, a smell of salt and crushed thyme. Once something large went away from them through the heather, unhurried, and Brask didn’t even turn her head. The level in Ilse’s ears had dropped as soon as they left the causeway. Out here it wasn’t a steady hum but a low, uneven note, like a hum from someone who has forgotten the tune.

At last Brask stopped on a slight rise and set the lantern down and opened its shutter wide.

“Look,” she said. “Don’t talk. Look.”

Ilse looked. Gorse, a scatter of it, black-green and glittering with wet. Heather. A slab of grey stone half-buried. Beyond the light, darkness, and very faintly in the east the first thinning of it over the Reach.

“The gorse,” said Brask, after a while. “Which way’s it leaning?”

It wasn’t leaning. Ilse said so. Brask said nothing, and Ilse looked again, longer, and saw that it was. Not the bushes, which grew any way they liked, but the tips of them, the young green spines at the end of each shoot. On every bush within the light they turned very slightly the same way, north and east, like a congregation turned toward the pulpit.

“Toward the Cape,” she said.

“Toward the hold. Gorse is a coward. It grows toward whatever’s firmest. In the Reach it doesn’t bother, because everything’s firm. Out here it’s got opinions.” Brask crouched, awkwardly, her stilled hand held out from her body, and touched a fingertip to the stone slab. “Now the dew.”

The slab was furred all over with dew, fine grey beads close as the hairs on a moth. Beside it, on a patch of bare peat no more than a foot away, the dew lay differently: fewer beads, larger, and when Ilse bent close she saw they were trembling. Not much: as water trembles in a cup when a cart goes by in the street.

“Held ground keeps its dew still,” said Brask. “The stone’s held. Someone charted it once, and it hasn’t forgotten. The peat isn’t. It doesn’t know quite where it is, so its water doesn’t either. On a still morning you can walk a line across the Margin by the dew and never put a foot wrong. You won’t find that in a book. Most of the people who write books have never been up at four.”

She stood, and drove the Long Lie into the peat with a soft chock, and then into the edge of the stone, where it rang. Two notes: one dead, one bright.

“Now shut your eyes,” she said, “and tell me what your ears are doing.”

Ilse shut her eyes. It was hard, at first, to separate the level from the wind and the sea. Then she had it: the note was higher when she faced the stone and lower when she turned from it, and when she turned slowly round in a full circle it rose and fell like a voice saying a long word.

“It changes,” she said. “With direction.”

“That’s the grain. You’re hearing which way is more held. Cass Tenter walks by that the way you walk by your feet; she doesn’t even know she’s doing it. You can learn it, slower. You’ll never be as good as her, and you’ll always be better at saying what you heard. Between the two of you, you might make one surveyor.”

They went on. Brask taught as she walked, in short hard sentences with long gaps between them. She showed Ilse how a stream on the Margin cut its bank deeper on the side toward the Reach. She showed her a wayhorn’s scrape on a birch trunk, and the shed tine lying under it, its points all curving the same way, like a hand pointing home. She made Ilse walk fifty paces with her eyes shut, following the level, and Ilse came out eleven paces off line and furious, and Brask said, “Eleven. Good. Last week you’d have been in the sea.”

It was when Brask bent to pick up the antler that Ilse saw the hand properly. Brask had hooked the Long Lie through the curl of her grey fingers to free her good hand, as you might hang a coat on a peg, and the fingers didn’t close round it or open to let it go. They stayed as they were. The staff rested in them as in a bracket bolted to a wall. When Brask straightened and took the staff back, the hand stayed in its curl around nothing, and the skin of it in the lantern light had the dull sheen of old pewter.

Ilse looked away, but not quickly enough.

“Don’t,” said Brask.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to.”

They walked another furlong. The sky had gone from black to the colour of a bruise, and the gorse was coming up out of the dark in grey shapes, each one leaning its tips toward the College.

“Why’d you come, Marrow?” Brask said.

“To be a surveyor.”

“Liar.”

Cass had said that, at the cairn. Ilse was beginning to think it was a thing people said to her the way they said good morning. She walked a few paces, and then, because the dark made it easier and because Brask hadn’t once used her first name, she said, “My family had a field. Eleven acres, in Wick Fallow. When I was twelve a lord’s surveyor re-charted the parish and the boundary moved sixty yards, with the well in it. It was all legal. The Court upheld it.” She kept her voice flat. “I want a Patent. Then I can chart it myself. Properly.”

It was true, every word. It just wasn’t all of it. It was the bones without the joints, and she waited for Brask to see the gaps.

Brask said nothing at all. She walked, the Long Lie swinging, the lantern swinging, and after a while Ilse understood that nothing was what she was going to say. It had been heard, and weighed, and put somewhere. Ilse didn’t know whether to be relieved.

They stopped at the last rise before the causeway. From here she could see the Drift Wing strung out along the dark, its windows beginning to light as the College woke, and out beyond it, west, the Margin going on and on into a grey that wasn’t sky and wasn’t land.

“Yara Penhallow,” said Ilse. “Will you go out again?”

“I’ve been out four times.” Brask looked west. The patch on her right eye made her face, in profile, a face with only one side. “The Margin keeps what it keeps. Sometimes it gives things back. Fell it gave back. Penhallow it hasn’t yet.” She knocked the Long Lie twice against the causeway stones, ringing, and turned for the College. “That’s the bell in ten minutes. Run.”

* * *

It was eleven before she was done in the Fieldwork shed, and then Brask sent her off with a canvas roll of instruments under one arm and orders to take them to the infirmary.

“Orle’s calipers,” Brask said. “And her fine chain. She lent them for Fell. Tell her the chain had a kink in the forty-third link and I took it out, and if she wants to argue she knows where I live.”

The infirmary was at the north end of the Old Court, where the stone was oldest, in a long range of whitewashed wards. It smelled of carbolic and boiled linen, which made Ilse think of her mother’s copper with a homesick little jolt. A porter pointed her down a corridor. It was quiet, with tall windows on one side giving onto a garden of herbs, and benches against the other wall, and on one of the benches a young man was sitting with his hands in his lap, looking at the wall.

She knew him slightly; everyone knew everyone slightly by now. Wil Ashdown, a second-year, a chain. She had seen him in the Mess in her first week, she thought, or someone like him. He was a tall young man with a long, pleasant, bony face and brown hair that wanted cutting, and he was sitting quite straight, with his feet together, as though someone had arranged him there and gone away.

“Good morning,” Ilse said.

He turned his head and looked at her. It took him some time. It wasn’t slowness, quite. It was that he seemed to have to decide, at each part of the movement, that he would go on with it.

“Good morning,” he said.

His eyes were brown, and there was nothing wrong with them, and they were the worst thing she had ever seen. They were flat. That was the only word she could find. They took her in the way a window takes in light, without any interest in it, and when she smiled at him they did nothing at all.

Then she looked at his hands.

The left hand was an ordinary young man’s hand, chapped from the cold. The right lay palm-down on his knee, and from the knuckles to the tips the fingers were grey. It wasn’t dirt and it wasn’t bruising. They were grey the way slate is grey, all through, with a faint dull sheen, and the forefinger where a pen would rest was darkest of all.

Ilse knew that grey. Quince’s hands had gone that colour in his last two years, the right worse than the left, until he could hardly hold a pen and had to tie it to his fingers with thread. *That’s the bill coming in*, he’d said, and laughed, and she had laughed too. He had been signing land for fifty years.

Wil Ashdown was twenty, perhaps twenty-one.

“Are you waiting for someone?” she asked.

“Yes.” He considered. “No. I am waiting.”

A door opened down the corridor and a woman came out of it fast, drying her hands on a towel: middle-sized, middle-aged, in a grey gown with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, with a frown that looked as though it had been there since breakfast and expected to stay until supper.

“Who are you?”

“Ilse Marrow. Field-Surveyor Brask sent your calipers back. And your chain. She says it had a kink in the forty-third link and she’s taken it out.”

“Of course she has.” Dr. Sabeth Orle took the canvas roll and tucked it under her arm without looking at it. Her eyes went past Ilse to the young man on the bench, and something in her face shut and locked. “Wil, you’re to go back to the ward. Dinner’s at twelve.”

“Yes,” said Wil. He didn’t move.

Orle went and put her hand under his elbow, and he stood when she lifted, and walked when she walked. Ilse stood aside to let them pass. Through the ward door as it swung she saw a long whitewashed room and a row of beds. In the third a girl lay propped on pillows, a furlong by the look of her, with her hair in a neat plait over one shoulder and her hands folded outside the blanket. Her face was turned to the window. She wasn’t looking out of it.

Orle came back out alone. She seemed surprised to find Ilse still there.

“Is it catching?” Ilse said.

Orle looked at her properly for the first time. “What do you think it is, Miss Marrow?”

“I don’t know. I heard it called Drift-fatigue.”

“Did you.” Orle snapped the towel straight and folded it into precise thirds. “Lectures and fieldwork and early rising, and grown young men and women out of the best families in the Reach go flat as pond water and grey in the hand. It isn’t fatigue. Whatever the Provost likes to call it.” She seemed to hear herself, and stopped. “Thank Brask for the chain.”

She went back into the ward. The door swung shut behind her and went on swinging, smaller and smaller, until it didn’t.

* * *

The Provost spoke at supper.

The Mess was full, as it always was at the evening meal: three long tables of students down the length of the hall, and the high table across the end under the painted ceiling, where the College’s stars wheeled in gilt across a sky of blue that had faded to the colour of a thrush’s egg. Provost Steyne rose between the soup and the mutton. He was a tall, silver-haired man with a beautiful voice, and he used it the way some men use a good walking stick, not to lean on but to be seen with.

“A word before we eat,” he said, “and I shall keep it brief, because Cook has views on mutton going cold. Some of you will have heard that a few of our students are resting in the infirmary. I want to put your minds at ease. We are seeing a little seasonal Drift-fatigue, as we do most autumns when the weather turns and the Wing is restless. It is not serious. It is not catching. Dr. Orle has the matter well in hand. The cure is rest and broth, and I am assured by Cook that the College has a great deal of broth.”

There was a ripple of laughter. Someone at the furlongs’ table called out, “Is that the cure or the cause?” and the laughter went up a notch, and the Provost smiled in a way that let them know he had enjoyed the joke and would now like it to stop.

“Rest,” he said, “and broth. And a little less of the Chain & Anchor, perhaps, for some of you. Thank you.” He sat down.

“Broth,” said Cass, into her soup. “Road and rain.”

“It’s quite nice broth,” said Teo, doubtfully.

“That isn’t the point, Teo.”

“I know it isn’t the point. I was only saying.”

“My gran had a cure for fatigue,” said Hen. “She’d stand you in the brine-pan up to your knees till you felt better. Nobody was ever tired twice.” She put her hand over her mouth. “Tide’s out on broth, I think. We should send them a gran.”

She turned to laugh at something Orla Devane was saying further down the table. Ilse wasn’t listening to any of them. Across the hall, near the door, a proctor had come in, and Wil Ashdown was with her. Ilse didn’t know why. Perhaps someone had thought supper in the Mess would do him good, or perhaps he had simply walked here, because he had been told to walk somewhere and had not been told to stop. He stood beside the proctor looking at the long tables with his flat eyes, and the chains near the door had gone quiet, and then a second proctor came, and between them they turned him round, gently, and led him out again, his grey right hand hanging at his side.

At the high table, Master Hallam had watched it too. He sat a little way down from the Provost, small and round-shouldered in his dark gown, his bald head shining under the lamps and his white beard neat on his chest. His grey-gloved hands lay on the cloth on either side of his plate. He had put down his spoon. He had eaten, Ilse thought, almost nothing. As the door closed behind Wil Ashdown he took off his spectacles and pressed finger and thumb to the bridge of his nose and sat like that for a moment with his eyes shut, an old man tired by a sorrow that wasn’t his.

Then he put the spectacles back on and murmured to the Provost, and the Provost laughed, and the mutton came.

* * *

She couldn’t sleep.

It wasn’t the room, though the room was restless tonight. The wardrobe creaked. The floor made small sounds like someone turning over in bed. Through the wall she could hear Teo snoring, gently and with great regularity, like a man sawing a very soft plank, and through the other wall Hen’s breathing, and nothing at all from Cass, who slept like the dead or like a fox, it was hard to tell which. Sorry lay on Ilse’s feet, a warm grey weight no bigger than a muff, and when she moved it complained in its sleep without waking.

She got up in the end, and lit the candle, and sat at the table with her father’s coat around her shoulders.

There was scrap paper in the drawer: old Mensuration sheets of Hen’s, written over on one side in her quick round figures. Ilse turned one over and took up the pen and drew her mark.

Three strokes and a spur. The first stroke down and a little left. The second straight down, the longest. The third down and a little right. Then, lifting the pen not quite off the paper, the spur, a short hook back from the foot of the middle stroke. A wren’s foot, as a wren leaves it in frost on a windowsill.

She drew it again. And again, a row of them across the page like a small bird walking.

For three years she had drawn this mark over the top of another one. Quince’s mark had been a sort of knot: a crooked, old-fashioned loop with a hook caught through it, like a fish on a line. She had traced it under her own so many times in blind ink that her hand could make it in the dark. She had never liked drawing it. It had always felt, under the pen, like walking in someone else’s boots: they went where you wanted, but they rubbed, and they were shaped by another man’s feet. She had thought that was just the blind ink. She had thought that was just what it was like.

It wasn’t. She could feel the difference now in her wrist and the heel of her hand. The wren’s foot came off the pen as naturally as breath. It didn’t rub anywhere. It was hers, and on the Old Wall it was cut in stone under a brass arrow with her name beside it, and every time she set it on a sheet the sheet tightened a hair in her ears and let go again, and there was no one underneath it. Nobody carrying her. Only her.

She turned the pen over in her fingers, and looked at them. Her right forefinger was stained black along the side, as it had been since she was fourteen. Under the ink the skin was pink.

She thought of Wil Ashdown’s forefinger, darker than the rest, where a pen would rest.

She thought of Quince laughing, *that’s the bill coming in.* Fifty years of signing land. And a chain at twenty, in a College that kept its students on rest and broth.

She didn’t know what that meant. She didn’t know enough yet to know what it meant, and she was too tired to pretend she did. She folded the page of wren’s feet and slipped it into the lining of her father’s coat, where she had sewn a pocket the week she arrived and kept the things she didn’t want found. Then she pinched out the candle.

Sorry had moved in her absence to the warm place she had left. When she got back into bed it grumbled and shifted down again to her feet, and turned round twice, and lay down with its too-big ears flat, and she lay in the dark listening to Teo’s soft saw and the creak of the wandering wardrobe, with her right hand closed loosely on the blanket, until the window began to grey.
