# Chapter 9
## Rain on the Register

It had rained for two days, and on the morning of the tenth of Gleaning it was still raining, and the Long Gallery was raining too.

The Gallery ran along the top of the Second Range under a roof of leaded glass, a hundred and ninety-odd links of bare boards with tall windows down one side and a row of doors down the other. On a fine morning it was the best room in the College, full of grey light and the smell of the sea. This morning water came through the leading in a hundred places and stood on the boards in shining puddles, and the party went down it at First Bell in the dark with the lantern hissing and the chain slithering wet through their hands.

“Tally,” called Hen from the far end, invisible.

“Forty,” said Ilse, feeling the four-pointed brass tag go past her fingers. “Forty-one. Hold there.”

“Holding. I’m standing in a lake. Did you know I was standing in a lake?”

“We all know you’re standing in a lake,” said Cass, who had the levelglass and was crouched in the middle of the Gallery over a puddle, watching the bead of quicksilver. “Whole Gallery’s a lake. Bead’s swimming. Ground’s leaning toward the stair this morning. Gallery’s long.”

“How long?”

“Long.”

“Cass.”

“Couple of links. Ask the chain, it’s the one with numbers.”

The chain said two links and a half. The Gallery had been one chain and ninety-four links yesterday, and today it was ninety-six and a half, and the Wren Stair had a new tread in it, about a third of the way down, that Teo had found with his foot in the dark and nearly fallen over.

“Thirty-one treads,” he said, at the bottom, his voice coming up the stairwell with a little echo to it. “It was thirty yesterday. I counted. I always count. There’s a new one and it’s shorter than the others by, I think, an inch and a bit, and it’s wet, and I don’t like it.”

“Nobody likes it, Teo.”

“I know. I just want it written down that I especially don’t.”

They drew it on the board under the dripping lantern, all four of them hunched over it to keep the rain off the paper: the Stair with its new tread, the landing, the Gallery two and a half links longer, the doors in their places. It was ugly work and they did it fast, the way you bail a boat. Three chains and forty-one links had become three chains and forty-four, and they had to say so, honestly and precisely, before First Bell ended and the stretch was signed, or by tomorrow it might be three chains and sixty and the Wren Stair might open on the gorse.

Sorry rode through all of it inside Ilse’s coat. It had not meant to. She had left it asleep on the bed, but when she reached the landing it had begun to make a noise, a high thin wail like a kettle left on the hob, and she had gone back and stuffed it inside her coat before it woke the Stair. Now it sat with its head out between the second and third buttons, ears flattened against the wet, watching the lantern.

“You know they’re not allowed in the Register,” said Hen.

“It’s not in the Register. It’s in my coat.”

“And your coat’s going in the Register.”

“My coat,” said Ilse, “is a Member of the College, and has privileges.”

* * *

The Register Antechamber lay at the bottom of a long stair under the Old Court, through a door of black oak bound with iron. It was the first level of the Register and the only one most students would ever see: a long vaulted room, dry and warm after the Gallery, lit by brass lamps that hung on chains from the groins of the vault. Slanted desks stood along both walls, and at each desk a party bent over its folio, copying fair from its rough board. The air smelled of ink and lamp-oil and wet wool. Past the desks, at the far end, a second door stood shut. It led down to the levels where the parish charters were kept, and to Master Hallam’s study, and no student went through it.

At a high counter by the entrance sat Merrin Coyle, the Registrar-Adjunct, handing out folios. He was a thin, damp-looking man of about thirty-five, with spectacles he kept pushing up his nose with one inky knuckle and shirt-cuffs so stained with ink that they might have been dyed. He kept a cup of tea at his elbow, milky and very sweet, and drank it in quick nervous sips between parties, like a bird at a puddle.

“Wren Stair,” he said, before Ilse had spoken. He found their four folios in a rack without looking. “You’re wet. Don’t drip on them. Master Hallam likes them signed here, mind, not upstairs—”

“—not in the Ranges,” said Hen, Cass and Teo together, and Coyle looked hurt.

“It’s a good rule,” he said. “It keeps the sheets together.”

The folios were heavy linen-rag sheets, a little larger than a quarter, each printed with a faint grey frame, the College seal and the title of the stretch in copperplate. The *Wren Stair, Second Range*; the *Landing*; the *Long Gallery, East*; the *Long Gallery, West*. Each needed one mark. They took the desk nearest the counter because it was under a lamp, and Teo, who had the best hand for numbers, read off the board while Ilse drew the Stair and Hen the landing and Cass, swearing softly, the doors of the Gallery.

“I’ll sign the Gallery West too,” said Hen, when Cass had finished. “If you like. I’m quickest.”

“You signed two yesterday,” said Teo.

“I’m quickest every day.” She dipped her pen. “It’s the Salt Steps. We’re brought up to sign for things. Barrels, nets, brothers.”

Ilse took the Gallery East. She drew the last line, the end wall, and checked the length against the board—one chain, ninety-six links and a half, Atlas help them—and dipped her pen and set her wren’s foot in the box at the bottom right: three strokes and a spur, fresh and black and glistening under the lamp. She felt it take. A small, clean tightening in her ears, and then the familiar lightness, nothing pulling, nothing behind it. One chain and ninety-six links and a half of the Long Gallery, held by her.

Sorry chose that moment to see the tea.

She felt it gather itself inside her coat, a small hard coil of interest. She said, “No—” and grabbed, and caught a handful of grey fur, and the rest of Sorry went out between the buttons like water out of a cracked jug, up onto the desk, across Teo’s board, and onto the counter, where Merrin Coyle’s tea stood steaming at his elbow.

Coyle saw it come. He made a sound like a hinge. Sorry put its face into the cup, and Coyle snatched at the cup, and the cup went over.

Milky tea went in a broad brown wave across the counter and over the edge of it, and down onto the desk below, and across all four of the Wren Stair’s folios, ink still wet.

“Oh, *no*,” said Coyle. “Oh no no no—the blotters—the *blotters*—”

He came round the counter faster than Ilse would have thought him capable of moving, with a sheaf of blotting paper in each hand, and began to press them down on the folios with little moaning sounds. Teo was apologizing in a continuous stream, not to anyone in particular. Cass had Sorry by the scruff and was holding it at arm’s length, where it hung blinking its amber eyes with milk on its whiskers, unrepentant. At the other desks heads had come up all along the room.

Ilse lifted the Gallery East by its dry corner before Coyle could reach it.

Her drawing had survived. The tea had caught only the bottom edge, a pale brown stain running in across the corner where her mark was. The ink of the wren’s foot had bloomed a little in the wet, the way gall ink does, its sharp edges gone soft, the black going faintly purple at the rims.

And under it there was a second line.

It was very faint. It ran from beneath the spur of her mark, where the tea had soaked the paper through, out toward the edge of the sheet in a fine straight thread, and it was violet. Not the faint purple bloom of her own wet ink. A paler colour, clean and cold, the colour of a crocus in frost. She knew that colour. She had seen it a hundred times by shuttered lantern, under the damp edge of her own forgeries, where Quince’s knot lay hidden in the paper and showed only when the paper was wet.

She stood quite still with the sheet in her hand.

Coyle took it from her. “Let me, let me, it must be blotted at once or it’ll cockle—” He laid it flat and pressed a blotter across the corner and held it down with the flat of both hands, and when he lifted the blotter the stain had gone pale and dull and the paper was only damp, and under her mark there was nothing at all. Only linen-rag and a fading tea-mark, and her wren’s foot gone a little fat at the edges.

“There,” said Coyle, breathing hard. “There. No harm. No harm at all. It’s still a good sheet. The mark’s taken, I can see it’s taken. Oh, Miss Marrow, your *cat*.”

“I’m very sorry,” said Ilse.

“I know you are. Everyone always is.” He gathered the four folios together, damp and stained, and squared them on the counter and looked at them with the misery of a man whose day has been spoiled before breakfast. “Master Hallam won’t like it. He likes them clean.”

“It’s an ossel,” said Cass. “It’s not a cat. It’s just being a cat for her.”

“I don’t care what it is,” said Coyle, with surprising force. “Get it out of the Register.”

Out on the stair Ilse stood in the cold with her back to the wet stone and Sorry inside her coat again, its small damp head under her chin, and thought it through. It was what she did with any strangeness: walked round it, measured it, and wrote it down.

She had smudged it. That was what had happened. The tea had wet her mark and she had dragged the sheet, lifting it, and drawn a thread of her own ink out across the wet. Gall ink went violet when it was wet; everyone’s did. College ink was gall ink. The line had been straight because she had pulled the sheet straight. It had been a different violet because it was thinned with tea. And she had been looking for blind ink for nine years, every time paper got damp, because that was the habit of the trade, and she had seen what she was used to seeing. That was all. That was all it was.

It was a good explanation. It closed. She had made a hundred charters close on worse.

“Marrow?” said Teo, behind her. He had come up the stair with the board under his arm and rain still in his curls. “You all right? You look as if you’ve seen—I don’t know. A long division.”

“I’m fine. I’m hungry.”

“I’m always hungry,” said Teo, relieved. “Come on. There’s porridge. Hen says there might be honey, but Hen says that every day.”

* * *

Master Hallam’s lecture on the Principles of Hold was given in the Antechamber itself, at ten, when the Morning Survey sheets had been filed away and the slanted desks cleared. The first-years sat on benches brought in for the purpose, in rows, with the lamps lit overhead even in daytime because no daylight reached that far underground. Ilse had been dreading going back. In fact the room looked entirely ordinary. Coyle was at his counter with a fresh cup of tea, which he kept a hand around at all times. He did not look at her.

Hallam came in by the far door with a flat leather case under his arm. He was smaller than she had remembered from the Lantern Gate, and older; he walked carefully, the way a man walks in a house full of things he doesn’t want to break. But when he turned to face them and smiled, the room warmed by a degree.

“Good morning, my dears,” he said. “I hear the Long Gallery has been raining. I hope none of you drowned. It would make a great deal of paperwork for Merrin, and Merrin has had a trying morning already.”

Coyle went pink. The benches laughed.

Hallam set the case down on the lectern and unbuckled it. His hands were gloved in grey kid, close-fitting and soft, buttoned at the wrist with a single pearl button, and he moved them with the delicacy of long practice. From the case he drew a single sheet, old and brown and soft as cloth, and held it up.

“This,” he said, “is a charter of the Ammonite Hills. The northern quarries, the Delve and Corbel Edge, in the hand of a surveyor called Ottilie Vance, attested in the spring of 803. Four hundred years. It is not a copy. It is the original, from Level Four, and if any of you sneeze on it I shall have to ask you to leave the College.” He smiled over his half-moon spectacles. “I am going to pass it round. Hold it by the edges and feel it. I don’t mean the paper. I mean what the paper is doing.”

It came along the rows from hand to hand, slowly, each student holding it a moment longer than they meant to. Ilse watched their faces change as it reached them. Grice’s went blank with surprise. Piety Mercer closed her eyes. Kasimir, at the end of the row in front, held it very still and very level, and when he passed it on she saw his fingers stay curved for a moment, as though the shape of it were still in them.

Then it was in her hands.

The level in her ears dropped and deepened all at once, the way the sound of the sea deepens when you put your head under. It wasn’t loud. It was low, lower than any note she had ever felt, the sound of a bell heard through a hill. The sheet weighed nothing and it was heavy. It was heavy the way a held breath is heavy. She looked down at it and saw the fine brown lines of the quarry faces, the hachures of the slopes, the little numbered benches of the old workings, and all round the margin, crowded in, dozens and dozens of small dark marks, re-attestations, the witness-marks of four centuries of surveyors who had walked those hills and agreed with Ottilie Vance and set their names to it.

Four hundred years of people saying *yes, this is true; yes, still.* That was what she was holding. That was what was humming in her ears. Not the drawing. The agreement.

She passed it on to Hen with great care. Her hands, after, felt strange and light and empty, like hands that have just put down a child.

“What you felt,” said Hallam, when the sheet had come back to him, “is the oldest truth in our trade. A charter is not strong because it is old. It is strong because it has been *kept*. Every surveyor who walks a boundary and finds it true and signs again adds to its hold. Every farmer who ploughs to the line and no farther, every child who walks the lane to school by the way the map says, agrees with it a little. The Ammonite Hills have been agreed with for four hundred years. That is why the Delve has never slipped.” He laid the charter down in its case as gently as if it were asleep. “Every map is an act of love, my dears. We hold her still so that the children of the Reach can find their way home.”

Ilse had come to the College ready to despise everything in it. She found that she could not despise that.

* * *

At the end of the lecture, as the benches emptied, he said, “Miss Marrow, would you stay a moment?” and she stayed, with Cass making faces at her from the door until Teo pulled her away by the sleeve.

Hallam was buckling the case. He did it slowly, the gloved fingers patient with the old straps.

“I am sorry about the tea,” Ilse said.

“The tea?” He looked up, puzzled, and then his face cleared. “Oh, the folios. Merrin told me. My dear, if I expelled every student whose ossel had got into the Register I should have no students and a great many ossels. Is it a nice one?”

“It’s—” She considered. “It eats ink.”

“They all eat ink. I had one myself, at your age. It ate most of a treatise on precedence and was never quite the same afterwards. Neither was I.” He finished the last strap and laid his hands flat on the case. “No. I wanted to ask you something else. I watched you at Benchmarking. I said then that you had a draftswoman’s hand, and I have been looking at your Charting work since, which Master Pell sends me, and I was right. Your cobble moved a hair and a half,” he added, with a twinkle, “which is very respectable, and the quartz vein was beautifully observed. Pell was quite moved.”

“Thank you.”

“I need a copyist in the Register. Two evenings a week, after supper. Fair copies of the older parish charters, for the working files, so the originals are handled less. It is dull work and close work and it wants a steady hand and a good eye, and I think you have both.” He paused. “It is paid, of course. Half a mark an evening. And you would have the run of Levels One to Three, which no first-year has had in some years. I think you would find them interesting.”

Half a mark an evening. A mark a week. Ilse had six marks and six left of everything she had saved, after the coach, and nothing at all coming in. She did the sum before she could stop herself: by Thaw, near twenty marks. It wasn’t the Long Field. It was a long way from the Long Field. But it was something going into the tin instead of out of it, for the first time since she had left Hobbet.

And it wasn’t only the money. That was the part she didn’t want to look at directly. He had watched her at Benchmarking. He had read her Charting work. He thought she had a good eye. No one had said that to her since Quince, and Quince had said it only once, drunk, and then denied it in the morning.

“I’d like that,” she said. “Very much.”

“Good. Good.” He looked genuinely pleased, the way a man looks when a small plan has come right. “The week after next, then. Merrin will show you where everything is.” He took up the case and turned to go, and then turned back, and when he spoke again it was more quietly. “I gather you have been helping Field-Surveyor Brask.”

“Carrying instruments. Cleaning chains.”

“She is a remarkable surveyor. I want you to know that I think so. We were on the Sallow together, a long time ago.” He was silent a moment, and his face, in the lamplight, looked older. “She is a brilliant woman, my dear, broken by grief. It is not her fault. But grief makes people hard to stand next to, sometimes. Don’t let her break you.”

He touched her shoulder lightly with one grey-gloved hand, as an uncle might, and went away through the far door toward his study, and the door closed behind him without a sound.

* * *

Brask was in the Fieldwork shed, sitting on an upturned bucket by the open door with a chain across her knees, working tallow into the links with her good hand and holding the chain down with the heel of the grey one. Outside, the rain had thinned at last to a drifting mist, and the heath had gone silver with it, every spike of gorse strung with drops.

Ilse took the other end of the chain without being asked, and sat down on the step, and worked.

They did twenty links in silence. The tallow smelled of mutton and smoke. Brask’s pipe was in her teeth, unlit, and she moved it from one side of her mouth to the other now and then without seeming to notice she was doing it.

“Master Hallam’s asked me to copy in the Register,” said Ilse. “Two evenings a week. Paid.”

Brask’s hand went on working the tallow into the link. Forty. Forty-one.

She did not look up. The rag went round the link, and through it, and round again, and the mist moved over the heath outside like breath on a glass, and a long way off a cairn-crow said something short and harsh and was answered. Ilse waited. She found that she was holding her end of the chain very tightly, and made herself loosen her hand.

Forty-six. Forty-seven. The fifty-link tally, round and brass, went through Brask’s fingers and she rubbed it clean with her thumb.

She took the pipe out of her mouth, and looked at it, and put it back.

“Mind the stairs,” she said.
