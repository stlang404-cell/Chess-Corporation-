# Chapter 10
## The Six Laws

“The Law of Witness,” said Master Hallam, and wrote it on the slate in a round, clear, old-fashioned hand. “A record binds only what its maker has personally measured.”

The chalk squeaked. His grey glove left a faint pale smear along the bottom of the slate where his wrist brushed it, and he didn’t notice, or didn’t mind.

“This is why we walk,” he said. “Not because the Board is cruel. Because the land knows the difference between a surveyor who has stood on it and a clerk who has copied what she said. You may copy the finest charter in the Register a thousand times, my dears, and every copy will be a picture. Only the walker binds.”

It was the fourteenth of Gleaning, and the Antechamber was cold. They had begun the Laws at ten and it was now nearly noon, and the slate had filled slowly down its length like a field being ploughed. Witness. Fidelity: a record holds in proportion to its truth. Precedence: where records conflict, the truer wins, and at equal truth the older. Attestation: a record binds only when its maker signs it with intent. At each one Hallam stopped and told them a story, a lost case or an old disaster, a road in the Salt Steps that had held for two hundred years on the strength of one careful woman’s chain, and the room listened as children listen. Ilse wrote it all down. She knew most of it already, in the way you know the bones of your own hand. She had never heard it said so well.

“The Law of Burden.” Hallam wrote it, and turned, and for a moment didn’t speak. “An attesting surveyor carries the strain of every record she has signed. When the land pulls against the record, she feels it. It is the price of the trade, and I will not pretend to you that it is small. Some of the masters you see at high table carry more than you will carry in your whole lives. We carry it gladly. It is what holds her.” He smiled. “And it is why I shall be grateful, when you are all very great surveyors, if you would draw your charters carefully, so that the land has nothing to complain of. A good charter is light. A careless one is a stone in your shoe for fifty years.”

The sixth he wrote at the bottom of the slate, and underlined. *The Law of Latitude.* A record can fix land only within the range of states it might plausibly take. Held land had a latitude of inches. The Margin had rods and furlongs. The deep Drift had more than anyone had ever measured.

“You cannot chart a mountain into a lake,” said Hallam, “however beautiful your drawing. You can chart a hedge a little to the west, if the ground would bear a hedge there. That is the whole of Concordance in a sentence, and Magistra Calder will spend a year teaching you the rest of it.” He laid down the chalk and dusted his gloves together. “Six Laws. They are the grammar of the world. Everything that holds, holds by them.”

Ilse waited.

She waited with her pen above the page, the way you wait at the top of a stair for the last step. He had stopped at six. He was turning to the lectern. He was opening his notes. He was going on, in that kind, careful voice, to talk about the Ninefold and how they had come to the Laws, by trial and long error, in the years of the Wandering.

There were seven.

Every hedge-forger in the Fallowmarch knew there were seven. Quince had taught her the seventh before he taught her to hold a chain properly. *The Law of Transfer*, he’d called it, sitting in his kitchen with his grey hands round a cup of something that wasn’t tea. *A mark can be ghosted. Trace a man’s mark under your own in blind ink, and the weight goes down his line instead of yours. They don’t teach it at the Big College, girl, and do you know why? Because the Big College would sooner nobody knew it could be done.* He had laughed, and coughed, and she had thought it was the drink.

She had thought for nine years that the College didn’t teach it because the College didn’t know. Gentlemen with Patents didn’t trace dead men’s marks in the dark. They had no need. Now she sat in the Register Antechamber with the six Laws in chalk on the slate before her, and the seventh missing from the bottom like a stair taken out, and found she could not tell which was true: that they didn’t know, or that they didn’t say.

“Master Hallam.”

It was Kasimir. He had raised his hand, not high, the way a man does who expects to be seen.

“Mr. Reyne.”

“The Law of Precedence speaks of conflicting records,” Kasimir said. “Two honest surveyors who disagree. But what of contrary records? A record made untrue on purpose, within latitude, by someone who knows how to make the land believe it. Does such a record hold by the same Laws? Or is there some other principle at work?”

He did not look round at Ilse. He did not need to. She felt the question go through the room and come to rest against her like a hand laid flat on a door.

Hallam took off his spectacles and polished them on a fold of his gown. He seemed to consider it seriously, which was worse than if he hadn’t.

“A very good question,” he said. “And the answer, I am afraid, is a very dull one. A contrary record holds by Fidelity, like any other. It holds exactly as well as it is made, and no better, and the moment a truer record is set against it, it fails. There is no special principle, Mr. Reyne.” He put the spectacles back on and smiled at the room. “Forgery is not a law of nature. It is a failure of character.”

They laughed. All of them. Hen laughed, and Teo, who laughed at everything Hallam said because he liked him, and Piety Mercer laughed with a little sideways glance at Cass as if the joke had been meant for the Margin. Even Kasimir’s mouth moved, though he wrote something in his notebook while it did.

Ilse didn’t laugh. She sat with her pen above the page and felt the heat climb up her neck and into her face, slowly, the way water climbs a wick, and she kept her eyes on the slate and her face perfectly still. *A failure of character.* Agnes Tull’s Pearmain. The Hedleys’ ditch, old Brack’s gatepost, twenty other small corrections across three parishes, every one of them restoring a line that a better-paid surveyor had moved. And under every one of them a dead man’s mark, carrying it. She thought of Quince’s grey hands round the cup.

She wrote, very small, at the bottom of her notes, *7. Transfer.* Then she inked it out until it was a black bar, and turned the page.

* * *

Master Pim Halloway wore three watches. One was on his left wrist, one on his right, and one on a chain across his waistcoat, and he consulted all three at the start of every lecture and frowned at the differences, which he wrote on the board in the top left corner each morning under the heading *Today’s Disagreement.* This morning it was eleven seconds.

“Eleven,” he said. “Eleven s-seconds between the three of them. Which is right?”

Nobody answered. Nobody ever answered. He seemed to like it that way.

“None of them,” said Halloway. “All of them. That is the l-lesson. Sit, sit.”

He was a slight, twitchy man of forty with a high forehead and hair that stood up at the back where he ran his hands through it, and when he was talking about anything but numbers his words came out in a stammer, catching and restarting like a cart on a rutted road. When he came to the numbers the ruts smoothed out and he went along like a canal barge. Ilse had liked him at Benchmarking. She liked him now. That was the trouble.

“Error theory,” he said, and the stammer went away. “Every measurement you will ever make is wrong. The chain stretches. The ground is not flat. The man at the far end is cold and does not pull straight. You will never know the true length of anything. What you can know is how *probably* wrong you are, and in which direction, and by how much. That is not a weakness of the trade. That is the trade.” He chalked five figures on the board, one under another. “A line in the Old Court. Measured five times, by five of last year’s links. Four chains twelve links. Four fifteen. Four eleven. Four fourteen. Four thirteen. What is the length of the line? Miss Marrow.”

She stood. The figures looked up at her from the board. She knew this; she had done this all her life. You chained a line, and chained it back, and if the two disagreed you chained it again, and you took the one that felt right.

“Four chains thirteen,” she said. “Near enough.”

“Good. How did you get it?”

“The fifteen’s wrong. It stands out. Someone let the chain sag. Throw that away, and the others are round about thirteen.”

There was a short silence. Halloway’s face did something kind and pained, as if she had shown him a drawing of a horse with five legs.

“Why is the fifteen wrong?”

“Because—” She looked at it. “It’s the farthest out.”

“The eleven is as far out the other way. Why not throw that away?”

She had no answer. She had thrown away the fifteen because it looked wrong, the way the stream bank on the Walk had looked wrong, and her hand had moved to smooth it before her head had been asked.

“You cannot discard a measurement because you dislike its face, Miss Marrow,” said Halloway, not unkindly. “If you do that, you are not measuring the line. You are measuring your opinion of the line. Come up to the board, please. Show me the residuals.”

She came up to the board. She took the chalk he gave her. She looked at the five figures.

She did not know what a residual was.

She knew how to chain a field in a rainstorm with a drunk man on the other end. She knew how to read a hedge-line through four hundred years of ploughing. She could draw a boundary so true that the ground went to it like a dog to its bed. And she stood at the board in front of fifty-odd first-years with a stick of chalk in her hand and could not have told them what a residual was if they had offered her the Long Field for it.

“The d-difference,” said Halloway gently, “between each measurement and the mean.”

She did that. She could do subtraction. Minus one, plus two, minus two, plus one, nothing. She wrote them in a column. Behind her somebody shifted on a bench.

“Now square them, and sum the squares.”

“Why square them?”

“Because we must,” said Halloway, “for reasons I will explain next week. For now, trust me.”

One, four, four, one, nothing. Ten. She wrote ten.

“Divide by one less than the number of measurements. Take the square root. Multiply by nought point six seven four five.”

She stood with the chalk against the board. Ten divided by four was two and a half. She knew that. The square root of two and a half was—something. One and a half, and a bit. She didn’t know how to find the bit. Quince had never needed to know the bit, and she had never needed to know anything Quince didn’t. She tried, under her breath, and lost the thread of it, and started again, and the room behind her went from quiet to a particular kind of quiet that she remembered from the village school in Wick Fallow, the quiet of thirty children enjoying someone else’s trouble.

From the third bench Piety Mercer said, not loudly, “One point five eight one.”

Halloway didn’t look at Piety. He didn’t take the chalk away from Ilse. He came and stood beside her at the board, a little stooped, his three watches ticking out of time with one another, and when he spoke it was only to her.

“One point five eight one. Times the factor, one point oh seven. That is the probable error of a single measurement, in links. Divide by the root of five for the probable error of the mean, and you have four chains thirteen links, plus or minus nought point four eight.” He wrote it under her column, neatly. “You were right about thirteen, Miss Marrow. You were right for the wrong reason. Your eye is very good. It is better than the arithmetic of most people in this room. But an eye cannot tell you how much to trust it. Only this can.” He tapped the board. “Sit down. You did well to try.”

She sat down. Her face was burning for the second time in a week.

At the end of the lecture, as they filed out, he caught her eye and beckoned, and she went to him, braced.

“Miss Marrow,” he said. “You w-were—you were embarrassed.”

“I didn’t know what a residual was.”

“No. Now you do.” He was winding the watch on his left wrist, carefully, not looking at her. “Error is not s-sin, Miss Marrow. Not knowing is not sin. Throwing away the fifteen because you did not like it—that is the beginning of sin. *Unrecorded* error. That is the only kind that kills anyone.” He snapped the watch-case shut. “Next lecture. Squares. Come early, if you like. I am always here early. The watches insist.”

* * *

The Errata Society met in the Mess kitchen after the last bell, when Cook had gone to bed and the great ranges had been banked down to a red glow, and it was, as far as Ilse could tell, neither a society nor particularly concerned with errata. There were about a dozen of them that night, chains and furlongs and a few links, sitting on the scrubbed tables and the flour bins in the warm dark under hanging copper pans. The chair was a large, mild furlong called Dunstan Moss, who presided with a ladle and banged it on a pan when people talked over one another, which was always.

“Tonight’s motion,” said Moss, “is that a map is a theft. Somebody please argue it, or we’ll just eat.”

“We’ll just eat,” said several people at once.

Teo was already at the end range with one of his spirit-stoves going on the iron top, because he didn’t trust the range, and a slab of pastry on a floured board. He had been told about the motion and had decided it was beyond him. He had decided instead to explain probable error to Ilse, and he had decided to do it with pie.

“Look,” he said. He had the rolling pin in one hand. “You roll the crust. You want it a quarter of an inch, all over, because a quarter of an inch is right, and my grandmother will hit you with a spoon if it isn’t. Now. It’s never a quarter all over. It’s a bit thick here—” he pressed the pastry with his thumb, “—and a bit thin there. Yes? Those are your residuals. How far each bit is from right.”

“I know what a residual is now,” said Ilse.

“I know. I’m only saying. So. Why square them?” He looked at her earnestly over the rolling pin. “Because a crust that’s a bit too thick in two places isn’t half as bad as a crust that’s *very* too thick in one. It’s worse. The very-too-thick bit is raw in the middle. Raw is much worse than a bit chewy. A mistake twice as big isn’t twice as bad, it’s four times as bad, because—well, because it’s raw. So you square it. It makes the big mistakes count for more. It’s fair to the crust.”

Ilse looked at the pastry. It was, she realized, exactly right.

“And one less than the number,” she said. “Why one less?”

“Ah.” Teo set down the rolling pin. “That’s the oven. You’ve made five crusts, yes? And you used one of them to find out how hot the oven is. You burned it a bit, finding out. So you’ve only really got four left that can tell you anything new. You spent one finding the middle.” He considered. “That’s not quite right. But it’s nearly right. Halloway would say it’s within probable error.”

“Teo,” said Ilse, “you’re a genius.”

He went red from his collar to his curls. “I’m not. It’s just crust. In Lisk everyone knows about crust. In Lisk we don’t measure bread, we *understand* it.”

“That’s the most Lisk thing anyone’s ever said,” said Hen, who had come over to see what was on the stove. She leaned over his shoulder to look into the pan, where onions were doing something golden, and her blond head came very close to his ear, and Teo stood absolutely still with the rolling pin in his hand like a man who has been told not to startle a bird.

“What’s that going to be?” said Hen.

“It’s—” Teo swallowed. “It’s going to be a pie. Mutton and onion. With a crust of probable error.”

Hen laughed, her big laugh, right by his ear, and Teo turned the colour of a boiled crab and dropped the rolling pin on his foot.

The kitchen door banged open, and Cass came in backwards out of the dark with a stone jar under each arm and a third hanging from her fingers by its loop, and said, “Hey-ho. Who’s arguing for theft? Because I am.”

“Cass,” said Moss, with feeling, “is that cider?”

“It’s rain,” said Cass. “Very old rain. From an apple. Don’t ask me where from.” She set the jars on the table. A great many people became friendly to her at once. She pulled the cork from one with her teeth, poured it into whatever was nearest, a pudding basin, and passed it along, and swung herself up onto a flour bin with her heels drumming on the side.

“Here’s my argument,” she said. “You lot come out to the Margin with your chains. You walk a bit of heath that’s been nobody’s, that’s been the wayhorns’ and the gorse’s and our wagon’s on the way to winter, and you draw it on a bit of paper and put your name at the bottom, and then it’s *yours*. It’s held. It can’t move. It can’t go anywhere it used to go. Ground didn’t ask to be held. Wayhorns didn’t ask. *We* didn’t ask.” She drank from the basin and passed it on. “That’s theft. You’re taking the moving out of it. You just do it with very nice handwriting.”

“But it’s the moving that kills people,” said a chain from the Salt Steps. “The Lost Wing. The Wandering. If we didn’t hold the ground, it’d—”

“It’d *go*. Yes. It goes for us too. We go with it. That’s what feet are for.” Cass grinned. “I’m not saying don’t hold Tessary. Hold Tessary. Hold the whole Reach, I don’t care, it’s yours. But you keep coming *out*.”

They argued it for an hour, while the cider went round and Teo’s pie came out of the iron oven of the range with its crust risen and golden and very slightly uneven, which he apologized for and everyone ate anyway. Dunstan Moss banged his ladle at intervals. Hen sat next to Teo on the end of the table and ate her slice off his plate as well as her own. And at some point, warm with cider, Ilse found herself saying, “It’s only theft the first time.”

They turned to look at her.

“The first map,” she said. “The one that says *this is mine* when it wasn’t anybody’s. That’s the theft. After that, every map is just a receipt. It says what was taken, and who has it, and how much.” She looked at the cider in the basin. “And if the receipt’s wrong, you can write a better one.”

Cass looked at her for a long moment from the flour bin.

“Huh,” she said.

Moss banged his ladle. “I’m calling it a draw,” he said, “on account of the pie.”

* * *

On the twentieth of Gleaning she sat alone at the table in the Wren Stair sitting room, with the others asleep and Sorry asleep in the coal scuttle and a candle burning down beside her, and did thirty problems from the back of Halloway’s textbook.

She did them slowly. She did them on Hen’s scrap paper, writing every step, every residual, every square, even the ones she could see in her head, because she had learned that seeing it in her head was where it went wrong. She found square roots the way Teo had shown her over the cold end of the pie, guessing and halving the difference and guessing again, a little closer every time, until the figure stood still. She did not throw anything away. When a number looked wrong she wrote it down anyway and worked it through, and twice it turned out to be wrong, and once it turned out to be the only right one.

At two in the morning she checked her answers against the back of the book.

Twenty-six.

She sat and looked at the page for a while. Four wrong. She went back to each of the four and found where she had gone off, a slipped figure in one, a square root she had stopped too soon in another, and she wrote beside each one in small square letters exactly what the mistake had been and where. It took twenty minutes. It was the most satisfying twenty minutes she had spent since she came to the College.

Then she pinched out the candle and went to bed, and lay in the dark with her hands behind her head, listening to the wardrobe ease itself another hair out from the wall, and thought, without quite meaning to, *nought point four eight*, and then, *plus or minus*, and fell asleep before she could get any further.
