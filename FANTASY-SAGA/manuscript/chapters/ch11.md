# Chapter 11
## The Ladder

The Ladder was a tower at the far end of the Third Range, square and windowless and older than the rooms around it, and it was called the Ladder because the only way up it was one. An iron ladder ran up a shaft in its middle, bolted to the stone, and off the shaft at every landing opened a single door. Behind each door was a room. There were six of them, one above another, and they were called the rungs. They were the least held rooms in the College, apart from the ones nobody could find.

“Rung Five,” said Lisbet Arnaud, reading from her list at the foot of the shaft. The senior proctor had a voice like a well-oiled lock. “Marrow, Anselm, Tenter, Solberg. Turn out your pockets.”

They turned out their pockets. She took Teo’s tinderbox, which he had forgotten was there, and Hen’s little brass pocket-lens, and a stub of candle from Cass that Cass claimed was for luck. She ran her hands down their coats. She did not touch Cass’s hair, where among the black braids a dozen knotted route-cords were tied off in their usual places, red and brown and undyed, and Ilse, who had asked Cass the night before to wear every cord she owned, was careful not to look at them.

That morning, like every link in the College, Ilse had gone down to the Crypt of Benchmarks and put a penny into the grey stone palm of a surveyor who had stilled two centuries ago, a woman with her chin lifted and her eyes on the vault. She had felt foolish doing it. She had done it anyway.

Master Halloway stood at the foot of the ladder with all three watches out in his hands at once, like a man dealing cards.

“The rules,” he said, and stammered on none of them, because they were numbers. “You will have one chain, one folding rule, one plumb-line, one sounding-mallet, a board with paper pinned, a box of pins, pen, ink and chalk. You will have two hours exactly, from the closing of the door. You will produce an attested survey of the room and its contents. You will have no light. None. The room will not hold still for you. It has a latitude of something above a rod, and it uses it.” He looked up from the watches. “You will f-find it very dark.”

Kasimir’s party had gone up first, to Rung Two. Ilse had watched him climb the ladder with the chain over his shoulder and not look back.

The door of Rung Five was oak, a hand thick, and lined on the inside with black felt. The room beyond it, in the light from the shaft, was bare stone: a floor, four walls, a low ceiling, and a squat stone pillar standing a little off the middle like a man who has forgotten why he came in. Then the door swung shut behind them and the bar dropped on the outside with a sound like a coffin lid, and there was nothing at all.

* * *

She had thought she knew dark. She had worked in the Fallowmarch on moonless nights with the lantern shuttered to a slot. That was not dark. This was dark the way deep water is wet. It pressed on her eyes. After a few seconds her eyes, offended, began making their own light: slow green blooms, a drifting purple wheel, pricks like sparks from a struck flint. None of it was real. She shut her eyes against it, which made no difference, and found that with them shut she could at least stop trying.

The level was louder in the dark. It hummed in her ears like a hive, low and uneven, and under her feet she could feel the floor not quite still: a faint slow uncertainty, as though she stood on the deck of a ship moored in a harbour.

“Hen,” she said. “Count.”

“One,” said Hen, at once, and then went on under her breath. Ilse had asked her two days ago whether she could count seconds for two hours without losing her place, and Hen had looked at her as though she had asked whether she could breathe. *I counted the brine-pans for my da from the age of six. You lose your count on a pan, it crusts, you lose the pan.* Her voice in the dark was a small steady murmur, like a stream heard through a wall.

“Right,” said Ilse. “Teo. Walls. Cass, floor. I want corners first.”

They had planned it in the Wren Stair over three nights and a great deal of sausage, and it went at first exactly as planned, which frightened her. Teo found the wall by walking into it, apologized to it, and began working along it with the sounding-mallet, knocking at every pace and listening. He had been doing this since Soundings began and was better at it than anyone in the year. Ilse heard the knock change: dull, dull, dull, and then a hollow note like a knuckle on a cask.

“There’s a niche,” said Teo. “Here. About a yard wide, two feet deep. A hearth, maybe, bricked at the back. And further on, here—” knock, knock, “—that’s a doorway that’s been filled in. Different stone. It rings higher. Thinner.” He went on. “Corner.”

“Mark it. Chalk. A.”

They chalked a cross at the foot of each corner, by feel, and Teo called them as he found them, A at the corner nearest the door, then B, C, D round the room. Cass meanwhile had gone down on her hands and knees and was crawling slowly across the floor with her palms flat on the stone, like someone feeling for a dropped ring.

“Ground’s leaning,” she said. “Toward D. Not much. It’s—” She was silent a moment. “It’s uneasy. Like a horse before weather. Don’t like it.”

“Count?”

“Four hundred and eighty,” said Hen.

“Pillar,” said Cass, a little later, and then, “Ow,” and then, “Found the pillar.”

They chained it. It was the oldest kind of survey there was, and the only kind possible in the dark, and Ilse had known it was what they would do from the moment Halloway said *no light*. You couldn’t sight an angle without light. But you didn’t need angles. You needed lengths. Three sides fixed a triangle; a triangle could only be one shape. Chain every side of the room, and both of its diagonals as well, and the room had nowhere to hide.

So Ilse held the end of the chain in the crack of corner A and Teo walked it out along the wall to B, and when he had it taut he called, and Ilse ran her fingers along the links from her end, counting the brass tallies by touch: one point at ten links, two at twenty, three at thirty, four at forty, the round tag at fifty. Then two links past it, and the handle.

“Fifty-two,” she said. “AB, fifty-two links.”

“Count fifteen hundred and twelve,” said Hen.

Hen called the count every time a length was taken, and Ilse said the length and the count back to her, and Hen kept both. That had been Hen’s idea. *If you’re going to forget something,* she had said, *forget it with me.* AB fifty-two. BC thirty-eight. CD fifty-two. DA thirty-eight. Then the long diagonal, B to D across the room, with Teo stumbling round the pillar in the dark: sixty-six links. Then the pillar tied from A and from B. Each number went into Hen and stayed.

Ilse knelt at the board with the cords.

Cass had untied them from her braids with bad grace the night before, one at a time. *This one’s the road to Corrow, and you don’t cut it, paper-wall. You don’t even look at it rude.* Ilse didn’t cut it. She tied knots in it. With the folding rule, which had a brass stud set into its edge at every inch so that you could read it with a thumbnail, she measured off cords to scale, one inch to five links. AB, ten and two-fifths inches. AD, seven and three-fifths. BD, thirteen and a fifth. Each length she marked with a knot pulled tight, and checked against the studs, and checked again.

Then she pinned A to the board, and B ten and two-fifths inches from it along the bottom edge, and took the AD cord from A and the BD cord from B and drew them both out taut across the paper until the two knots met. Where they met was D. It could be nowhere else. She pushed a pin through both knots into the board, and felt the triangle under her fingers, three cords drawn tight between three pins, a shape that could not lie.

“That’s pretty,” said Cass, who had crawled over to feel it. Her fingers moved lightly over the cords. “That’s nearly a song.”

“It’s a triangle.”

“Song’s just a triangle that goes somewhere.”

C from B and D, the same way. Then the check line: A to C, the second diagonal, which the figure on the board said ought to be sixty-two links and three-quarters if the room was what they had measured. Teo and Ilse chained it.

“Sixty-three,” said Ilse.

“Count two thousand six hundred and ten.”

It closed. Within a quarter link, it closed. Ilse sat back on her heels in the dark, and felt the tight bright pleasure of it go through her: a room she could not see, laid out under her hands in pins and cords, every side agreeing with every other. Forty-three minutes. They had more than an hour left. They could tie in the niche and the old doorway at leisure, prick it through, ink it, and sign with half an hour to spare.

The level dropped out of her ears like a stone down a well.

* * *

She felt it a moment before it happened. Then the room groaned.

It was a long, low grinding sound that came from everywhere and from the floor most of all, stone moving against stone, and the floor under her knees slid. Not much. A few inches, sideways, smoothly, the way a rug slides on a polished floor when someone treads on its edge. Teo shouted. Something clattered: the sounding-mallet, dropped. The plumb-line Cass had hung from a pin at corner A began to swing; Ilse heard the bob tap the wall and tap it again.

Then it stopped. The level came back, thinner than before, a high uneasy whine.

“Everyone,” said Ilse. “Say something.”

“Here,” said Hen, and then, without a break, “three thousand and forty-one.”

“Here,” said Teo. “Sorry. I dropped the mallet. I’m sorry.”

“Grain’s turned,” said Cass, sharply. Ilse heard her palms slap the floor. “It was leaning to D. Now it’s leaning to C. Whole room’s gone round, paper-wall.”

“Chain BD,” said Ilse. “Now.”

They chained it. Her fingers ran along the links: four points, the round tag, then four points again, which from that end meant sixty, and on past it, counting.

“Sixty-nine,” she said. Her voice came out level. That surprised her. “It was sixty-six.”

“AB?”

They chained AB. Fifty-two. BC: thirty-eight. The sides were the same. The sides were exactly the same, and the long diagonal had grown three links.

Ilse knelt in the dark with the chain in her hands and understood. The room hadn’t moved. It had racked: leaned, the way a bookcase leans when you push its top, every shelf still the same length, but the whole thing slewed out of square into a lozenge. AB stood where it had stood. The far wall had slid along it. Their figure on the board was a room that no longer existed.

“Chain it again,” she said.

They waited. Hen counted. Ilse could hear Teo breathing, quick and shallow, the breath of a man trying hard not to think about the ground. At three thousand four hundred they chained BD again. Sixty-nine. At four thousand, again.

“Seventy.”

“It’s still going,” said Teo. His voice cracked. “Marrow. It’s still going.”

“I know.”

“We have to measure it all again, fast, all of it, before it—”

“It’ll move while we do. It’ll move while we pin it. It’ll move while we sign.” Ilse heard her own voice saying it and was almost calm. “Every number we take is already wrong by the time we write it down.”

Nobody said anything. Hen counted.

She knew what she could do. She had known it from the moment the floor slid. She knew it the way her hand knew the wren’s foot. Take the figure on the board, the clean one, the rectangle that closed, and ink it, and sign it, and lean on it. Lean the room back into square. The room had been square, nearly, when they came in; that was well within its latitude. A good confident charter, beautifully drawn, and a hard push behind the pen, and the room would grind back to the shape she said it was, and hold. It would hold beautifully. It would be a lie, and it would hold, and nobody would ever know but her.

It would be the stream bank again. She had made the bank on her Walk survey agree with itself because it would not agree with the ground, and it had felt so right, so clean, that her hand had done it before she had decided. She felt that same rightness now, waiting at the end of her fingers.

*You cannot discard a measurement because you dislike its face, Miss Marrow.*

Seventy at four thousand. Sixty-nine at three thousand four hundred. Sixty-nine at three thousand and forty-one, just after the groan.

She sat very still in the dark.

“Hen,” she said. “When we chain BD again, what count will it be?”

“Four thousand six hundred, if you keep to it.”

“Teo. If it’s seventy-one at four thousand six hundred—”

“Then it’s a link every ten minutes,” said Teo. And then, slowly, as though he were hearing it as he said it: “Oh. Oh, Marrow.”

At four thousand six hundred they chained BD.

“Seventy-one.”

“A link every six hundred counts,” said Teo. The tremble had gone out of his voice. It had the far-off sound it took on when he was doing long division to steady himself. “Steady as a clock. It’s not lurching any more, it’s creeping. If it’s creeping steady—”

“Then we don’t need to beat it,” said Ilse. “We don’t need to measure it all again. The sides don’t change. Only the lean changes, and we know how fast. We pick a count. The count we sign at. We work out what the room will be at that count, exactly, and we draw *that*. And then we write on the sheet how fast it’s going.”

“That’s not allowed,” said Hen, and then, “Is that allowed? Six thousand and two.”

“Nobody said it wasn’t.”

“Ground’ll like it,” said Cass, out of the dark. She sounded surprised. “You’re not telling it to stop. You’re telling it what it’s doing. Ground likes being told what it’s doing.”

There was something in that, something that brushed past Ilse’s mind like a moth in a dark room and was gone before she could see its shape. There wasn’t time to go after it.

“Teo. If it’s racking, the other diagonal shortens. Can you say how much?”

“The squares of the two diagonals,” said Teo, “add up to twice the squares of the sides. Always. In a lozenge. It’s in Euclid, or it’s in a pie, I forget which.” A pause. “Twice fifty-two squared and thirty-eight squared is eight thousand two hundred and ninety-six. So at seventy-one, AC wants to be fifty-seven and a bit. By four thousand eight hundred it’ll have crept. Fifty-six and a half, about.”

They chained AC at four thousand eight hundred. Fifty-six links and a half, near as the tallies would tell.

“That’s within probable error,” said Teo, and laughed, a little wildly, and then stopped.

* * *

It went fast after that, because it had to. They chose to sign at the count of six thousand nine hundred, five minutes before the bar would lift. Teo worked it out in his head with his eyes shut, though there was no need to shut them: at six thousand nine hundred BD would be seventy-four links and eight-tenths, and AC fifty-two near enough exactly. They tied the pillar again from A and B, twice, and Hen gave the count each time, and Teo carried the lean forward to the signing count. Ilse retied the cords. She had to shorten the AC cord and lengthen the BD, and pin the figure afresh. Her fingers were stiff with cold and she dropped a pin and found it again by touch in the crack between two flags. Her hands did not shake. She noticed that from a long way off.

When the new figure was pinned she laid a sheet of paper over the board, smoothed it with the flat of her hand, and pressed down until she felt the pinheads through the paper like the knuckles of a hand under a sheet. Then she took a fresh pin and pricked through the paper beside each one, a hole at every corner, every tie, both edges of the niche, both sides of the filled door. It was how Quince had taught her to copy a map she could not take away: prick it through, and join the holes. She took the pins out. She found each pricked hole with her left fingertip, laid the edge of the folding rule against it and the next, and drew the line between them with her right, slow, the pen held light so it would not catch.

Then the words. She pinned a cord taut across the bottom of the sheet to keep her letters level, and wrote, large and square and slow, one letter at a time:

*At count of attestation. BD 74 links 8. AC 52. The figure racks about AB. BD lengthens one link in each ten minutes. Recorded.*

“Six thousand six hundred,” said Hen.

“Last check. BD.”

They chained it. Ilse ran her fingers along the links. Seventy-four, and a third of the next, near as she could feel.

“It’s on time,” said Teo softly. “It’s on time.”

They knelt round the board in the dark, the four of them, close enough that Ilse could feel the warmth of Teo’s shoulder on one side and Cass’s knee against hers on the other, and Hen counted down from six thousand eight hundred and ninety in a voice that never once quickened. At *six thousand nine hundred* they signed: Hen’s mark, Teo’s, Cass’s, and Ilse last, her wren’s foot found by touch on the bottom corner, three strokes and a spur.

The charter took. She felt it go into the room like a key into a lock, and the faint creep under her knees, which she had stopped noticing, stopped. The level steadied to a single held note. Nothing groaned. Nothing slid.

“Seven thousand,” said Hen, after a while. And then, “Can I stop counting now?”

* * *

When the bar lifted and the door swung in, the light from the shaft was so bright that they all cried out and covered their faces. Then Halloway was in the doorway with a lamp, and behind him Lisbet Arnaud with another, and Ilse saw Rung Five for the first time since they had come in.

It was not a square room any longer. It leaned. The far wall stood slewed along the near one, so that every corner was a little wrong, two sharp and two blunt, and the pillar stood off-centre in a way that made the eye ache, as if the whole room had been drawn on a sheet of paper and someone had pushed the top of the sheet sideways. In the middle of the floor lay the board with its pins and cords, a cat’s cradle of knotted red and brown string stretched between brass heads. Beside it lay the charter, the lines a little wavering but every one of them joined, and the black words straggling across the bottom.

Halloway lifted the sheet and read it by the lamp. He read it twice. He took out his chain, and measured BD himself, and then AC, and wrote the figures on his cuff. Then he looked at the words at the bottom again.

“*Recorded*,” he said.

Nobody spoke.

“You w-wrote,” said Halloway, “you wrote down how fast you were wrong.” His face had gone pink. He looked at Ilse and at Teo and at Hen, and at Cass, and at the route-cords on the board, and back at the sheet. “In eleven years of setting the Ladder no one has ever written down the *rate*. They fight it. They chain like madmen and sign in a panic and half the time the charter tears. You asked it how f-fast it was going. And then you believed the answer.” He held the sheet as Hallam had held the charter of the Ammonite Hills. “This is error theory. This is the whole of error theory. Oh, *well* done.”

* * *

The results went up on the board in the Hall of Instruments at six. Rung Two, Reyne, Devane, Mercer, Grice: held to a fifth of a link, first. Rung Five, Marrow, Anselm, Tenter, Solberg: held to two-fifths, second, *with commendation for the rate annotation*. Under that, in Halloway’s hand: *Best result by a Walker party since 1189.*

Kasimir was standing in front of the board when she came in. He read it, and then he turned and saw her. They were alone for a moment in the crowd, the way two people sometimes are.

“Your room racked,” he said.

“Yours didn’t?”

“Mine racked. I chained the whole of it in forty minutes, and again in the last twenty, and signed the second set.” He looked at the board. “I fought it. You recorded it.” He seemed about to say something more, and then didn’t. “Interesting,” he said, and went away.

* * *

The Chain & Anchor in Verity Cross was full to the doors that night, as it always was the night of the Ladder, with first-years who had passed and first-years who had failed and furlongs who had come to watch both. There was a fiddler, and a man with a squeezebox who could play only three tunes but played them with great conviction, and the lanterns along the beams swung a little in the heat and smoke so that the light went to and fro across the faces like light on water.

Teo danced with Hen. He had said he could not dance, and it was true, and Hen didn’t care. She took both his hands and pulled him out among the tables, and he went red and stepped on her twice and said sorry both times, and then forgot to be sorry and swung her round so that her feet left the floor and her big laugh rang out over the fiddle. Cass danced with a Lisk furlong, then with Orla Devane, then with a fisherman from the harbour whom nobody knew and who turned out to dance better than anyone in the room, and came back to the table flushed and grinning with her braids half-undone and three of her route-cords missing, which she said she would go back for in the morning.

Ilse didn’t dance. She sat at the end of the bench with her back to the wall and a cup of cider she wasn’t drinking. She watched Teo’s face when Hen laughed, and Cass flicking a bottle-cap at the fisherman, and the lantern-light going to and fro. Her hands were still sore at the fingertips from the pins. Her ears were full of fiddle and the noise of a hundred voices, and under it, faint and steady, the level of the held Reach.

She was happy. She noticed it the way you notice you have walked out onto ice. She had not meant to be. She had come to the College for a shield and a Patent, and she had a party now, and a room that moved and a cat that wasn’t a cat, and three people who would kneel round a board with her in the dark and trust her count. She had something to lose here. She had not had anything to lose since she was twelve.

Hen came back to the table, out of breath, and dropped onto the bench beside her.

“You’re not dancing.”

“I’m watching.”

“Tide’s out on watching,” said Hen, and took her by both wrists, and pulled.
