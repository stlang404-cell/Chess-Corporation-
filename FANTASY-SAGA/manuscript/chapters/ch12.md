# Chapter 12
## Watermarks

The Register hummed.

A vault cut seven levels down into a granite headland ought by rights to be as quiet as the inside of a stone. But every box on every shelf was a place, and every place was held, and every held place pressed very faintly against the back of Ilse’s skull. Walking the aisles of Level Two was like walking through a choir whose members were all humming different notes under their breath and pretending not to.

The charters were shelved by parish, in boxes of grey board with brass corners. The Ammonite boxes had a low, thick note, like a cellar door swinging shut. The Lisk boxes buzzed, which seemed fair. The Fallowmarch boxes stood along the far wall and sounded thinner and higher, a reed note. She had found Wick Fallow on her first evening and stood in front of it longer than she’d meant to, not touching it.

She was there to copy. It was the dullest work in the College, and she loved it with a passion she kept to herself. Old gall-ink browned and bit into the paper, and the reading-room clerks wanted fair copies a magistrate could read without a glass. A copy held nothing. Under the Law of Witness you could copy the entire Reach and not move a pebble, because you had not walked it. So the Register let a first-year link with a good hand sit at a long oak table two evenings a week, for four marks a month, and reproduce the oldest promises in the world with a steel nib.

Tonight it was Bellwether, a hill parish in the Ammonites, chartered in 811. She set the proportional dividers to one-to-one, walked them from the original under its glass to the fresh folio and back, and pricked the corners of each field so lightly that the holes would vanish under the ink. The hedges went in with a hard pencil first, and only when everything sat where it should did she ink. Quince had made her copy his maps forty times before he let her forge one. *You can’t lie well,* he’d said, *until you can tell the truth exactly.*

“Still at it, my dear?”

She hadn’t heard him on the stair. Master Hallam came down the aisle between Crane Abbas and Cullingford with a tray in his gloved hands: a brown teapot, two cups and a saucer of ginger biscuits. He set it down with the care of a man laying down a sleeping child.

“I thought you might be cold,” he said. “She keeps a chill down here.” He leaned over her copy, and his half-moon spectacles slid down his nose, and he pushed them back with one grey-gloved knuckle. “Bellwether. Oswy Tarrant’s hand. He drank, poor man. You can see it in the long strokes. Look how the hedges lean on the right side of the sheet; that’s Friday.”

Ilse looked. They did lean. She had copied the lean without thinking.

“You’ve kept it,” Hallam said, pleased. “Most copyists straighten him. They think they’re doing him a kindness.” He poured her tea and set the cup by her right hand, well clear of the folio. “You hold your pen like someone who was taught by a craftsman, Miss Marrow, not by a schoolroom.”

Her nib stopped. She made it start again.

“My father was a farmer,” she said.

“Then a farmer taught you well.” He didn’t press. He never did; it was the thing she liked best about him. He took a biscuit and ate half of it. “Don’t stay past the ninth bell. Bellwether has been there since 811. She’ll keep another night.”

He went up the stair slowly, his gloved hand on the rail, like a man who has once fallen and never quite forgiven the stairs. Ilse sat with both hands around the cup, warming the ache out of her knuckles and feeling, absurdly, looked after.

* * *

Merrin Coyle came down an hour later with an armful of paper that reached to his chin.

“Miss Marrow, I’m so sorry, would you mind awfully —” He lowered the stack onto the table, apologised to the table, and straightened his cuffs, which did no good; they had been ink-stained so long that the stain had become the cuff. “It’s the year-store. The Master likes every Morning Survey folio kept, every one, and Level One is full to the ceiling, and they have to be bundled by year and boxed, and you have such neat hands.”

He said it as if neat hands were a medical condition. Ilse took pity on him.

The bundles were Morning Survey sheets, kept for no reason she could see except that the Register had never in its life thrown anything away: each party’s stretch for each morning, drawn in a hurry by cold students in the dark. They smelled of damp wool and lamp oil. Ilse squared them, tied them with linen tape, and wrote the year on each label in Coyle’s copperplate, which was not hard to imitate. It was mindless work, until her hands did the thing they always did with paper and lifted a sheet to the lamp.

Quince had taught her that too. *Know your paper before your paper knows you.* So, without thinking, she held a sheet from Candlemonth 1200 up to the flame.

The light came through the linen-rag in a warm, uneven glow. The chain lines ran down it an inch and a quarter apart, and in the lower corner stood a wheat-sheaf with a ribbon round its middle and the letters D & S beneath: Dunmore and Sons of Tessary, who had made the College’s paper for as long as the College had made maps.

She picked up the next bundle, Thaw 1201, and held a sheet to the flame out of nothing more than the rhythm of the thing.

The wheat-sheaf was gone. In its place a bridge stood in the corner of the sheet, a little bridge of three arches over a wavy line of water, with no letters at all. The chain lines were closer, an inch apart or a hair under. The sheet was heavier in the hand and harder-sized, so that it clicked when she flexed it instead of sighing.

Seedtime 1201: bridge. Highsun 1202: bridge. The last week of Candlemonth 1200: wheat-sheaf. The year had turned, and the paper had turned with it.

“Master Coyle,” she said, “the paper changed.”

Coyle looked up as if she had told him the stair was on fire, then saw the sheet and the lamp, and his shoulders came down half an inch.

“Oh — yes. The Master changed the supplier.” He blotted a figure. “Cheaper. From Lisk. The Bridge Mill, on the Ambry. Dunmore’s had got very dear, and the Survey goes through two hundred sheets a morning.” He laughed apologetically, as if the economy had been his own idea. “You’d think the Register could afford Dunmore’s. But he’s very careful with the College’s money.”

She tied the bundle and put it in its box. It was a detail, the kind a clerk changes on a Tuesday to save four marks a quarter. She put it in the long untidy cupboard at the back of her mind where Quince had taught her to keep everything she couldn’t yet use, and left a little before the ninth bell, because the Master had asked her to.

* * *

The way home ran out through the Old Court, under the Lantern Gate with its single flame burning on the keystone, and down the covered causeway into the Drift Wing, whose corridors stretched a little every night, like a cat in its sleep. Ilse counted her paces home out of habit. From the foot of the Second Range stair to the Wren Stair door was eighty-eight paces by daylight. Tonight it was ninety-one, which was about normal for a damp evening in Fogmonth.

At pace sixty she stopped, because there was a door in the wall.

It stood between the third lamp and the fourth, where for five weeks there had been nothing but whitewashed plaster and a damp stain shaped very like the city of Lisk: oak gone black with age, iron studs, a ring handle the size of a saucer, and at eye height a brass plate, green at the edges:

FAR RANGE — CLOSED BY ORDER 1105

Under that, in a much later hand, someone had scratched with the point of a knife: *and stay closed.*

Sorry was sitting in front of it.

It must have come looking for her, as it sometimes did when the room got too quiet. It sat bolt upright on the flagstones with its too-big ears pricked forward and its tail wrapped round its feet, perfectly still, the way it watched moths. Its amber eyes didn’t move when she came up beside it.

The door hummed wrong. Everything in the College had a note: the deep, settled note of the Old Court, the nervous, wavering note of the Wing. This door had no note at all. It *swayed*. Standing in front of it was like standing on a jetty and feeling the planks lift on a swell you couldn’t see. She put her hand out to the iron ring.

“Oh, don’t — oh, please don’t, not with your bare hand.”

She snatched her hand back. A tiny old man was hurrying down the corridor, so bent that he seemed to be arriving mostly by willpower. He wore a scholar’s gown that had once been black, and a pair of round lenses on hinged brass arms was clamped to his head like the antennae of some studious insect. One lens was swung down over his left eye, which made that eye enormous. He smelled powerfully of vinegar.

“If you open it and it decides to go somewhere else while you’re halfway through, there’s no telling which half of you goes with it,” he said, arriving. “Archivist Damask. Palimpsest. You’re the Walker who copies for Aurek.” He beamed at the door as if it were a grandchild come to visit. “Isn’t it wonderful? It hasn’t been in the Second Range for eleven years.”

“What is it?”

“The Unheld Stacks. The Far Range library. The oldest books the College owns that aren’t in the Register, and a great many it doesn’t know it owns at all.” He swung the lens up and down, peering. “In 1105 the Provost and the Master of Works quarrelled about the connecting gallery. They pulled the old one down and argued for six years about the new one, and by the time they’d finished, nobody had re-surveyed the way in. Nobody has since. Ninety-eight years. It comes and goes like a cat.” He chuckled. “It’s been in the kitchens. Once — this is my favourite — it was behind the mirror in the Provost’s own dressing room for a week, and the poor man had to shave by feel.”

Sorry still hadn’t moved. Its ears were so far forward that they almost met.

“Why hasn’t anyone surveyed it?” Ilse said.

“Because it isn’t *here*, my dear, not in the way you mean. Survey it tonight and you’ve charted a door to a place that tomorrow is under the kitchens. You’d be attesting a falsehood by breakfast.” He tilted his huge eye at her. “What did you notice? Before I shouted. You put your hand out like someone who’d noticed something.”

She thought about lying, and couldn’t think of a reason to.

“It doesn’t sit,” she said. “Everything else sits. Even the Wing sits, a bit, until morning. This sways.”

The little man made a noise like a kettle starting.

“Oh! Look, *look*,” he said, to the corridor. “She feels the *grain* of it.” He clutched her sleeve in a hand that was all knuckle. “Do you know how many of my seniors can feel that? Two, and one of them only on Thursdays. Links don’t start Palimpsest until the winter term, I know, but come on a Thursday to my seniors and sit at the back and look at things. The fourth bell. Bring nothing. I have far too much of everything.”

“I — yes. All right.”

When she looked back, the door was gone. There was only plaster and the damp stain, and Sorry sniffing along the skirting board with its ears flat and offended, like someone who has been promised a pudding and handed a plate.

* * *

The letter came on Fogmonth 4, by the noon post, in her mother’s square, laundry-ticket hand.

Ilse took it up to the Wren Stair to read. The party’s rooms were empty for once. Teo was at Soundings, Cass was somewhere being rude to someone, and Hen had gone to the Mess to argue about a sum. The wardrobe had drifted a foot and a half from the wall overnight and stood in the middle of the floor like a guest who doesn’t know where to sit. Ilse took the window seat.

*Ilse,* her mother had written, *Your letter came. You did not say if you are eating. I expect you are not.*

*Tam Brewin’s mare is mended. The lane is still a bog and the parish has petitioned the Hundred and the Hundred has petitioned somebody else. Agnes Tull asks after you every Thursday at market and has sent a jar of her damson, which I have kept, as it would not survive the coach and neither would you if you ate it.*

*Now. Corwin Fask has been round the parish asking about the Tull hedge. He came to the laundry. He was very civil, which I did not care for. Jem at the vestry says an inquiry is coming from the Big College, he says, whatever that is when it is at home. He says the young gentleman in the good boots wrote for the papers to be sent on. I said I knew nothing about anything, which is true, as nobody ever tells me.*

*Your Long Field money. I hope it bought you something.*

*Write, you wretch.*

*Your mother, H. Marrow*

Ilse read it through once, quickly, the way she’d read a magistrate’s notice. Then she read *I hope it bought you something* a second time and a third, and folded the letter in half and in half again and sat with it pressed flat between her palms.

Thirty-eight marks for a seat on the roof of the express mail coach, and six marks and six left in her pocket at Verity Cross. That was the field: the well, the sixty yards of good black frontage, her father at the window with the lamp. She had paid for her own neck with it, and she would do it again; she was not a fool. But her mother had not written *I hope it was worth it*. Her mother had written *I hope it bought you something*, the way you’d say it about a dress, and Ilse found she could not breathe properly around the difference.

And Kasimir Reyne had written for the papers. When they came, the Board would hold the Tull charter to a damp lens, and under the wren’s foot now cut into the Old Wall in her own hand, they would find the mark of a man three years dead.

The door banged. Hen came in with her cheeks pink from the wind and her blond hair coming out of its pins, saying, “He was *wrong*, he was wrong to the third place and he knew it, and he had the face to say rounding —” and then she saw Ilse and stopped.

Ilse waited to be asked. Hen didn’t ask. She came across the room, kicked off her boots, climbed onto the window seat beside Ilse and pushed her stockinged feet, which were very cold, under Ilse’s leg.

“Mothers,” Hen said.

“How do you know it’s my mother?”

“Because you’re holding it like a summons and you’d rather it was one.” Hen tipped her head back against the window. “Mine writes to tell me the salt’s fine, and then three pages about what the salt’s doing. The salt is very white this year, Henrike, whiter than the Pellows’ salt, which your father says is grey as a curate.” She wriggled her toes. “Tell me what yours says and I’ll tell you if the tide’s in or out on it.”

“She says I’m not eating.”

“Tide’s in. You’re not.”

“She sent damson jam and kept it.”

“That’s love, that is. On the Steps that’s practically a proposal.”

Ilse laughed. She hadn’t meant to. And Hen laughed too, her proper laugh, which went up all at once like a gull off a pan-wall, loud and sudden and entirely without dignity, so that two first-years passing on the stair outside stopped to see who was being murdered. Sorry came out from under the wardrobe at the noise and climbed onto the window seat, where, before either of them could stop it, it put its nose to the folded letter and licked the word *wretch* clean off the paper.

“Oh, you *animal*,” Hen said, delighted. “That was the best bit.”

“It wasn’t,” Ilse said, and unfolded the letter, and looked at the pale smear where her mother’s word had been, and at the line above it, which Sorry had left alone. She folded it again and put it inside her shirt.

* * *

Brask held the Fieldwork class that week at night.

“Wayhorns,” she said, at the door of the shed, with the Long Lie in her right hand and her grey left hand hooked into her belt. “The herds are coming down the Margin. You’ll lie in the gorse and you’ll keep your mouths shut and you’ll take a bearing off three animals. Anyone who frightens them walks home in front of them.”

There were fourteen of them that night, Piety Mercer among them, looking as if she’d been told this was a lecture. They went down onto the heath in a ragged line, each with a sightvane, a shuttered lantern and a plane-table board slung across the back. The moon was a day off full, and the fog had gone back out to sea and left the whole of the Margin silvered and dripping.

Brask put them down in a fold of gorse above a shallow valley and lay down herself, full length, the Long Lie beside her like a rifle. Ilse lay next to Hen. Teo lay on Ilse’s other side and immediately began to whisper.

“I’m sorry, I just — are they big? I know they’re big, my uncle sells the velvet for gloves, but are they the kind of big where if one stood on you —”

“Anselm,” said Brask, without turning her head.

“Ma’am.”

“Count something.”

Teo got to four hundred and twelve under his breath, and then the herd came over the shoulder of the valley, and he stopped.

There must have been eighty of them. They came down out of the dark at a slow walk, heads low, grey-brown, deer-shaped but heavier in the shoulder, and their antlers caught the moon. Every antler was the same pale silver and every antler branched the same way. The tines didn’t spread evenly like a stag’s; they all leaned toward the north and the east, so that the herd moved under a forest of silver hooks bent toward the same invisible thing, like iron filings walking. When a young buck turned his head to crop at the heather, his antlers stayed leaning the same way, and Ilse could see the stiff effort in his neck to keep them there.

“High hold,” Brask breathed. “They grow toward it. Every season, a tine at a time. Best compass on the Margin. Three animals. Bearings. Go.”

Ilse put the sightvane to her eye and laid its edge along the leading tine of the big doe at the front of the herd. She read the bearing off the plane-table rose by the slot of the lantern: north sixty-one east. Then a young buck halfway down the herd: north fifty-four east. Then an old animal at the back with one antler broken off at the burr: north forty-eight east. She pencilled three lines across her board, each from the place she had paced the animal to be standing. They met in a small, untidy triangle about eleven chains off to the northeast.

“Mine cross in a triangle you could cover with a farthing,” Hen whispered. “Look at *that*.”

“You paced them. I guessed mine.”

“You never guess.” Hen made a noise of pure satisfaction into the heather.

Cass hadn’t touched her sightvane. She lay with her chin on her forearms, watching the herd with something very like tenderness.

“They’re pointing at the old stone in the hollow,” Cass murmured. “Past that gorse. Could have told you that from the shed.”

“Then tell me with numbers, Tenter,” said Brask, “or it’s gossip.”

Cass sighed through her teeth and picked up the sightvane.

When the herd had gone on down the valley, with the sound of eighty animals walking through wet heather, which is the sound of a very large, very soft rain, Brask made them chain it out: eleven chains and forty links through gorse up to the waist, Teo apologising every time he snagged the chain. At the end of it, on a flat grey rock among the heather, they found a cairn no taller than Ilse’s hand: eleven small stones piled with fussy precision, each one set on the one below so that it neither rocked nor wobbled. A white feather stuck out of the top.

“Cairn-crows,” said Brask. “They build where the ground’s surest, and they’ve never been wrong. Note the lodestone.”

Ilse held the compass flat on her palm. It pointed with great confidence twelve degrees east of where the wayhorns had said, and then wandered off, as if it had thought better of the whole business.

Teo, standing on a rock in the middle of the Margin at midnight, raised his hand as if they were in a lecture hall.

“I’m sorry, ma’am, I just — the Drift. Out there. Past the Margin.” He pointed west into the dark, where the heath ran down to nothing and the horizon couldn’t quite decide where to sit. “Is it trying to kill us? As a — as a general policy?”

Piety Mercer snorted. Brask didn’t. She looked west with her one eye for long enough that Ilse looked too: dark country, a long low line that might have been hills, and above it the stars, the only things in that direction that kept still.

“It’s not trying to kill you,” Brask said. “It isn’t trying anything. That’s the frightening part.” She hefted the Long Lie. “And it’s also a lie, but you’re not ready for that one.”

She set off back toward the College without waiting to see if they followed. They followed. Behind them the cairn sat on its rock with its white feather lifting in the wind. Far down the valley, the herd had stopped to graze, and as Ilse looked back, every silver head in it lifted at once, every tine leaning the same way, toward the little cairn and the lit windows of the Meridian beyond it, as if they had heard someone there call their name.
