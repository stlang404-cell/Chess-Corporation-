# Chapter 13
## Brine End

“No copying for you this week,” Hen said, sliding onto the bench with a bowl of porridge and a notice she had plainly torn off a door. “Coyle’s posted it on the Antechamber. *The Master of the Register is absent on inspection in the Salt Steps until Fogmonth 14. Copyists will not attend.*” She salted her porridge with the solemnity of a priest. “He’ll be up on the Steps eating our herring and calling it *bracing*. They all say bracing. It’s what Reach people say when they mean cold and they’re too polite to say cold.”

“It is cold,” said Teo, who had his hands wrapped round his tea like a man warming them at a hearth.

“It’s *bracing*, Teodor.”

Ilse was about to say that she would miss the Register, when a shadow fell across the table. It belonged to Lisbet Arnaud, senior proctor, who had a voice like a ruled line.

“Marrow. Field-Surveyor Brask wants you at the Lantern Gate with your boots on and a week’s worth of chain.” She considered Ilse’s porridge. “Now, I think she meant.”

Cass, opposite, raised both eyebrows. “Week’s worth of chain. Somebody’s lost something.”

* * *

What somebody had lost was a village.

Brask told her that much at the Gate, and nothing else. There was a College dog-cart waiting on the causeway with a fat dun pony in the shafts, a bag of instruments in the back, and a wicker crow-basket that swore at them in a low continuous mutter as Ilse climbed up.

“Nettle’s,” said Brask, as if that explained the language, and took the reins in her right hand.

Brine End lay six miles south, on the salt-marsh edge of the Margin, where the heath ran down into reeds and the reeds into mud and the mud, eventually and without much conviction, into the sea. The road there was a Margin road, which meant it was mostly an agreement between previous travellers. Brask drove it without looking at it. The pony seemed to know the way; or rather, Ilse thought, it seemed to know which way the way was leaning this morning, and leaned with it.

Brask didn’t speak. She had her clay pipe clamped in her teeth, and Ilse waited for her to strike a light, because out of doors she always did, filling the Fieldwork mornings with a smell like burnt hay. She didn’t strike it. The pipe stayed cold. After the first mile Ilse stopped waiting and watched the country instead.

The level changed as they went south. Near the College it was a steady pressure, the Cape’s bedrock humming under the heath. Two miles out it began to waver, and by the fourth mile Ilse could feel the land breathing under the cart wheels, a slow, uneven rise and fall that made her stomach wish it hadn’t had porridge. Then, at about the fifth mile, the level dropped.

It wasn’t silence, not quite. It was a hole in the sound, off to the left and ahead, the way a missing tooth is a hole in a smile. She had felt it once before, in a green lane at home with Tam Brewin’s mare screaming, and she had hoped never to feel it again.

“You feel it,” said Brask. It was the first thing she’d said in five miles.

“Yes.”

“Where?”

Ilse pointed, without thinking, south-southeast. Brask looked at her finger and then at the reeds, and then she nodded once and turned the pony a little left, and for the last mile neither of them said anything at all.

* * *

Brine End had seven cottages, a chapel the size of a cart shed, a row of eel traps drying on poles and, until four nights ago, a well on the green.

The well was still there. It had simply gone somewhere else. It stood forty yards off its old place, in the middle of somebody’s onion bed, a ring of grey stones with its windlass and bucket intact and its rope still swinging a little in the wind, as though someone had just let go of the handle. A path of trodden shingle ran from the cottage doors across the green to where the well had been and stopped at a round patch of dry, pale ground, like the mark a cup leaves on a table. The onions around the new well-head had been shoved outward in a neat ring, roots and all, and lay on their sides in the furrows like spilled skittles.

Two cottages at the seaward end had slid. They hadn’t fallen; they had slid, a yard or two each, and the ground between them and their gardens had torn open in long brown seams. The garden wall of the nearer one was still coming apart. Ilse stood and watched it. A stone in the third course eased out of its place, slowly, as if it were being drawn by a patient hand. It tipped, rolled down the face of the wall and lay in the grass. A minute later the stone beside it followed. The whole wall was unknitting from one end, course by course, the way a sock goes when you pull the right thread.

The hens were the funniest, and Ilse was ashamed of finding them funny. The coop had moved a few feet in the night; the hens had not. Twelve brown hens were walking in a slow, puzzled circle round the place where their coop had stood, pecking at the bare ground and making the small, appalled noises of creatures who suspect that someone is having a joke at their expense.

At the doorstep of the second slid cottage a woman was on her knees, crying.

She was forty or so, with her sleeves rolled and her arms red from the brine pans. The doorstep in front of her was a single slab of old sandstone, worn hollow in the middle by generations of boots. It had turned round. It lay in its place under the door, but backwards, the worn hollow facing into the house and the sharp unworn edge out into the weather. The woman had her palms flat on it.

“My mother scrubbed that step every day for forty years,” she said to nobody. “Every day. You could have eaten off it.”

Brask stood looking at her for a moment. Then she turned to Ilse.

“Chain the well,” she said. “Old site to new. Then the slides. Then I want the levelglass on every corner you can find. I’m going to see the parish copy.”

Ilse chained the well, glad of something to do with her hands. She set an arrow in the dry ring and paid out the chain across the green into the onion bed, a boy from the nearest cottage holding the far handle with an air of tremendous responsibility. One chain. Eighty-two links to the stone kerb of the new well-head. A hundred and twenty feet and a little over. Forty yards, near enough, as the reeve had said. She sighted the bearing: south seventy east, toward the sea. She set the levelglass on the kerb and watched the bead of quicksilver swim in lazy circles and refuse to settle, like a fish in a bowl.

“Is it coming back?” said the boy.

“I don’t know.”

“Mam says the Tenting did it.”

Ilse wrote down *1 ch. 82 l., S. 70 E.* and didn’t answer.

The reeve, a lean worried man called Tobin Sallis, kept the parish copy of the charter in a tin box under his bed. Brask unrolled it on the cart-tail with a stone on each corner. It was a neat clerk’s copy of a charter made in 1006, ink a little faded, every cottage and the well and the green and the chapel in their proper places, with *A True Copy* written at the foot and a College seal.

“Fine,” said Brask. “It’s perfectly fine.”

“Then why —”

“Because it’s a copy, Marrow. A copy is a picture of a promise. It doesn’t hold a blade of grass.” She rolled it up. “The master’s in the Register. The master’s what holds this place. If the ground’s let go, either the master’s been overruled, or it’s been damaged, or it isn’t there.”

She wrote a note on a page torn from her field-book, folded it small, and took a crow out of the wicker basket. It was a big grey-necked bird with a beak like a bent nail, and it said something to her that Ilse was glad she couldn’t translate. Brask tied the note to its leg and threw it up, and it went off north in a straight grey line for the crow-loft on the Cape, the one fixed point it trusted.

They worked the rest of the day. Ilse chained the slides and set the levelglass on every corner-stone and doorpost, and the bead swam everywhere. By late afternoon she had a sketch of Brine End as it now stood, and it looked like a drawing of a place made by someone who had been told about it once, in a pub. Brask walked round with her, prodding things with the Long Lie and saying very little.

The crow came back at nightfall. It dropped out of a sky the colour of slate onto the cart-tail, and Brask untied the reply and read it by the reeve’s lantern. Then she handed it to Ilse.

It was in Merrin Coyle’s copperplate, which wobbled.

*Field-Surveyor — The Brine End master is not in its place. The parish box is present. The sheet is not. I have looked twice, and on the shelves either side, and in the year-store. I cannot account for it. — M. Coyle, Registrar-Adjunct.*

“Misfiled,” said Ilse.

“Coyle doesn’t misfile,” said Brask. “He’s too frightened to.”

Ilse looked across the dark green to the well in the onion bed. She thought of the Little Wick lane, the milestone sinking to its shoulders. She had thought then, sitting in the stubble with Tam Brewin, that it had felt as though someone had reached into the parish chest and burned the charter. She had told herself it wasn’t possible.

* * *

In the morning the village had decided who to blame.

There was a Tenting wagon camped on the dunes half a mile north, two wagons really, painted red and ochre with route-diagrams in white along their sides, and a smoke going up from a cookfire. By the time Brask and Ilse came out of the reeve’s loft, where they had slept on sacks, five men were standing on the green with a hay-fork, two cudgels and a great deal of opinion.

“Came the week before,” one was saying, “and then the ground goes. You tell me.”

“Wainwrights,” said another. “They’re all Wainwrights. They were at Little Sallow when the ditch went, my cousin says.”

“Nobody’s going anywhere,” said Brask.

She didn’t raise her voice. She was a head taller than any of them, and the eye-patch did half her arguing for her. The man with the hay-fork looked at the Long Lie and then at her stilled grey hand, and lowered the fork about six inches.

“With respect, ma’am —”

“With respect, Tobin’s boy could chart better than you can think. Stay here.” She turned to Ilse. “You. With me.”

They walked out across the dunes. The marram grass hissed against Ilse’s boots. When they were halfway, someone at the wagons began to whistle through their teeth, a long falling note that Ilse had heard Cass make, and an old woman came down the wagon steps and stood waiting for them with her arms folded. She was small and brown and very upright, with grey braids tied up in route-cords like Cass’s but three times as many, so that her head looked like a ball of knotted string.

Brask stopped six feet from her and did a thing Ilse had never seen her do. She bowed. It was short and stiff, but it was a bow, the kind you would give to someone in their own house.

“Hey-ho, mother,” Brask said. “Road and rain to your wagon.”

The old woman considered her. “And rain to yours, paper-wall,” she said, “though you’ve not got one. Thessaly Ford, are you? You’ve got the Ford in your mouth.”

“I was.”

“Tansy Wainwright.” The old woman nodded at the village. “They want to burn us, then.”

“They want somebody to blame,” said Brask. “It’s not the same, but it ends the same if nobody’s careful. Did you feel it go?”

“Course we felt it go. Four nights back. Woke the babbies. Felt like a knot slipping.” Tansy Wainwright spat neatly to one side. “We sang the wagons down off the soft and come up here on the sure. We didn’t touch their bloody well. What would we want with a well that walks? We’ve got legs.”

“I know,” said Brask.

The old woman looked at her for a long time, and then at Ilse, and then back toward the reeds and the grey sea beyond the village, and her face did something complicated.

“Ground’s been letting go up and down the Margin since Thaw,” she said. “Little Sallow. The Hollin camp at Dunny Water. Here. Your people say it’s us. Our people say it’s rent.” She unfolded her arms. “Rent’s coming due, paper-wall. Everybody on the road knows it. Only you lot in the Big House think you can write your way out of paying.”

Brask went very still.

It was not the stillness of her grey hand. It was a living stillness, the kind a hare has in the grass when the hawk’s shadow crosses it, and it lasted as long as it took the marram grass to hiss once in the wind. Then Brask nodded.

“Stay up here on the sure,” she said. “I’ll speak to them.”

“You do that,” said Tansy Wainwright, and went back up her wagon steps.

Brask took the five men into the chapel and shut the door. They came out a quarter of an hour later looking like boys caught in an orchard and let off, and the hay-fork went back into the barn.

Then Brask made Ilse chart the village.

“Not as it was,” she said, when Ilse began to lay out the old 1006 lines. “As it is. The well where it stands. The cottages where they’ve gone. You’re holding what’s here so it stops sliding. It’s a stitch, not a repair.”

“The well’s in an onion bed. They want it back on the green.”

“Can you put it back?”

Ilse looked at the bead in the levelglass. The ground had moved, and it was still moving; its latitude was wide open. She could feel the give in it, the way you feel a loose tooth with your tongue. She could chart that well back forty yards and the ground would very likely let her.

“Yes,” she said.

“Are you sure where it was? To the link? Are you sure the water’s still under the old ring, and not under the new? Put it back and you’re guessing with their water.” Brask jabbed the Long Lie at the onion bed. “Hold it where it is and they’ll trample a new path by Candlemonth. Paths are cheap.”

They ran a closed traverse round the village core: nine stations, the well, the green, the seven cottages and the chapel. Brask held the far end of the chain and read the levelglass, and Ilse set the stations and drew, and they did it fast, the way Brask had taught them on the Walk, so the ground couldn’t shift between one station and the next. The whole loop ran to fourteen chains and six links. When Ilse closed it on her board, the last line fell three links short of the first station.

She looked at the gap. Three links. A little under two feet. On the Walk she had smoothed fourteen links out of a stream bank without a second thought. Her pencil hovered over the misclosure, and she could see exactly how she’d lose it: a hair added to each of the long legs, nothing anybody would ever find.

She wrote, in the margin, *Misclosure 3 l., distributed. Ground moving at time of survey.* Then she inked.

Brask watched her do it and said nothing.

“You sign it,” said Ilse, holding out the pen. “You’re the Field-Surveyor.”

“I measured it. So did you. I’ve signed enough of the world, Walker.” Brask didn’t take the pen. “You sign.”

So Ilse put her wren’s foot at the foot of the sheet, with intent, and felt Brine End take hold. It was not like the hedge. It was not like anything she had done before. The whole village, all fourteen chains of it, gripped her right hand like a frightened child grabbing a sleeve, and then the grip eased, and the garden wall at the seaward end stopped unknitting with a stone halfway out of its course, and the bead in the levelglass on the well-kerb gave one last swim and lay down.

The ache came after, a long dull pull from her wrist to her elbow.

Afterwards she borrowed a crowbar from the reeve and spent an hour with the woman’s son turning the doorstep round by hand. It took the two of them and a great deal of swearing on the son’s part. When it was done, the worn hollow faced out into the weather again, and the woman got down on her knees and ran her palm over it and said nothing at all, which was better than thanks.

* * *

The hollowing came at dusk.

Ilse was on the green packing the board, with the charter rolled in oilcloth on the cart-tail. Brask was at the pony’s head. The sun was going down red behind the dunes, and the light came level across the marsh, so that every reed threw a shadow ten feet long. Ilse noticed the gorse first. There was a clump of it at the edge of the green, forty yards off, bright yellow in the last light, and as she looked at it, it went soft.

It didn’t move. It went soft the way a thing goes soft when your eyes are tired, the edges of it blurring into the heath behind. She blinked. The blur stayed. It was about the size of a haycock, and it was not anything. It was a place where the evening didn’t come into focus, like a thumbprint on a lens. And it was coming toward the green.

Where it passed, the ground slumped. Ilse watched a line of turf sag as the blur slid over it, the way sand sags when the tide goes out from under it. A loose stone from the seaward wall, lying in the grass, sank an inch into the earth and lay there looking surprised. The blur made no sound. It slid on toward the middle of the green, toward the well and the fourteen chains and six links of fresh charter she had signed at noon, and Ilse’s level went out like a candle.

It was the silence from the lane, the silence on the road yesterday, but closer, and *hungry*. She had never thought of an absence as hungry before.

“Marrow,” said Brask, quite quietly. “Step back from it.”

The charter. It was on the cart-tail, and the cart was beside the well, and the blur was ten yards from both. Every forger’s nerve in Ilse’s body said *the sheet*, and she was moving before she had decided to. She got her hand on the oilcloth roll. The edge of the blur was a yard from her, then a foot, and she reached past it to pull the charter clear, and her fingertips went in.

It was cold. That was all she had time to think: cold, like putting her hand into a stream in winter.

And then she was sitting on the grass with her back against the well-kerb, and she didn’t know how she had got there.

The sun had gone. A minute ago its rim had been on the dunes; now there was only a red line where it had been. Her right hand was numb to the wrist. The oilcloth roll was in her lap, held so hard that her fingers had crushed it. Someone was singing.

It was Brask. She was standing between Ilse and the blur with the Long Lie planted in front of her, and she was singing, low, in a voice Ilse had never heard her use and would not have believed she owned: a deep, steady, unhurried voice like a woman singing a child down, or a cart going over a long road. The words were in Tenting, or something near it. Ilse caught the shape of them and not the sense. It was a road-song. She knew it from the rise and fall, the way Cass’s song at the cairn had risen and fallen, a road going by the high stone and the low ford. The tune went down and down, and came round, and went down again.

The blur flinched.

There was no other word for it. It drew back from the song the way a hand draws back from a stove, and its edges crawled, and the gorse behind it shuddered into focus and out again. Brask sang on, not louder, only steadier. The blur slid back across the green the way it had come. The turf it had slumped stayed slumped. At the edge of the heath it seemed to thin, and then the gorse was only gorse, dark in the dusk, and the level came back into Ilse’s ears with a rush that made her retch onto the grass.

Brask stopped singing. She stood for a moment with her head down, leaning on the staff. Then she came and crouched by Ilse, stiffly, on one knee.

“What’s my name?”

“Brask. Odile Brask.” Ilse’s tongue felt thick. “What happened?”

“You put your hand in a hollowing. You’ve been sat there five minutes asking me what happened. That’s the fourth time.” Brask took Ilse’s right hand in her own good one and turned it over and looked at the fingertips, which were white and wrinkled as if they had been too long in a bath, and then let it go. “It’ll come back. The feeling. The five minutes won’t.”

Ilse looked down at the oilcloth roll in her lap. The charter was safe. The well stood in the onion bed with its rope hanging still, and the hens had gone to roost somewhere, and the cottages were lit, and nobody in Brine End seemed to have seen a thing.

“It came for the sheet,” she said. “Didn’t it? For what I signed.”

“They come for anything fresh.” Brask stood up, using the Long Lie, and her knee cracked like a stick. “Get in the cart.”

“That song —”

“Get in the cart, Marrow.” Brask turned to the pony and began to check the traces, her back to Ilse, her grey hand hooked in her belt. When she spoke again her voice was her own again, dry and flat, as though the other one had never been. “And not a word of that song to the Provost.”
