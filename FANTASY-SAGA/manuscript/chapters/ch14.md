# Chapter 14
## What Brask Knew

The note was under the Wren Stair door when Ilse came back from Mensuration, written in Brask’s square capitals on the back of an old chain-tally.

MY HOUSE. NETMAKERS’ ROW, VERITY CROSS. BLUE DOOR. AFTER THE SIXTH BELL. COME ALONE AND DON’T BRING THAT ANIMAL.

“That animal,” said Cass, reading it over her shoulder, “is sitting on Teo’s pillow eating a pencil.”

“It’s eating the paint off the pencil,” said Teo. “It doesn’t like the wood. I’ve been watching. It’s very particular.” He looked at Ilse’s face and stopped smiling. “Is it the hollowing? Are you all right? You still can’t feel your fingers properly, you said, and you’ve been holding your pen like it’s a stranger all day, and I don’t think —”

“I’m all right.”

“You said that yesterday,” said Hen, from the window seat. “Four times. In a row. With the same face.”

That was the thing about the five minutes. She didn’t remember them, and other people did. She had come back up the causeway in the dog-cart last night with Brask silent beside her, and the party had been waiting on the stair, and Hen had told her afterwards, quite kindly, that she had asked Teo what day it was and then asked again before he’d finished answering. The tips of the first two fingers on her right hand were still numb, white and puckered like the skin of a cold custard. Everything else had come back. The five minutes hadn’t, and Brask had said they wouldn’t.

She put on her father’s coat. It was too long in the sleeve and too broad in the shoulder and had been his good coat, his chapel coat, and in the lining at the left hem, under a seam she had unpicked and re-sewn herself, was the small flat oilcloth packet that held everything Quince had left her. She felt it knock against her knee as she went down the stair. She always felt it. It was the only thing in the College that nobody knew she had.

* * *

Verity Cross was two miles inland from the Cape, along a lane between stone walls with the wind coming off the sea behind. Netmakers’ Row was at the bottom of the town, near the old harbour, a crooked terrace of cottages with their backs to the weather. The blue door had a cairn-crow sitting on the lintel above it. It looked at Ilse with one pale eye and said nothing, which, for a cairn-crow, was practically a welcome.

Brask opened the door before she knocked.

“You’re late,” she said, and stood back.

The cottage was one room downstairs and a ladder to a loft. It was full of books. They were not on shelves, because there were no shelves; they stood in stacks on the floor, waist-high, leaning against each other, so that walking from the door to the table was like walking a path between cairns. There were crow feathers everywhere, grey and white, stuck in jam jars on the windowsill and pushed between the pages of the books as markers and tucked behind a cracked mirror. The hearth was cold. There was ash in it that might have been a week old. The table was an old plane-table board on trestles, its brass compass-rose still set into one corner, and on it lay, flattened under two stones and a candlestick, Ilse’s survey from the Walk.

She knew it at once. She would have known it across a field. Her own hand, her own linework, the route from the Lantern Gate out to the cairn and back, the stations numbered in her neat small figures. And the stream bank.

“Sit,” said Brask. “I’m making tea.”

She made it the way a person makes tea who has read about it once and disagreed with the method. She put a fistful of black leaf into a pan of water on a spirit-lamp and boiled the two together until the water was the colour of a peat bog. Then she poured it, leaves and all, into a chipped enamel mug for Ilse and a jam jar for herself, and sat down opposite, and put her grey left hand on the table, where it lay curled like a thing she had brought in from the garden.

Ilse drank the tea. It tasted like a boot. She drank it anyway, because her hands needed something to do.

“Fingers?” said Brask.

“The first two are still numb.”

“And the five minutes?”

“No.”

“Didn’t think so.” Brask took a lens from her pocket, a plain brass reading-glass, and set it on the survey over the stream bank. “Look.”

Ilse didn’t need to look. She looked anyway.

It was station nineteen to station twenty-three, the return leg, the morning after the cairn. She remembered every link of it: the cold, the shaking in her legs, the stream that had moved in the night, the bank that wouldn’t close. Her chainings had come out fourteen links long. Fourteen links was nine feet and a bit, and the bank simply hadn’t been where her figures said it should be. And she had been so tired, and the drawing had been so nearly beautiful, and there had been a way to make it beautiful that nobody would ever see. So she had taken the fourteen links out. She had shaved them from the long legs, a link here, two links there, and redrawn the bank half a rod west, where it agreed with everything else, and she had gone over the new line twice to give it weight.

Under the lens, the line had gone over twice. She could see it now. The ink lay thicker along the bank than anywhere else on the sheet, a darker, more confident brown, the line of someone who wants very much to be believed.

“Nobody closes a Margin traverse to nothing,” said Brask. “Nobody honest. The ground moves under you while you work; that’s the Margin. There’s always a gap. On every sheet that came in that day there was a gap, two links, nine links, Tenter’s was out by most of a chain and she wrote it down in letters a foot high. Yours closed like a box lid.” She tapped the bank with a fingernail. “And you went over that twice. People go over a line twice when they’re not sure of it, or when they want you to be sure of it. You weren’t unsure.”

Ilse said nothing. The tea was cooling in her hands.

“You corrected it,” said Brask. “Forgers correct. Surveyors record.”

There it was.

Ilse sat with the mug in both hands and thought, with great calm, about the coach fare home. She had six marks and six. The express was thirty-eight. The slow coach was eleven, and took nine days, and would set her down in Hobbet just in time for Corwin Fask and the parish magistrate to meet her in the yard. She thought about her mother’s letter inside her shirt. *I hope it bought you something.* She thought about the iron ring on her finger, the meridian line on it, and how it would feel to take it off and put it on the table beside the jam jar. She found she wanted very badly not to cry in front of this woman, and that she wasn’t going to, and that this was the only thing she was sure of.

“Yes,” she said.

* * *

“Good,” said Brask.

Ilse looked up.

Brask was leaning back in her chair with the jam jar in her good hand, and her one eye was on Ilse, and there was something in her face that Ilse couldn’t place, because she had never seen it there before. It took her a long moment to recognise it as relief.

“So was I, once,” Brask said. “Every surveyor who ever chose what to leave out.”

“I don’t —”

“You thought I’d brought you here to put you on a coach.” Brask drank her terrible tea. “If I wanted you gone I’d have said so in the Hall of Instruments with the survey in my hand and Aurek Hallam at my elbow, and you’d have been in Verity Cross by nightfall looking for a cart east. I passed you. I wrote *true enough* on it. I meant it. It was true enough. It was the best Walk survey I’ve seen in eleven years, and the only lie on it was a lie about nine feet of stream bank, and the stream bank moved again two days later anyway, so you were wrong and it didn’t matter.” She put the jar down. “But I wanted to see what you’d do if I put it in front of you. You could have said the ground moved after you chained it. You could have said you smudged it. You’re a good liar. I’ve watched you tell half the Fieldwork class you’d done the reading, and they believed you.”

“Cass didn’t.”

“Tenter doesn’t believe anybody. It’s her best quality.” Brask looked at her. “You didn’t lie to me just now. That’s what I wanted to know.”

Ilse put the mug down because her hands had started to shake.

“I’ve done it before,” she said. “Not on sheets like that. On charters. At home. Small things, hedges and ditches, for people who —”

“Stop.” Brask held up her good hand. “I don’t want the parishes. I don’t want the names. What I don’t know I can’t be made to say, and someone, one day, is going to try very hard to make me say it.” She paused. “I know the shape of it. That’s enough. I knew the shape of it the moment I put the lens on that bank. I know what a forger’s hand looks like.”

“How?”

Brask was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind had got up, and the crow on the lintel said something disgusted and shifted its feet. The candle on the table leaned and recovered.

“Because I’ve left things off sheets,” Brask said. “Not hedges. Not for money. But I’ve stood on ground and measured it and gone home and drawn it, and chosen what went on the paper and what didn’t, and signed it, and the land held to what I drew and not to what was there. Every surveyor does it. Every map is a choice about what matters. Most of them don’t know they’re choosing, so they call it accuracy.” She turned the jam jar slowly on the table. “The ones who know they’re choosing are either very honest or very dangerous. Usually both.”

“The Sallow,” said Ilse, before she could stop herself.

Brask’s eye came up. For a second Ilse thought she had gone too far. Then the older woman laughed, a short dry bark with no humour in it.

“Everybody knows that much,” she said. “Even Walkers. All right. You’ve earned a bit of it, putting your hand in a hollowing for a sheet of paper. That was the stupidest thing I’ve seen a link do in nineteen years, and I’ve seen a Patent boy try to chain a sounder.”

She told it plainly, without looking at Ilse, the way a woman might read out an inventory.

“Eleven eighty-three. Seven of us. Me in charge, because I was the best field surveyor in the College and I knew it and so did everybody else. Aurek Hallam as my second, because he was the most careful man I’d ever met and somebody had to stop me walking off cliffs. Ansel Corrow, Mira Tey, Jory Paske, Benedek Vass, and a girl called Wenna Rook who was nineteen and could read grain like Tenter. Thirty leagues southwest, into the deep Drift, further than anyone had chained in a hundred years. We were going to find the sounders’ singing-grounds and chart a road to them.”

“Did you?”

“We found them.” Brask’s voice didn’t change. “A valley where the ground was all sounders, Marrow, hundreds of them, rising and going down like porpoises in a bay. The whole valley singing, so low you heard it in your teeth. You’ve seen one breach. Imagine a sea of them.” She stopped. “And we found something else. I’ll not describe it. I can’t, and every time I tried, back here, the Board of Errata wrote down *hallucination under Drift-sickness* in a very neat hand. It was there. It was there, and it was trying to talk to us. The ground moved when it spoke. I don’t know what it was saying. I know it wasn’t saying *go away*. That’s all you’ll get.”

Ilse sat very still.

“Four of them didn’t come back. Corrow and Tey in a fold that closed. Paske and Vass in the night; we never found them. Rook and Hallam and I came out. I lost this” — she touched the leather patch over her right eye — “to a hollowing on the way, three days from the Margin. Bigger than the one at Brine End. Much bigger. I don’t remember the next day and a half at all, which is a longer five minutes than yours. When I came back to myself I was on Aurek’s back.” For the first time something moved under the flat voice. “He carried me twenty leagues. Over ground that wouldn’t keep still for him. I was not a small woman, then or now. He never once said I was heavy.”

Ilse thought of the kind old man coming down the aisle on Level Two with tea and ginger biscuits, his gloved hand careful on the stair rail.

“The gloves,” she said. “He told Teo it was frostbite. From the Sallow.”

“It was cold enough out there.” Brask looked down at her own grey hand on the table. “We all brought something back. I brought this, and an eye short. Aurek brought his hands. The Board brought a verdict, and that was the end of me as a field surveyor. They gave me Fieldwork and a shed that leaks.” She shrugged with one shoulder. “I don’t sign maps now. I signed enough of the world out there to last me. It can hold itself or it can’t.”

“And the song? At Brine End?”

“Thessaly Ford had Tenting neighbours. A Wainwright woman wintered at our gate every year when I was a girl and taught my mother songs for the soft ground. They work. Hollowings hate a song and they hate ground that moves. Nobody at the College will tell you that, because the College doesn’t hold with things that work for reasons it can’t sign.” She looked at Ilse. “The Provost would like that song a great deal less than he’d like you.”

She leaned forward, and her voice changed again, became the Fieldwork voice, the one that bit at the end of every sentence.

“The map is a promise, Marrow. Not a cage. Every time you sign one, you’re promising the ground something. That it’s here. That it’ll stay here. That this is what’s true about it. Sometimes that’s a good promise, and the ground is glad to keep it. Sometimes it’s a cage, and the ground strains at it, and the strain comes back up your arm for the rest of your life.” She tapped the table beside the stream bank. “Know which one you’re making. That’s all the craft there is. The rest is arithmetic.”

* * *

It had grown dark outside. Brask got up and lit a second candle from the first, and the books in their stacks threw long leaning shadows up the walls.

“So,” she said, sitting down again. “Here’s the bargain.”

Ilse waited.

“I know what you are. I’ll keep it. I won’t lie for you; if the Board asks me straight I’ll tell them straight. But nobody’s asked, and I don’t volunteer.” Brask held up one long finger. “One condition. No more lies on any College sheet. Not a link. Not a hair. Not on Morning Survey, not on the Ladder, not on a practice sheet you throw in the stove. Every gap you find, you write it down. Every misclosure. Every bloody stream bank. You did it yesterday at Brine End, three links, *ground moving at time of survey*. I watched you think about losing it, and I watched you not. That’s the surveyor I took on. You keep being her, inside this College, and what you did outside it is between you and the parishes.”

“Yes,” said Ilse.

She said it at once, and she found she meant it, and the meaning of it went through her like a long drink of cold water. Not a link. Not a hair. She thought of the oilcloth packet in the lining of her coat, the blind ink and the damp-lens and Quince’s mark folded small, and she thought: *I won’t need it. Not here.* It was a lighter thought than she’d expected.

“Yes,” she said again. “I promise.”

“Don’t promise. Promising’s what you do on paper. Just do it.” Brask drained her jam jar and made a face at the dregs. “And one more thing. Your young man from the Court.”

“He isn’t my —”

“Reyne. He came to the shed a week ago. Very polite. Hat in his hand, which is more than most of the Patent boys manage. Asked whether there’d been anything irregular in your Walk survey. Asked whether I’d looked at it closely.” Brask’s mouth twitched. “I told him I look at everything closely. I told him I’d passed it true enough, which is what I wrote. He wrote down what I said in a little black book, word for word. I watched him do it.”

Ilse felt cold all the way down. “What else did he ask?”

“Whether I knew where you’d learned to draft. I said Hobbet, I supposed, since that’s where you’re from. He wrote that down too.” Brask looked at her for a long moment. “He’s not a fool, that boy. And he’s got the look of someone who finishes things. I don’t know what he thinks he knows. I don’t want to know. But I’d not leave anything lying about, if I were you.”

“There’s nothing to leave lying about.”

“Good,” said Brask, in a tone that didn’t believe her in the least and didn’t mind. She got up. The interview was over. At the door, with her hand on the latch, she said, without turning round, “That was a decent charter at Brine End. The well held. Sallis sent a boy this morning to say so.”

Then she opened the door, and the wind came in and set every feather in every jam jar trembling, and Ilse went out.

* * *

She walked back the long way, because she wanted to.

The lane up from Netmakers’ Row ran past the Chain & Anchor, which was full and loud and smelled of fried fish, and up through the market square, where the stalls were shuttered for the night and a lone Tenting girl was packing away a cage of something grey and indeterminate that peered at Ilse with large amber eyes as she passed. Then the town ran out, and there were only the stone walls and the wind and the two miles of dark lane to the Cape.

She had been trusted. That was what had happened in the blue-door cottage, under the books and the crow feathers. She turned it over as she walked, the way she would have turned over a coin she didn’t recognise, looking for the mint mark.

Quince had trusted her, in his fashion. He had trusted her with his hands and his tricks and the mark of a dead man, and she had loved him, but he had always kept one eye on the till. Her mother trusted her the way you trust weather, which is to say she expected the worst and laid in candles. Teo trusted everybody, and Hen trusted anyone who laughed at her jokes, and Cass trusted nobody and made exceptions with her whole heart and a great deal of noise. Master Hallam was kind to her, and kindness was a warm thing, and she liked it more than she let herself show. But kindness was not the same as this.

Brask knew what she was. Brask had put the worst thing Ilse had done in Ilse’s own handwriting on the table between them, under a lens, and looked at it, and then looked at her, and said *good*. And then she had given Ilse a rule and her silence, and asked nothing back except that Ilse keep her word.

Ilse had never in her life been given something she couldn’t hide. Everything she owned that mattered had a lining to go into. The tin behind the brick. The packet in the coat. The mark under the mark. This had no lining. She would have to carry it out where anyone could see.

She found she didn’t know where to put it. So she carried it in her hands, as it were, up the dark lane, and it was not heavy.

At the top of the rise the wind dropped, and the College came into view on its headland. The Old Court sat squared and honey-dark against the sea, and the lantern burned on the keystone of the Gate, a single steady point. Beyond it the Drift Wing trailed out over the heath, its hundred windows lit. As she watched, one of them in the Second Range slid a foot or two along the dark and stopped, as though somebody inside had shifted in their sleep.

The fingers of her right hand had begun to tingle. She held them up in front of her face in the starlight and flexed them. It was pins and needles, sharp and hot and alive, running from her knuckles to the tips, the feeling coming back into the first two fingers a little at a time. She stood in the lane and let it come. Then she put her hand in her father’s pocket, where it was warm, and walked on toward the light.
