# Chapter 15
## Blind Ink

For a week she kept her promise without even noticing she was keeping it, which was the best kind of promise to keep.

Morning Survey on Fogmonth 18 came up out of a fog so thick that Teo, at the far end of the Long Gallery with the chain’s handle, was a voice and nothing else. “Sixty-four,” said the voice. “Sixty-four and a — sorry — sixty-four links and a hair, Marrow, I can’t see the arrow, I can see my own hand if I hold it very close to my face and that’s all, it’s like being inside a dumpling.”

“Write the hair,” said Ilse.

“How big a hair?”

“Your best guess, and that it’s a guess.”

She heard Hen, somewhere behind her in the white, laugh and write it down. It was the old drill now, and they were good at it. The Wren Stair ran to one chain and nine links from the landing to the turn, if it hadn’t turned in the night, which this morning it had, by about a hand’s breadth, so that its bottom step now pointed fractionally more toward the sea. The Long Gallery ran two chains thirty-two from the stair-head to the end window, and this morning it ran two chains thirty-three and a bit. Three chains forty-one, the stretch was meant to be. Three chains forty-two and Teo’s hair, it was. Cass walked the length of it with her hand on the wall, as she always did, and said the grain was leaning southwest and sulky with it, and Ilse drew it all in, the hand’s breadth and the link and the hair and the sulk, on four sheets of good hard Lisk paper, and wrote *est.* beside Teo’s guess.

Then they went to the Antechamber to sign.

It was a long low room on Level One of the Register, lit by oil lamps in brass brackets, with a smell of wet coats and ink. Eight sloping desks ran down the middle, each with a chained brass inkwell and a rack of pens, and at the end of them Merrin Coyle sat behind a high clerk’s counter with the roll, ticking off parties as they came in out of the dark. Master Hallam liked every sheet signed here, under the Register’s roof, and nowhere else. It was the first thing Coyle had told them on their first morning, and he had looked so anxious saying it that Ilse had wondered what happened to students who signed in the Ranges.

Hen pulled the four sheets toward herself at the desk.

“I’ll take the first round,” she said. “I’m fastest.”

She was. She signed like she counted, quickly and with total confidence, a neat little mark like a gull’s wing over a line, and she had signed all four before Cass had uncapped her pen. Every sheet wanted two marks at least and could take four. Hen always signed all four. It had become a joke, early on, and then a habit, and then a thing nobody noticed. Ilse signed two, the stair plan and the gallery tie-sheet, the wren’s foot set down cleanly in the printed box at the foot of each sheet, three lines and a spur.

She looked at the box afterwards. She couldn’t have said why. It was a plain printed rule, a rectangle a little larger than a playing card, with *Attested* in small type above it, the same on every Morning Survey folio she had ever signed. She looked at it for long enough that Coyle cleared his throat from the counter.

“Is something the matter, Miss Marrow?”

“No,” said Ilse. “Nothing.”

On the way to breakfast she found she was thinking about rain.

It had been nagging at her for days, under everything else: under Brine End and the blue-door cottage and the promise. On Gleaning 10, in the driving rain, Sorry had knocked Coyle’s tea across the folios, and on the wet sheet, under her fresh wren’s foot, she had seen a line. Pale and violet, for no more than a breath, and then it dried away to nothing. She had told herself then that she had smudged her own mark. She had been barely a fortnight in the College and frightened of everything and very glad of the excuse.

But she had not smudged it. She knew how her own pen moved. And she knew exactly one ink in the world that showed violet when it was wet and dried to nothing on the page, because she had a bottle of it sewn into the hem of her father’s coat.

The Brine End master was not in its place. Coyle had looked twice. A sheet had gone out of the Register that should never have gone anywhere, and a village had come loose. And somewhere in her chest, in the place where she kept the things she did not let herself think, a small cold voice that sounded like Quince said, *You know what blind ink is for, girl. You’ve only ever used it for one thing.*

* * *

The Wren Stair was empty that evening. Teo was at the Sounders’ Choir, practising notes so low that the Old Court windows hummed with them. Cass had gone to Verity Cross with Orla Devane to do something she described as *none of your business, paper-wall, and also cider*. Hen had gone to bed straight after supper, which was unlike her. She had said she was tired, and she had looked it, and Ilse had thought nothing of it.

She sat on the floor with her back against the wardrobe, which had drifted nearly to the middle of the room again, and took her father’s coat across her knees.

The seam at the left hem was her own work. She had unpicked it the night before the coach and sewn it up again with brown thread, small even stitches that a laundress’s daughter could do in her sleep. She cut four of them now with the point of her knife and drew out the oilcloth packet. It was the size of a prayer book and about as thick. She unrolled it on the floorboards in the lamplight.

Everything was there. The corked bottle of blind ink, the size of her thumb, with its pale contents clinging to the glass. Two steel nibs, very fine. A square of chamois. A little paper twist of alum crystals. A disc of watch-glass two inches across, ground thin at the edges, in a ring of brass with a short handle, which Quince had made himself out of a broken pocket-watch and a curtain ring. And folded small in the middle of it all, on a scrap of old vellum, the mark of Abelard Quince, traced in his own hand: a crooked letter Q with a hook through it like a fish on a line.

She didn’t unfold the vellum. She didn’t need to. She had drawn that mark so many times she could have done it blind in the dark, and had, more than once.

Sorry came out from under Teo’s bed and sat at the edge of the oilcloth, watching. It reached out a paw toward the alum twist, sniffed it, and sneezed so hard it fell over.

“Serves you right,” said Ilse.

She made the lens the way Quince had taught her. She ground a few crystals of alum to powder in her palm with the back of the knife, tipped the powder into an eggcup, and added warm water from the kettle on Teo’s spirit-stove, a thimbleful at a time, stirring with the nib-end of a pen until the crystals had gone and the water was faintly cloudy. Then she dipped a corner of the chamois, wiped the watch-glass with it so that a film of alum-water lay across the whole disc, thin and even, and breathed on it twice to keep it damp. Blind ink was made with gall and alum and a whisper of quicksilver, and dried invisible. A damp alum film over it, held close, wet the gall back up through the paper just enough to show. Quince had called it *asking the paper politely*.

To be sure, she tested it. She dipped a nib in the blind ink and drew a short line across a scrap of paper. The line went down pale and shining and dried in a few seconds to nothing at all. She held the damp glass over it, an inch above the paper, and looked.

There it was: a hair-fine line, violet as a bruise, lying on the paper as plain as day.

Ilse sat back on her heels. Her heart was going quickly. She told herself that she had made a promise to Brask, and that the promise was *no lies on any College sheet*, and that this was not a lie; it was looking. Looking was what surveyors did. Brask herself had said it: if you can’t say how you might be wrong, you haven’t measured anything. She was only trying to find out whether she had been wrong.

It was a good argument. She noticed that it was a very good argument, and that she had built it very quickly, and that she had been building good arguments very quickly since she was fourteen. She wrapped the lens in the chamois and put it in her pocket anyway.

* * *

She had a copyist’s key. Hallam was back from the Salt Steps, and the copying evenings had begun again, and on Fogmonth 19 she spent two hours on Level Two finishing Bellwether while the Register hummed around her and her mind was entirely elsewhere. The Master did not come down with tea. A note from him lay on her table instead, in a hand so neat and careful it was almost a drawing: *My dear — kept upstairs with the Provost all evening, alas. Bellwether looks splendid. Don’t stay past the bell. A.H.* At the ninth bell she capped her ink and went up the stair as if to leave.

The Antechamber was dark and empty. Coyle had gone. The lamps were out except for one turned low over the clerk’s counter, and the eight sloping desks stood in the gloom like a row of empty pews. The filing presses ran along the back wall, tall oak cabinets with doors of brass mesh, and behind the mesh lay the Morning Survey folios for the current year, bundled by week and by party, waiting for Coyle to carry them down to the year-store.

Ilse turned the lamp up a quarter-inch and took down the bundle for that morning.

She found her own party’s sheets first, because she knew them by heart. The Wren Stair plan in her hand, with Teo’s guess marked *est.* and Hen’s gull-wing at the foot and, beside it, her own wren’s foot, three lines and a spur, a little heavier on the spur than she liked. She laid the sheet flat on the counter. She took the lens out of the chamois and breathed on it twice. Then she held it over her mark and looked.

The line was there.

It lay beneath her wren’s foot, violet in the damp glass, finer than a hair. It didn’t touch her mark so much as run through it. It came in from the left along the printed rule of the attestation box, exactly under the rule, so exactly that the visible black line and the hidden violet one were a single line until the lens pulled them apart. At the place where the mark sat, the violet line turned and made a small, intricate figure, a knot of curves no bigger than a fingernail, and her wren’s foot had been set down squarely over it, the way you set a seal over wax. Then the line went on out of the box’s right-hand side, ran along the foot of the sheet to the binding edge, and stopped there at the margin, as if it continued on some other sheet that wasn’t there.

She moved the lens to Hen’s gull-wing. The same line. The same small knot under the mark. The same run to the edge.

She took the next sheet, and the next. Teo’s mark, a looped T like a pothook. Cass’s, a quick Tenting zigzag. Hen’s on all four. Her own on two. Every one of them sat squarely on a violet knot, and every knot ran out along the rule to the margin and stopped.

She put her own party’s sheets down and took up another party’s at random. Then a third. Then she went along the press and pulled bundles from the back, last week, the week before, Reaping, Haysel. Then Kasimir Reyne’s party: Orla Devane’s flourish, Absalom Grice’s laborious cross, Piety Mercer’s spiky little steeple, and Kasimir’s own mark, a beautifully drawn R with a level line through it, perfect, every stroke as clean as an engraving, sitting on its violet knot like a jewel on a pin.

Every mark on every sheet.

Her hands had gone cold, but very steady. It was the old steadiness, the one that came over her at two in the morning in a stranger’s orchard with the lantern shuttered and the ink wet. She stopped being frightened and began to look the way Quince had taught her to look: at how the thing was made.

It was not drawn by hand. That was the first thing. She knew hands. Every hand wavered, every hand pressed harder at the start of a stroke than at its end, every hand made each knot a hair different from the last. These knots were identical. Sheet after sheet, the same curve, the same thickness, the same tiny hook at the same place. No hand on earth drew two hundred identical figures a morning. These had been printed, from a plate, in blind ink, before the paper ever came to the Antechamber. They had been printed exactly under the attestation box, so that any student who set a mark in the box, as they were told to, set it precisely over the knot.

And that was the second thing, the thing that turned her stomach over. She knew what the knot was for.

It was what she did. It was what Quince had taught her to do in his back kitchen in Hobbet, with the shutters closed. You took the mark that would carry the weight and you put it under the mark that would be seen, line for line, in blind ink, and you signed over it with intent, and the charter took. The burden ran down the hidden line and away to whoever it belonged to. She had done it on the Tull charter. She had done it on every charter for three years. A ghost was a channel. It had two ends, and what ran along it was weight.

Here, one end was the student’s mark. The other end was off the edge of the sheet.

She looked at the knot under Hen’s gull-wing and thought of Wil Ashdown on the bench outside the infirmary in Gleaning, staring at nothing. She thought of his right hand lying in his lap, the fingers grey to the second knuckle. The signing hand.

She looked down at her own right hand on the counter. The first two fingers were pink again. The pins and needles had gone two days ago. It looked like a hand.

*Someone is ghosting the whole College.*

She made herself go on. A surveyor finds the edge of a thing. She pulled the year-store bundles that Coyle hadn’t yet carried down, and when those ran out she found the key to the stair and went down to Level Two in the dark and brought up the boxes she had taped and labelled herself a fortnight ago. Highsun 1202: violet knots. Seedtime 1201: violet knots. The first week of Thaw 1201: violet knots, every one. The last week of Candlemonth 1200, the old wheat-sheaf paper, and she held the damp glass over a dozen marks, then twenty, then every mark in the bundle, and there was nothing under any of them but paper.

Clean. Clean until the year turned, and then every mark on every sheet, every morning, for nearly three years.

Something about Thaw 1201 snagged in the long cupboard at the back of her mind, and she reached for it.

Then it came to her, all at once, what it would mean to tell.

She would go to someone, Brask or the Provost or Master Hallam. She would say: *Every Morning Survey sheet since 1201 has a ghost-line under every student’s mark. Someone is passing burden onto the students. That’s why they’re going Quiet.* And they would look at her, kindly or not, and say: *How do you know, Miss Marrow?* And she would have to hold up the watch-glass in its curtain-ring and say: *With this. It’s a forger’s lens. I know because I’ve done it. I know what a ghost looks like because I’ve drawn hundreds.* And the Board of Errata, which would in a few weeks be holding the Tull charter up to a damp lens of its own, would not need to wait for the papers from Hobbet at all.

Only a forger would see this. Only a forger could say so. And the moment she said so, she would be finished.

She stood very still at the counter with the lens in her hand and the violet line under Hen’s mark, and somewhere at the far end of the Antechamber, a page turned.

* * *

She closed her fist round the lens. The damp film soaked cold into her palm. In one movement she slid it up her left sleeve, where her cuff held it against her wrist, and turned.

There was a light among the far presses, behind the last row of cabinets, where the room ran back into an alcove she had always assumed was a store. It was a dark lantern, half-shuttered. It moved. And then Kasimir Reyne stepped out from between the cabinets with the lantern in one hand and a ledger with a red spine under his arm, and stopped, and looked at her.

For a moment neither of them said anything. Ilse was acutely aware of the counter behind her: the open bundles, the sheets fanned out, the year-store box labelled *1200* in her own copy of Coyle’s hand, standing open on the floor at her feet with its tapes undone.

“Miss Marrow,” said Kasimir.

“Mr. Reyne.”

He came forward a few steps into the light of the counter lamp. He was in his shirtsleeves and waistcoat, without his gown, and his hair, for once, was not quite perfect; a strand of it had come loose over his forehead. He moved the ledger from under his arm to behind his back, not quickly, but not slowly either. His eyes went past her to the counter and the sheets and the open box, and she watched him read the scene the way she would have read it, item by item.

“It is past the ninth bell,” he said.

“I’m a copyist. I have a key.” She kept her voice level. “What’s your excuse?”

“I do not believe I owe you one.”

“You’re in the Register after hours with a lantern you’ve been keeping shuttered, and you hid when you heard me come in. You’ve been standing back there in the dark listening to me for —” she had no idea how long; she thought of the whole silent hour at the counter and went cold again — “for some time. I think you owe somebody an excuse.”

“I was reading.” He tilted his head at the counter. “You were not copying. Those are Morning Survey folios. They are not on your list; Coyle does not let copyists touch the current presses. And that” — he nodded at the box at her feet — “is from the year-store, which is on Level Two, which is locked at the ninth bell.” His voice was very even and very precise, the voice of a man reading out a charge. “You have the sheets of the whole College spread on a counter in the dark. What are you doing to them?”

“Nothing.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s the true one.” She lifted her chin. “What are *you* doing here? With your mother’s Court so very interested in the Register, and you the son of the Chancellor, reading in the dark behind the presses with a book you don’t want me to see. If I went to Master Hallam tomorrow and said I’d found Lady Reyne’s boy in the Register after hours with a lantern, what do you think he’d say?”

Something moved in his face, quickly, and was put away.

“I think,” he said, “that he would ask what *you* were doing here. And I think you would not want him to.”

They stood and looked at each other across ten feet of lamplit floor. The Register hummed beneath them, seven levels of held places singing under their breath. Ilse could feel the lens against her wrist, the damp spreading into her cuff. She could feel her heart in her throat. She kept her hands still at her sides by an effort that made her shoulders ache.

Kasimir looked at her for a long time. He was not looking at the sheets now. He was looking at her face, the way he had looked at her in the dark by Widow Tull’s hedge, as though she were a boundary stone he was checking against a record.

“You are shaking, Miss Marrow,” he said quietly.

“It’s cold.”

“It is not especially cold.” He set the lantern on the nearest desk. “I am told you sat in the dark at the Ladder with the room turning under you and drew it by touch, and were not frightened. I have seen you come back from Brine End with your hand dead to the wrist, and you were not frightened. You are very frightened now.” His grey-blue eyes went once more to the sheets fanned across the counter, and back to her. “For someone who is merely copying.”

She said nothing. There was nothing she could say that would not be a lie he could check, or a truth that would hang her.

He waited. When she didn’t speak, he nodded once, slowly, as if she had answered after all and he had written the answer down. He picked up the lantern. He did not open its shutter. With the red-spined ledger held close against his side, away from her, he walked past her down the length of the Antechamber, between the empty desks, and did not look at the counter again.

At the foot of the stair he stopped, with his back to her.

“I would put those back exactly as you found them,” he said. “Coyle counts.”

Then he went up. She heard his footsteps on the stone, unhurried and even, one after another, up the long stair toward the Old Court, until the door at the top opened on a breath of cold air and closed again, and there was nothing but the Register humming, and the violet line under Hen’s mark, and her own heart going like a chain paid out too fast.
