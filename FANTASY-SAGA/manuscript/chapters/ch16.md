# Chapter 16
## Hen

Teo noticed first.

It was the twenty-fifth of Fogmonth, a grey morning, the Mess full of steam and the smell of burnt porridge, and Teo was explaining to the table why the bread of Lisk was the finest in the Reach and the bread of every other place was a kind of upholstery.

“In Lisk,” he said, “we don’t say a loaf’s gone stale. We say it’s *matured*. We say it’s *developing character*. My grandmother sold the same loaf for three days at three different prices and called it a vintage.”

Cass snorted into her tea. Ilse, who had been half listening, smiled. And Hen, who had heard the story before and had laughed at it each time, a laugh like a gull going up off a pan-wall, looked up from her bowl and said, “Yes.”

Just that. *Yes.* Then she went back to her porridge.

Teo looked at Hen, and then across the table at Ilse, the way he looked at a sum that hadn’t come out. Ilse looked away. Everyone was tired in Fogmonth, she told herself. The fogs came in off the sea and sat on the Wing like a wet fleece, and half the links in the College had colds.

“She’s not laughing, Marrow,” Teo said on the stair afterwards, low. “Have you noticed? At anything. Since about Thursday. I told her the one about the eel and the bishop and she just nodded, like I’d told her the time.”

“She’s tired.”

“She was tired at the Ladder and she laughed so hard she had to sit on the floor.” “I don’t like it.”

Ilse said she was sure it was nothing. She said it well; she had been saying things well for nine years.

Since the night in the Antechamber, she had signed one sheet each morning. One was the least Coyle’s roll would tick. She had found reasons: a cramp in her hand, a pen that wouldn’t draw, the Gallery sheet she wanted to check before she put her mark to it. And Hen, who always took the first round, who was quickest, had gone on signing all four without a word, because Hen always did. Ilse had noticed this. She had put it in the cupboard at the back of her mind with everything else, and shut the door, and leaned on it.

She had written a note to Dr. Orle on the twenty-first, unsigned, in a disguised hand. *Look at the Morning Survey sheets under a damp alum glass.* She had read it over twice and seen at once that there was only one kind of person in the College who would know to write it, and she had burned it in Teo’s spirit-stove and watched the corner curl.

One more week, she had told herself. One more week to think of a way to say it that wouldn’t hang her.

On the twenty-seventh, Hen didn’t come down to the window seat after supper. She sat at the table in the common room instead, with her hands flat on either side of a sheet of error problems she wasn’t doing, and when Sorry climbed into her lap she didn’t stroke it. It turned round twice and settled, and looked up at her with its amber eyes, waiting to be told what it was.

Hen used to describe it every night. *Look at you, you ridiculous article, ears like two mainsails.* Sorry liked it; its ears grew a little every time, until it looked like a cat assembled from spare parts by an enthusiast.

Hen looked down at it.

“It’s a cat,” she said.

She said it with perfect confidence, because it was true, and Sorry obeyed. Its ears drew in and settled, until they were a small grey cat’s ears, neat and ordinary, the ears of a cat in a picture-book. It looked up at Hen with its amber eyes in its ordinary face, and Hen looked back, and neither of them found anything to say.

Ilse got up and went over and picked it up.

“A small grey cat,” she said, “with ridiculous ears.” And the ears came back, too big again, and Sorry pushed its head hard into her chin, and Ilse stood holding it in the middle of the room with her eyes stinging, while Hen looked at the error problems and didn’t do them.

* * *

Cass tried on the twenty-eighth.

She came in with the look of someone who has decided to solve a thing with violence, sat down across from Hen, put her boots up on the table, and began.

“Salt Steps,” she said. “What d’you do up there all day? Rake salt? Watch it dry? Hey-ho, salt’s dry, let’s have a sing-song about it. Paper-walls on a beach.” Hen didn’t look up. “Your sums are wrong, Solberg. That one. And that one. Halloway’ll have you for it. Little Henrike, best at Mensuration, can’t carry a seven.”

“All right,” said Hen.

Cass reached across and took the pen out of her hand.

It was Hen’s good pen, the one her father had sent from the Steps in a box with a ribbon on it, the one she had once chased Teo the whole length of the Long Gallery to get back. Cass held it up between two fingers, like a dead mouse.

“Mine now,” Cass said. “Come and get it.”

Hen looked at the pen. Then she looked at Cass, quite calmly, with her flat pale eyes.

“You can have it,” she said.

Cass stood up so fast that her chair went over. She threw the pen on the table, where it rolled off and clattered on the floorboards, and went out, and Ilse heard the outer door bang. Hen bent, slowly, and picked the pen up, and put it on the table beside the problems, and sat looking at it.

Ilse found Cass on the stair outside, sitting on the bottom step in the cold with her knees pulled up and her braids hanging forward over her face. She was crying, and furious with herself for crying, and wiping her nose on her wrist.

“She’d have hit me,” Cass said. “A week ago she’d have hit me with the inkwell. Two weeks ago she’d have hit me with the *table*. Road and rain, Ilse, I called her salt-scraper and she said *all right*.” She dragged in a breath. “What is it? What’s the matter with her? You know things. You always know things. Tell me what’s wrong with her.”

Ilse sat down beside her on the cold stone and did not tell her.

* * *

On the first morning of Frost the Long Gallery windows were furred white on the inside, and every breath went up in a cloud, and the chain was so cold it burned to hold.

They did the stretch. Teo held the far end. Cass walked the wall with her palm flat against it. Ilse drew. And Hen counted, because Hen always counted, in a clear, steady voice that carried the length of the gallery.

“Sixty-three,” said Hen. “Sixty-three. A chain and sixty-three links.”

It was right. She had always counted perfectly. But she used to count the way other people sang, with a lift on the round numbers and a little crow of triumph when a length came out even. This morning every number came out the same, the same weight and the same pitch, like pegs driven one after another into the same hard ground.

The stair had turned a hand’s breadth again. The gallery was three links long. Ilse drew it all in, four sheets, and they went down to the Antechamber with the frost still in their hair.

Coyle was at the counter with the roll, his nose red, and a dozen parties were ahead of them at the sloping desks, signing and blowing on their fingers. Ilse laid the four sheets on their desk, and before she had straightened, Hen reached past her and drew them toward herself.

“I’ll take the first round,” Hen said. “I’m quickest.”

She dipped her pen. Her right hand came down on the *Wren Stair* sheet, steady, the pen held between thumb and two fingers, and Ilse, standing at her shoulder, saw the fingers.

The tips of the first two were grey.

It was the grey of pencil-lead rubbed with a thumb. It began at the very tips, under the nails, and ran down past the first joint and faded, the way a stain fades into cloth. In the lamplight it had a faint sheen to it, like stone that has been handled for a long time. Hen set down her wave over a line in its printed box, neat and quick and confident, and her grey fingertips lay on the paper beside it, and they didn’t look like a hand at all. They looked like a hand that had been drawn by someone who had only ever been told about hands.

Ilse stopped breathing.

She saw it then, the whole of it, as clear as a traverse closing. Hen signed four sheets every morning, every sheet, all four, since the last week of Reaping. Teo signed two. Cass signed two. And Ilse, these last two weeks, had signed one. Sixty mornings, four marks a morning: two hundred and forty knots, two hundred and forty violet lines running off the edge of the paper to wherever they went, and something coming back down every one of them into the first two fingers of Hen Solberg’s right hand. She was the best of them. She was the quickest and the most careful and the most willing. She was the most diligent signer in the party, maybe in the whole College, and she had been carrying it all.

Whatever came down a sheet came down to the hands that had signed it, shared between them, and Ilse had taken her hand off three sheets in four. Hen had been carrying Ilse’s share, too.

*Don’t carry what you can make someone else carry.* Quince’s voice, dry, in the back kitchen in Hobbet. Ilse had never once in her life thought she would hear it as an accusation.

Hen had moved on to the *Landing*. Ilse put her hand out and closed it over Hen’s right hand, over the pen.

It was cold. It was not the cold of a hand on a frosty morning, which warms when you hold it. It was the cold of a stone taken out of a stream: a cold that went on being cold, that sat in her palm and gave nothing back.

“Let me,” Ilse said.

“I’m quickest.”

“I know. Let me.”

Hen let go of the pen. She didn’t argue. She didn’t ask why. She stood up from the desk and stood beside it with her hands at her sides, the grey fingertips just showing under her cuff, and waited, like a passenger for a coach.

Ilse sat down and signed the other three sheets herself, one after another, wren’s foot over violet knot, three lines and a spur, knowing exactly what she was doing and doing it anyway. Her hand did not shake. She thought that it ought to. Across the desk Teo was watching her, and Cass was watching Hen, and from behind the counter Merrin Coyle looked up from his roll, and looked at Hen’s hands, and went on looking for a moment longer than a clerk needs to look at anything, and then looked down.

* * *

Hen sat down at breakfast and didn’t eat.

She sat with her porridge in front of her and her hands in her lap, and after a while she said, in the same voice she had counted in, “I’m cold.” Then she said nothing else. Teo put his own coat round her shoulders, which made her look like a child in a tent. He was very white.

“Infirmary,” he said. “Now. Marrow. Now.”

They took her between them. She could walk, but only as though each step had to be decided on. Ilse went ahead and opened the doors, and people in the Old Court turned to look.

Dr. Sabeth Orle met them at the door, a square, brisk woman with her sleeves rolled, and she took one look at Hen’s face and then at Hen’s right hand, and her mouth went thin.

“Bed by the window,” she said to an orderly. “The end one. Get her warm.” She turned Hen’s hand over in her own, gently, and looked at the fingertips under the window light, and pressed one of them with her thumb, and watched it not go pink. “How long has she been like this?”

“A week,” said Teo. “Since Thursday. The laughing stopped on Thursday.”

Orle wrote that down in a ledger she took from her apron pocket. Ilse watched her write it. Her pen moved fast and hard, with a kind of anger in it.

“What is it?” said Cass. “What’s wrong with her?”

Orle closed the ledger. “Drift-fatigue,” she said, and the words came out of her mouth like a thing she had been made to swallow and was bringing back up. “That is what I am to call it. Rest. Warmth. Broth.” She looked at the three of them, at their frost-wet hair and their white faces. “I’m sorry. She’s the fourth. I don’t know what it is. I know what it isn’t. Go and get warm. Come back after dinner. One at a time.”

There were five beds on that side of the long ward. Wil Ashdown lay in the second, on his back, looking at the ceiling. His hands lay on the blanket. Both of them were grey now, to the knuckles. The furlong girl with the plait, whom Ilse had glimpsed through the ward door in Gleaning, lay in the third, propped on her pillows as before. In the fourth a girl Ilse didn’t know lay asleep or not asleep, with her face to the wall.

Teo didn’t go and get warm. He sat on a bench outside the infirmary door, a bench made for one ordinary person, his huge frame folded onto it with his knees nearly at his chin. When Ilse came back after dinner he was still there, with a pie wrapped in a cloth on his knees that he had gone to the kitchens and made, and then not taken in.

“It’s eel,” he said, not looking at her. “Her favourite. She says it’s the only good thing that ever came out of Lisk except me. Then I thought she might say *thank you* in that voice, and I couldn’t.” He held it out. “You take it.”

She took it.

The ward was quiet. The windows looked west over the Margin, and the afternoon light came in long and pale across the beds. Hen was sitting up against the pillows with a blanket round her, quite still, her hands on the coverlet. Somebody had brushed her hair. The grey fingertips lay on the white linen like a child’s that had been drawing with charcoal.

Ilse sat on the stool by the bed and put the pie on the blanket.

“Teo made it,” she said. “Eel.”

Hen looked at it. “Thank you,” she said, in that voice.

Down the row, Wil Ashdown breathed in and out. Ilse wanted, very badly, to make Hen laugh. She had never been the one who made people laugh; she was the one who said the dry thing in the corner. She tried anyway.

“Teo says the pie’s a vintage,” she said. “He says it’s developing character.” Hen looked at her. “Tide’s out on that one, I suppose.”

Hen considered this.

“The tide goes out twice a day,” she said.

Ilse looked down at her own hands. After a while, Hen spoke again, without being asked. Her voice was quite level. It was the voice she had used to count the gallery, to say *I’m cold*, to say *you can have it*.

“I can’t remember why things were funny,” Hen said.

Ilse looked up.

Hen was looking out of the window at the Margin. Her face was calm. It was not sad. That was the worst of it. It had nothing in it at all to be sad with.

“I remember that they were,” Hen said. “I remember laughing. I remember the eel and the bishop. Teo told it in the Chain & Anchor and I laughed so much I spilled the cider on Cass. I can remember all of it. The words. Where we were sitting.” She turned her grey fingertips over on the blanket and looked at them, not with fear, only with attention, the way she would have looked at a sum. “I just can’t remember why.”

Ilse sat on the stool and did not move. She found she could not.

Nearly two weeks since the night in the Antechamber with the violet line running under Hen’s mark. She had known. She had seen it as plainly as she had ever seen anything in her life, and she had gone back to the Wren Stair and slept, and got up the next morning and done the Survey and signed one sheet, and let Hen sign the rest. She had written a note and burned it. She had told herself one more week. And she had done it all, every day of it, for the same reason she had done everything since she was fourteen: to keep herself out of the ledger. To keep the wren’s foot clean. To protect the one thing she had that was hers.

Hen had carried it. Hen had carried Ilse’s silence down two hundred and forty violet lines into the tips of her fingers, and had not known she was carrying anything at all.

“I’m sorry,” Ilse said. It came out very small.

Hen looked at her, mildly.

“What for?” she said. It was a real question. She wanted to know.

* * *

That night Ilse walked the Drift Wing.

She walked it because she couldn’t lie still, and because the Wing at night was the only place in the College that seemed to be telling the truth. It was never quite where it had been at dinner. She counted her paces without meaning to: ninety-three from the stair to the turn tonight, the frost making the hold go thin. On the Long Gallery the moonlight came in through the frosted windows and lay on the floor in long white panes, and the gallery was a few links longer than it had been that morning, and by First Bell it would be longer still, and four people would come with a chain and a levelglass to tell it where it was. And it would believe them, because they would sign. Hundreds of signatures every morning, holding the whole sprawling Wing in place over the moving heath. And every one of them sitting on a violet knot.

She stopped at the end window and put her palm flat against the wall. The stone was cold and faintly trembling, like the flank of a horse. She went over it the way she would have gone over a survey, station by station.

She could go to Brask. Brask would believe her. Brask would also have to ask how she knew, and Ilse would have to tell her about the lens, and the lens was the one thing she had kept back in the blue-door cottage, the mark under the mark. *No more lies on any College sheet.* She had kept that promise. She had kept it so carefully that she had let a girl go grey.

She could go to Master Hallam. He was the Master of the Register; these were his sheets. He would be kind. He would pour her tea and listen with his gloved hands folded, and he would be appalled, and then he would ask, gently, the way he asked everything: *But how did you see it, my dear?* And she would not be able to answer him either.

She could say nothing. She could go on signing her one sheet a morning and hope that someone else found the knots, someone who didn’t have to explain how they knew. Kasimir Reyne, perhaps, with his red-spined ledger. She could let the Board of Errata hold her tribunal, and keep her secret all the way to the end of it, and walk out with a clean wren’s foot, and come back to the infirmary in Thaw to sit by Hen’s bed and find it still and grey.

*Never let anyone carry you.* That had been the other half of Quince’s saying, the half she had always been proudest of. She had come five hundred and sixty miles on the Long Field money so that nobody would ever carry her. And for three years, on every charter, she had let a dead man do it, and for two weeks she had let a nineteen-year-old girl from the Salt Steps do it, and she had called the first one clever and had not called the second one anything at all.

She took her hand off the wall.

The truth was not complicated. It never was, at the bottom. She had always known that; it was what made her good at lying. The ghost under the Morning Survey sheets could only be seen by a forger, and only told by a forger, and the moment she told it she would be one, out loud, in daylight, with her name on it. She would lose the Patent she didn’t have yet. She would very likely lose the College. She would lose the field, for good this time; there would be no buying back the Long Field for a woman struck off by the Board of Errata. She looked at the whole cost, every link of it, and wrote it down in her head with no gap smoothed over.

Then she went back along the gallery and down the stair, ninety-three paces, to the Wren Stair.

The Bell of Hours had struck one some time ago; it was the third of Frost now, strictly speaking. There was a line of lamplight under the common-room door. Of course they were awake. Teo would be sitting at the table with his hands round a cup he wasn’t drinking. Cass would be at the window with her boots on, because she always put her boots on when she was miserable, as if she might need to walk somewhere in a hurry.

Ilse stood outside her own door. She had never knocked on it. Nobody knocked on their own door.

She knocked.

There was a silence inside, and the scrape of a chair, and then Teo opened it, enormous in the doorway with the lamp behind him and his eyes red. Past him she could see Cass at the window, in her boots, turning round. Sorry was on the table among the cold cups, its too-big ears pricked forward, watching her.

“Marrow?” said Teo. “Why did you knock?”

Ilse looked at the two of them, at the lamp and the cups and the empty window seat where Hen used to push her cold feet under someone’s leg, and she made herself stand in the doorway, in the light, where they could both see her face.

“I have to tell you something about myself,” she said, “and then something about this College, and you’ll like neither.”
