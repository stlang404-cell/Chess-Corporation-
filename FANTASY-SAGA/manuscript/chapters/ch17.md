# Chapter 17
## Confession in the Mess Kitchen

The Mess kitchen after midnight smelled of cold mutton fat and lye soap and the ghost of a thousand loaves, and it was the only room in the College that Ilse trusted not to listen.

The Wren Stair listened; voices went through its lath walls the way water goes through a sock. Besides, Hen’s bed was up there, stripped to the ticking since Dr. Orle’s porters had carried her down, and Ilse could not have said any of this with that bed at the edge of her eye.

So they had come down the back stair with one candle, the three of them in their coats over their nightshirts, and Teo had lit his spirit-stove at the end of the long scrubbed table where the Errata Society argued on Thursdays. He did it the way other men crossed themselves: eleven pumps of the brass reservoir, a quarter turn of the valve, a match, and the blue ring of flame coming up with a noise like a cat sneezing. Nobody had asked for tea. Cass sat on the bread-board with her boots on the bench and her arms folded and watched Ilse the way she watched weather coming in off the heath.

Ilse took off her father’s coat, turned it inside out on the table and slit the lining along the hem with her knife.

“Marrow,” said Teo. “That’s your good coat.”

“It’s been unpicked and sewn up more times than you’ve had pie.” She drew the things out one at a time and laid them in a row on the boards, as she would have laid out instruments for Brask. A corked bottle the size of her thumb. A disc of watch-glass in a brass ring, a square of chamois and a twist of alum. The fine steel pen Quince had ground for her the year before he died. A scrap of old vellum, folded small, which she opened flat and turned to the candle so that they could see what was inked on it: a crooked, old-fashioned Q, its tail doubled back and hooked through its own ring.

“That,” Ilse said, “is the witness-mark of Abelard Quince, hedge-surveyor, of Hobbet in the Fallowmarch. He died three years ago in Seedtime. I’ve been signing it under my own ever since the week we buried him.”

Teo’s hand stopped on the kettle handle.

She told it in order, because order was the only way she knew how to tell anything. She told them about Quince, who had taken her on at fourteen because she had walked into his shop with a measured drawing of his own back garden and pointed out that his privy stood four links over his neighbour’s line. She told them about blind ink, gall and alum and a whisper of quicksilver, which dried to nothing and came up violet under a damp lens. She told them about the Law of Transfer, which nobody in the College taught and every hedge-forger in the Fallowmarch knew: a mark traced in blind ink under another mark would take the burden down into itself, as a drain takes rain.

“So the weight goes to him,” said Teo. “To—to the dead man.”

“The dead don’t feel it. Quince thought it was the neatest thing in the trade. Use the dead as a ditch.”

She told them how many. Thirty-four charters in nine years: three as Quince’s hands under Quince’s mark while he lived, and thirty-one since, under her own wren’s foot. Hedges, ditches, a mill-race at Fennock. Every one inside its latitude, and every one, she believed, put back where it ought to have been. She told them about Agnes Tull’s hedge, and the young man in the grey-blue coat crouched in the grass measuring the root-tear, and how she had spent the money she was saving for something else on a coach seat, and walked into the Margin because the Court could not try a Member of the College.

“That’s why you came,” said Cass. “Not to be a surveyor.”

“I wanted to be a surveyor as well. I just wanted not to be in prison more.”

“And the something else?” said Teo.

“A field.” Ilse looked at the vellum. “A man with a licence moved my father’s fence when I was twelve, and the Court said he could. That’s a long story and I’ll tell it you another night.”

The kettle began to tick and then to whine. Teo took it off the flame and didn’t pour it. He stood holding it in both hands like a man holding a cat that might go for him, and his round face had gone a dull and blotchy red.

“You let me tell you about the Patent exam,” he said. “Twice. About being sick in the examination-hall bucket in front of the Dean of Lisk. And you sat there the whole time with—with a dead man in your coat lining, and said *that’s rotten, Teo*, and I thought —” He stopped. “I’m sorry. I thought we were the sort of people who told each other things.”

“We are,” said Ilse. “I’m telling you.”

“Seventy-one days late.”

“Yes.”

He put the kettle down very carefully, as though it were the thing that had wronged him. Ilse had braced for a great many reactions on the back stair, and not for this one: the plain hurt of a man who would have lied for her if she had asked, and had never been asked.

Then Teo said, “Right,” and blew out through his nose, and poured the water over the leaves. “Right. You lied about yourself. All right. You’re not lying about this.” He looked at her. “Are you? About Hen?”

“No.”

“Then I don’t care about the hedges. I’ll care about them later, properly, with a list.” He pushed a cup at her. “Drink that.”

From the bread-board Cass made a noise. Ilse thought at first that she was choking. Then she saw that Cass was laughing, silently, with her braids shaking and her knuckles pressed against her mouth.

“Road and rain,” Cass said, when she could. “Oh, Road and rain. I knew it. Didn’t I say? Day of the Walk, I said you was a liar.”

“You said it was a nice one.”

“It was a nice one.” She wiped her eyes. “Knew you was Tenting at heart. Paper-wall with a hedge-thief’s hands. Thirty-four hedges, and you sit in Calder’s class prim as a chapel cat.” She swung her boots down off the bench. “Now tell the other part. The part about this Big House that we won’t like.”

* * *

She told them that part with Quince’s watch-glass in her hand.

She told them about the Antechamber at night, and the copyist’s key, and the thirtieth sheet, when she had stopped hoping the next one would be clean. On every sheet filed since 1201 there was a knot of blind ink printed on the rule of every signing box, fine as a hair and laid in before anybody ever touched pen to paper, so that every mark in the College came down on top of it in perfect registration. When you signed on the rule, as you were taught, you rode it, and you never knew.

“Every mark,” said Teo. “Every morning.”

“Two hundred marks a day. The lines run off toward the binding, going somewhere. I don’t know where.” She set the glass down. “It’s the same trick as Quince’s ditch, turned the other way. I put a dead man under my mark so I wouldn’t carry. Somebody has tied all our marks in under theirs. We’re the ditch.”

“Hen,” said Cass. The laughter had gone out of her altogether.

“Hen signs the most. She always has. She signs for the party when we’re slow, and she signs extra when Coyle’s short, because she’s quickest.” Ilse made herself say the rest. “I saw the lines on Fogmonth 19. I’ve known for two weeks. I didn’t say anything because I couldn’t say how I knew without saying all this, and I was frightened, and I told myself there’d be a better time. Hen went grey in the meantime. That’s mine. I’d like it recorded.”

“Recorded,” said Teo, automatically, and then looked stricken, as if the College had spoken through him.

“Who?” said Cass. “Who’s doing it?”

“Someone with a key to the Antechamber and a forger’s hand, or a forger’s money.” Ilse spread her hands. “Coyle prints the folios. The proctors carry them. Any Master could walk into that room. And on the night I found it, Kasimir Reyne was in the far stacks with a lamp, where he’d no business being, and he very much didn’t want to tell me why.”

“The Chancellor’s boy.” Cass’s eyes narrowed. “Court’s wanted the Big House on its knees a hundred years.”

“I don’t *know* it’s him.”

“You don’t know it ain’t.”

“There’s evidence it’s happening,” said Teo. “Not who. Those are different columns.” He sat down heavily. “So we go to the Provost. We wake him up if we have to.”

“And tell him how I know?”

Teo opened his mouth and shut it.

“The minute I say *blind ink* to Steyne,” Ilse said, “somebody asks how a Walker from Hobbet knows how to read it, and the Board already has a letter from Mr. Reyne about the Tull charter. They’d have me out of the Gate inside a month. Maybe that’s fair. But whoever is doing this would go on doing it, and they’d know someone had seen.” She turned the cup in her hands. “Brask knows what I am. She’s known since the Walk. She made me promise, three weeks ago, that I’d never put another lie on a College sheet. That was the price of her keeping quiet.”

“Then we stop signing,” said Teo. “All three of us. We just—don’t.”

“And the stair goes, and Lisbet Arnaud stands at the bottom of it asking very loudly why the Wren Stair party has stopped attesting. Whoever laid those lines reads the proctors’ book, I promise you. A party that suddenly won’t sign is a party that’s seen something. We have to sign every morning and look like idiots who don’t know anything.”

“That,” said Cass, “I can do.”

“So we sign,” said Teo, “and we carry.”

“No.” Ilse picked up the steel pen and turned it over in her fingers. “We sign crooked.”

* * *

Quince had called it *signing off the rule*. A ghost-line, he used to say, was a lodger who had copied your key, and it would only let itself in if the key went all the way home in the lock. You laid the blind mark down; the true mark came on top of it stroke for stroke, and the burden ran down from one to the other through every place they touched. But if the mark on top was set a hair aside, or turned the smallest part of a degree, the strokes crossed the ghost instead of riding it. They touched at a point or two, and a point or two carried next to nothing.

“It’s still a witness-mark,” Ilse said. She had taken a sheet of the Mess accounts paper from the drawer under the bread-board and was ruling a signing box on it with the edge of a ladle. “You mean it. You attest with intent, so the Law of Attestation is satisfied and the sheet holds. But it isn’t quite your mark. It’s your mark telling a small lie about where it is.”

“That’s the bit Brask won’t like,” said Cass.

“That’s the bit Brask would take my head off for.” Ilse didn’t look up. Three weeks ago she had sat in Brask’s cottage with a cup of terrible tea and promised, and meant it, and the meaning of it had lasted twenty-two days. She drew the box. “And it costs. A mark that’s off the rule holds a little weaker than a true one. The building will believe us a little less. If the stretch has a bad night, we might lose a few links.”

“How many is a few?” said Teo.

“I don’t know. I never signed the Drift Wing before.”

She showed them: a sixteenth of an inch high of the rule, turned a little sunwise, as if the hand had been jogged. She did her own wren’s foot ten times skewed and once true, and the difference was so small that Teo had to bring the candle over to see it.

Cass got it in four tries. Her mark had always looked as though it had been made in a moving cart; a slightly different cart cost her nothing.

Teo could not do it.

He tried nine times. Each time his big hand set the pen down a sixteenth high, and each time, as the stroke went on, the pen crept back onto the rule by itself, until the mark sat dead true in its box, as if a plumb-line had drawn it.

“I can’t,” he said. He was sweating. “I’m sorry. It’s like trying to sing flat on purpose. My hand knows where it goes.”

“Honest all the way down to the fingers,” said Cass. “Terrible affliction.”

“Start your mark from the wrong end,” said Ilse. “You can’t lie with your hand. Fine. Don’t try to. Lie with the order.” She took his pen hand and turned it on the paper, gently, the way Quince had once turned hers. “Begin where you usually finish. Your hand won’t know where it is, so it won’t know where to go back to. It’ll be a hair out all on its own.”

He did it. The mark came out stiff and very slightly wrong, like a word spelled correctly in someone else’s handwriting.

“That’s horrible,” he said.

“It’s perfect.”

“That’s what’s horrible about it.”

* * *

Sorry had come down inside Ilse’s coat and slept through the confession in the sleeve. It came out now and sat among the instruments with its too-big ears turned toward the inkwell, a small grey cat because she had said so at the Ossel Fair, give or take an ear.

“Go on, then,” said Ilse, and tipped a little of the common black into a saucer from the accounts inkwell.

Sorry went at it as it went at everything it was allowed to eat, with its whole body. The saucer was clean in under a minute. It looked up for more with black whiskers.

Ilse uncorked the little bottle from her coat lining and held it out at the length of her arm.

The ossel recoiled. Its fur went up all along its spine, its ears went flat, and it backed across the table, scattering the alum, until its hindquarters met the teapot.

“Quicksilver,” said Cass softly. “They hate it. Every Tenting kid knows. You crack a levelglass in camp, you won’t see an ossel for a week.”

“Blind ink’s got a whisper of it.” Ilse corked the bottle and set it down at the far end of the table, and Sorry’s fur settled a little. Then she reached into the coat lining again and drew out the last thing, a sheet she had taken from the spoiled-folio basket in the Antechamber the night she found the lines: a blank Morning Survey form, never signed, its signing boxes empty and ruled in common black. She laid it flat beside the saucer.

Sorry wouldn’t go near it. It wouldn’t even look at it. It turned its back on the folio and sat facing the wall with its tail wrapped tight round its feet, like a dowager who has been seated next to the wrong person at dinner.

“It’s blank,” said Teo. “It ate my Mensuration notes.”

“There’s ink on it,” said Ilse. “We just can’t see it.”

The sheet lay on the scrubbed boards, cream-coloured with its neat empty boxes, as innocent a thing as Ilse had ever seen, and all three of them leaned a little away from it.

Teo stood up to fetch more cups from the rack. He was a large man in a small kitchen at two in the morning, and his elbow caught the hanging copper pans, and the biggest of them came off its hook and hit the flagstones with a noise like the Bell of Hours falling down a well.

Sorry went up.

It didn’t jump. It *rose*, and while it rose it changed, so quickly that Ilse’s eye couldn’t follow the joins. The cat went long and then longer. When it came to rest it was standing on the table among the instruments on two thin dark legs like reeds. It had a body the size of a hen’s, slate-grey and sleek, and a neck folded back on itself in a tight S, and a long dagger of a bill, and at the back of its narrow head two plumes lifted like ears that had remembered they were supposed to be feathers. One foot was drawn up under its breast. It turned its head and looked at the three of them out of eyes that were unmistakably Sorry’s, pale amber.

Nobody had said *heron*. Nobody had said anything at all.

It held the shape for the space of three breaths. Then the plumes softened, and the neck shortened, and the bird folded down into itself like a wet sheet settling over a chair. A small grey cat with ridiculous ears sat on the table beside the alum and began, with great concentration, to wash a paw.

Teo hung the pan back on its hook with enormous care.

“That,” he said, “was a bird.”

“It must have heard someone say it.” Ilse’s voice came out thinner than she liked. “Sometime. It kept it.”

Cass had come down off the bread-board. She stood with one hand at her route-cords, and her face had the look of someone who has walked a familiar road and found the river on the wrong side of it. “Ossels don’t do that,” she said. “They go to what you tell ’em, or they go to mush. They don’t go to something else. Nobody told it bird.”

Sorry finished its paw and started on the other one.

Teo poured the tea, which had stewed black, and they drank it anyway.

* * *

First Bell rang at a quarter past five, and they were standing at the foot of the Wren Stair with the chain when it did, because none of them had been to bed.

It was the first Morning Survey without Hen, and it came at Ilse sideways. Hen had always held the zero end of the chain at the stairfoot and called the counts in her loud Salt Steps voice, *tally one, and tally two, and that’s the tide in*, so that other parties on other stairs had learned to set their pace by her. This morning Teo held the zero end and counted under his breath, and the Wren Stair was so quiet that Ilse could hear the links ticking against the treads.

The stair had turned in the night by about four degrees, sunwise, as it had all week. The Long Gallery had stretched six links at the north end, where the boards always crept. Cass read the grain with her bare palm flat on the floor, and they argued about the north wall, and Cass was right. They closed the traverse at three chains forty-one links. Ilse drafted it on the plane-table in the grey light, in the fair hand Hallam called a draftswoman’s, and she made it true, because the drawing was the one part of this that could still be true.

The Register Antechamber smelled of size and lamp oil and was full of parties queuing with their sheets. Merrin Coyle sat behind his desk with his cuffs dark to the wrist with ink, and he looked up at them with his usual anxious pleasantness, and at the space where Hen should have been, and away.

“Wren Stair,” he said. “Three this morning. I’m sorry. Sign here, and here, and the party line.”

Ilse went first. She took the College pen from its stand, dipped it, and set her wren’s foot in the box a sixteenth of an inch high of the rule and turned a little sunwise, as if the hand had been jogged.

She had never felt the ghost-line when she signed. But this time, as the three strokes and the spur went down, she noticed the absence of what she must have been feeling every morning since Reaping without a name for it: a faint, steady draw through the nib, like the draught that comes under a door when another door is opened somewhere far off in the house. This morning there was no draught. The pen moved through the ink like a pen. Her mark lay on the sheet a little crooked and entirely hers.

Cass signed off the rule without looking, as if she had done it all her life. Teo took the pen and stood over the box, breathing through his nose, and then he began his mark at its end and worked backward to its beginning, and it came out stiff and a hair askew.

Coyle blotted the sheets without a glance and laid them on the pile.

* * *

The day was long. At noon Ilse sat by Hen in the infirmary for a quarter of an hour, and Hen said *hello, Ilse* and *yes* and *I don’t mind*, and turned her grey fingertips over on the blanket as if they belonged to someone else. In the evening she went down to the Register for her copying, and when Coyle had gone to his supper she took the Wren Stair sheet from the morning’s pile, breathed alum-water onto the lens and laid it over the signing boxes.

The violet knot came up fine as a hair on the rule, its thread running off toward the binding. It ran *past* her mark. Her wren’s foot sat a little above it and a little turned, and the line went by beneath it like a road beneath a bridge, touching at one point where the spur crossed it and nowhere else. Cass’s mark crossed it twice. Teo’s, backward and stiff, lay across it like a stick across a stream.

She put the sheet back where it had been, square to the pile, and put out the lamp.

The Wren Stair was dark when she climbed it, and it did not feel right underfoot. It never had, quite, but tonight the treads had a faint give in them, the softness of a step on a ship, and at the turn of the landing the banister moved under her hand by the width of a fingernail and came back. The stair had held. It had held the way a tired man holds a heavy door, with his whole weight against it and his arms shaking.

Teo was sitting on the top step in the dark with his knees drawn up, waiting for her. He didn’t ask. She nodded, and he let his head fall back against the wall.

Below them the Long Gallery creaked, settling, all eleven windows grey with frost.

“Marrow,” Teo whispered, as if the walls might hear him and take offence, “we just lied to the building.”
