# Chapter 18
## Drift-Fatigue

The infirmary had been built in the days when the College believed in fresh air, and it had never stopped believing in it. The long ward ran the whole north side of the Old Court under a row of tall windows, and at least three of them were always open a hand’s width, so that the curtains round the beds breathed in and out all day like sleepers. Winter light lay along the floor in long pale bars. It smelled of carbolic and barley water and, faintly, of the sea.

There were nine beds with their curtains drawn back, and five of them were full.

Ilse had counted them on the way in, because counting gave her eyes a job. Two second-years she knew by sight from the Mess. A furlong from the Third Range, a tall boy with a ring in one ear, sitting up against his pillows with a book open on his knees that he was not reading. Wil Ashdown in the chair by the far window, as he had been in Gleaning, with his hands in his lap and his face toward the glass, though there was nothing outside it but the grey back of the chapel. And Hen.

Hen was sitting up. Someone had brushed her hair and plaited it, which Hen never did; she wore it loose and in her eyes and blew it out of the way when she was thinking. The plait lay over her shoulder on the blanket like a rope somebody else had coiled there. She looked up when they came down the ward, and she knew them, which was something.

“Ilse,” she said. “Teo.”

“Hello, Hen.”

“Hello.”

Teo had brought a seed-cake. He had baked it on the spirit-stove in two halves, because the stove would only take a small tin, and stuck the halves together with jam, and he had talked about it all the way across the Old Court in a long anxious river of words about caraway and whether Salt Steps people even liked caraway, which he thought they did, because Hen had once eaten four slices of a caraway loaf at the Chain & Anchor and said it tasted of her nan’s kitchen. He set it on the locker beside her bed and took the cloth off it.

Hen looked at the cake.

“It’s cake,” she said.

“It’s seed-cake,” said Teo. “Caraway. It’s—you said once, at the Chain, your nan —”

“Thank you.”

She didn’t touch it. She didn’t look away from it either. She looked at it the way you might look at a stone in a field: correctly, without interest, as a thing that was there.

Ilse sat on the edge of the bed. Hen’s hands were folded on the blanket. The grey had come further since Frost 3. It was past the first joint of every finger on the right hand now, smooth and cool-looking, the colour of a slate roof after rain. The left hand was only faintly dusky at the tips, as if she had been handling pencil leads.

“Did the stair hold?” Hen asked.

“It held.”

“Good.” A pause. “Who is counting?”

“Teo.”

“Teo counts too fast on the landing. He should count slower on the landing.” She said it without any of the old mockery, as a correction to be filed. “You should let me sign. I’m quickest.”

Ilse’s throat closed. “You’re resting,” she said. “Orle says.”

“Yes,” said Hen. She looked at her hands. “I don’t mind.”

Teo sat on the other side of the bed and told her about the Mess. He told her that Absalom Grice had tried to carry four bowls of porridge at once and been defeated by the third. He told her that Mother Nettle had called the Provost a gilded weasel in front of three proctors, and that the crows had agreed with her. He told it well, in his best Lisk storyteller’s manner, leaning in at the good parts. Hen listened to all of it. At the end of each story she said “Oh,” in the tone of someone being told what time it was.

Ilse had once watched Hen laugh so hard at one of Cass’s stories that she had fallen off the end of a bench in the Mess and gone on laughing under the table, with her boots in the air. She looked at the plait on the blanket and thought about that, and then made herself stop, because it was no use to anyone.

When the bell for second lecture rang, Teo cut a slice of the cake and put it on a saucer near Hen’s hand, where she could reach it without deciding to. She thanked him again.

* * *

Dr. Sabeth Orle caught Ilse at the end of the ward with two fingers on her sleeve.

“Marrow. A minute.”

The dispensary was a narrow room behind the ward with shelves to the ceiling and a long deal table scrubbed white. Orle shut the door. She was a square, quick woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her greying hair pinned up anyhow with what looked like a pencil and a knitting needle, and she had the look, always, of someone who had been interrupted halfway through a sum.

“You’re her party. You live with her. You do the Morning Survey with her.” Orle didn’t sit, and didn’t ask Ilse to. “Tell me what she was like a month ago.”

Ilse told her. The laugh. The Salt Steps sayings, *tide’s out on that one*, which Hen used for everything from a bad joke to a dead mouse. How she had begun missing jokes in Fogmonth, and then stopped making them, and then started answering in short flat sentences, like a ledger read aloud. Orle listened with her arms folded and asked short questions. When did she stop. Which came first. Did she sleep. Did she eat. Was she left-handed.

“Right-handed.”

“They all are,” said Orle. “Nearly. Watch.” She went to a shelf and brought down a ledger bound in green cloth, and opened it on the table, and ran her finger down a column without letting Ilse see the rest of the page. “Grey begins on the right hand in every case but one. The one is Benet Lowe in the Third Range, who writes with his left, and his grey began on his left. It always starts in the fingertips of the hand they sign with. Then the palm. Then up the wrist. The other hand follows, slower, weeks behind.” She shut the ledger. “Do you know how Drift-fatigue presents, Marrow?”

“No.”

“Neither does anyone. I’ve been reading about it for two years. It doesn’t exist in a single text before the Provost said it out loud at assembly.” Orle’s voice stayed level, but she put her palm down flat on the green cover as if to stop it opening by itself. “Drift-sickness I know. Vertigo, nausea, lost time. It comes on in the Margin and goes away on held ground. This doesn’t go away. This is burden. It looks like burden, the way it comes on old surveyors who’ve signed too much land. Except these are children, and they haven’t signed anything but their Morning Survey sheets and a paving stone.” She looked at Ilse very straight. “And fatigue doesn’t choose a hand.”

Ilse said nothing. Her own right hand was in her coat pocket. She took it out and laid it on the deal table between them, palm down, because it seemed cowardly not to, and Orle glanced at it, pink and inky and whole, and back at her face.

“I wrote to the Surgeons’ Hall in Tessary,” said Orle. “Twice. In Fogmonth, and again when your friend came in. I asked whether anyone had seen burden presenting in the young without cause. The Provost came to see me on Frost 3.” She smiled without any humour at all. “Very charming. He admired my windows. He said that the College was at a delicate juncture with the Court and the Assembly, and that a letter from the Meridian’s infirmary to Tessary suggesting an unknown malady among our students would be read by people who wished us harm. He said he relied on my discretion. He said it twice, which is how I knew it was an order.”

“Have you stopped?”

“I’ve stopped sending them.” Orle tapped the ledger. “I haven’t stopped writing them.”

The window behind her looked out on the chapel wall, and a cairn-crow was walking along its coping with its grey neck stuck out, like a clerk looking for a lost file. Ilse watched it. She was thinking about the Law of Transfer and the violet line and the copyist’s key in her pocket, and about how much she could say to this woman without saying everything, and she found, as she had found with Teo and Cass, that the lines didn’t come apart neatly. You couldn’t lift one out without the whole drawing coming with it.

“You know something,” said Orle.

“I think,” Ilse said carefully, “that you should watch who signs the most.”

Orle’s eyes narrowed. “Signs what?”

“Anything. Morning Survey. Errata. Extra sheets when a party’s short. The ones who sign the most, the ones who are proud of being quick at it.” She heard her voice and made it steadier. “Hen signs more than the rest of our party put together. She always has.”

Orle looked at her. Ilse had the sensation, familiar from Brask, of being measured by somebody who knew to a hair how wrong her instruments could be.

“You won’t tell me how you know that.”

“Not yet.”

“But you’d tell me if you could.”

“Yes.”

“Hm.” Orle opened the ledger again, turned to a clean page at the back, and wrote a line in a fast, untidy doctor’s hand. Ilse couldn’t read it upside down. “You can visit her any hour you like. Any hour. If the porter argues, send him to me. And Marrow.” She looked up. “If you find out what this is, you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone in a good coat. I’ve five children in that ward, and I’m tired of being admired for my windows.”

* * *

Lisbet Arnaud came for Cass at four o’clock.

They were in the Fieldwork shed, cleaning chains, because it was Ilse’s afternoon to do it for Brask and Cass had come to keep her company and complain. The shed leaked at the north corner, where a bucket stood under a drip that rang every eleven seconds; Teo had timed it. The chains hung from pegs along the walls in their long dull loops, and the two of them were working down the length of one with rags and a pot of tallow, link by link, when the door opened and let in a slice of cold and the senior proctor.

Lisbet Arnaud was a fifth-year with a long, handsome, disapproving face and a voice that had been designed for reading notices. She looked at the chain, and at Cass’s blackened fingers, and at the bucket.

“Miss Tenter. The Provost would like to see you in his lodging. Now, if you please.”

Cass didn’t get up. “What for?”

“I’m sure he’ll tell you.”

“I’m sure he will. Asking you.”

Lisbet’s nostrils thinned. “There are questions,” she said, “concerning your movements in Fogmonth. The week of Brine End.”

The rag stopped in Cass’s hands.

Ilse stood up. “I’ll come.”

“You weren’t asked for.”

“I’m her party. I’ll wait outside.” She wiped her hands on her trousers and looked at Lisbet with the expression she had once used on Jem Rudd, the parish clerk, when he asked how she knew so much about the Tull boundary. “Unless the Provost would prefer that Miss Tenter walk across the Old Court alone with a proctor at her back while the whole First Range watches out of the windows. In which case I’m sure he’ll tell you.”

Lisbet looked at her a moment longer, and then, to Ilse’s surprise, something in the long face gave a little.

“Outside,” she said. “On the bench. And you don’t speak unless he sends for you.”

The Provost’s lodging was at the west end of the Old Court, behind a door with a brass knocker in the shape of a meridian arrow. Inside it was warm and quiet and carpeted so thickly that their boots made no noise. The passage had a long-case clock and a portrait of some long-dead Provost holding a pair of dividers like a man about to carve a joint. Lisbet knocked at a door at the end, and a voice said “Come,” and Cass went in without looking back, with her chin up and the route-cords swinging in her braids. The door closed behind her.

Ilse sat on the bench and listened.

The door was oak and the carpet ate sound, and she couldn’t hear the words, only the shapes of them. Steyne’s voice was low and pleasant and went on and on without pause, like a well-oiled wheel going downhill. Now and then a second man’s voice put in a short phrase, a proctor or a clerk. Then Cass’s voice, short. Then Steyne again, longer, and the word *College* came through the oak twice, and the word *generosity* once. Then Cass’s voice, very much louder, and one phrase in it as clear as if she had been standing in the passage: *“— been on that heath since before your Ninefold had boots —”*

Then nothing. The clock in the passage ticked. The dead Provost carved his joint.

Footsteps came along the passage from the inner stair, light and unhurried, and Ilse looked up and found Master Hallam standing over her with a sheaf of papers under his arm and his spectacles pushed up on his bald head. His gloves were dove-grey kid and fitted like skin.

“Miss Marrow,” he said, with real pleasure, and then, taking in the bench and the door and her face, more gently: “Ah. Waiting for Miss Tenter, I expect.”

“Yes, Master.”

“Don’t fret, my dear.” He shifted the papers to his other arm. “The Provost is a careful man, and careful men ask a great many questions so that they never have to answer one. I’ve told him myself that the Tenting have no more to do with these voids than the gulls do.” He looked at the oak door with an expression of mild, weary fondness, the look of a man regarding a sideboard he has bumped his shin on for thirty years. “He’ll listen eventually. He usually does, once he’s frightened himself sufficiently. Will you be in the Register on Thursday?”

“Yes, Master.”

“Good. I’ve some Salt Steps tithe maps that want a steadier hand than Coyle’s.” He smiled at her over the spectacles, which had slid down again. “Be kind to your friend tonight. It’s a cold thing to be made a stranger in your own house.”

He went on down the passage. Ilse watched him go, and was grateful, and sat with her hands between her knees and waited.

When Cass came out her face was the colour of tallow.

She walked straight past the bench without stopping, and Ilse got up and went after her down the passage and out into the Old Court, where the light was going and the frost was coming up white between the paving stones. Cass didn’t speak until they were halfway across. Then she stopped dead on the meridian line, square on the brass studs where nobody ever stood, and turned round.

“They think we’re unmaking the ground,” she said. “*We.*”

“What did he ask you?”

“Where my wagon winters. Who my nan is. How many in the Tenter wagon and how many in the Wainwright and do we *talk*, the clans.” Her voice was shaking, not with tears. “Whether it’s true the Tenting have songs for loosening ground. Whether I’d been to Brine End. I said I’d been to Corrow to see my nan, and that’s eight miles off, and he said *eight miles is not very far, Miss Tenter, for someone who walks as well as your people do*. Smiling. Like he was paying me a compliment.” She spat on the frost, neatly, beside the brass stud. “And that proctor writing it all down. Every word. And then he says the College has extended great *generosity* to my people by admitting me, and he’s sure I’ll want to repay it, and if I hear anything from my kin, anything at all, I’m to come and tell him. Like a ferret down a hole.”

“What did you say?”

Cass looked at her. The fury in her face was white and set.

“I said we’d been on that heath since before his Ninefold had boots, and I’d bring him word when the gulls did.” She turned and walked on. “Then I said thank you, Provost, like Nan taught me, because she says you thank a man for his bread even if he’s spat in it. Then I came out.”

* * *

There was a skylight above the top landing of the Wren Stair that the party had found in Gleaning, when the landing had drifted half a yard and brought a trapdoor into reach that had not been reachable before. Nobody had reported it. You stood on the banister, pushed up the hatch and pulled yourself through onto the leads, and there you were on the roof of the Second Range with the whole of the Drift Wing laid out round you in the dark.

Ilse came up after Cass with two blankets and Teo’s last bottle of cider. It was bitterly cold. The leads had frosted, and the roofs of the Wing ran away to the west in a long uneven spine of slate and chimney and observatory dome, each one furred white along its ridge. Behind them stood the Old Court, solid and gold-windowed. Before them the roofs simply carried on, out over the heath, getting lower and more crooked as they went, until the Third Range ended in the glass dome of the Observatory of the Tern, and beyond that there was only the Margin, grey under the stars, and then the dark.

Down on the heath a line of wayhorns was moving. Ilse could see them because the frost on their backs caught the starlight: twenty or thirty great pale shapes walking single file along the edge of the Margin, slow as a funeral. Every one of them carried its head turned the same way, toward the Cape, so that the branched antlers made a long row of identical shapes against the heath, like the teeth of a comb. They were pointing at the Old Court. At the held ground. At her.

Cass sat with her back against a chimney stack and drank from the bottle and passed it over.

“Nan’ll laugh,” she said, after a while. “When I tell her. She’ll laugh till she has to sit down. She always said the Big House’d blame us for the weather one day. She just thought it’d be rain.”

“Will you tell her?”

“Course I’ll tell her. I tell her everything. That’s what I’m here for.” Cass turned the bottle in her hands. “*Go and learn what the paper-walls know, then come home and tell us.* That’s what she said. I’ve been sending crows all term. What the food’s like. How you lot measure a room four times and still lose the stair. Hallam’s lectures. She liked the bit about the Laws. She said six was an odd number for laws, and was I sure I’d counted right.”

Ilse laughed, and then wasn’t sure she should have.

They sat. The cider was sharp and warmed nothing. Out on the heath the wayhorns went on walking with their heads turned toward the Cape, and the frost thickened on the blankets.

“At Brine End,” Ilse said, “Brask sang to the hollowing.”

Cass turned her head.

“It was coming across the heath for the ground we’d just attested. I touched the edge of it and lost five minutes. And Brask stood in front of it and sang, low, in a voice I’d never heard her use. And it went away.” She hesitated. “She told me not to say a word of it to the Provost. You’re not the Provost.”

“How did it go?”

Ilse tried. She had no voice to speak of, and she had heard it only once, but she had a good memory for shapes, and she hummed what she could: a slow low line that turned over on itself at the end, like a plough at the headland coming back the other way.

Cass was quiet for a moment. Then she sang it back, properly. Her voice was rough and true, and it went down where Ilse’s couldn’t, and it had words, in a Margin cant Ilse only half followed: something about a road and a ford and the ground being let go of. The second time round she sang it a little differently, a turn added at the end, and the third time differently again.

“That’s a turning-song,” she said. “Nan sings it at them. Every kid on the heath knows it. You don’t fight a hollowing. You sing so the ground’s going somewhere, and they don’t like ground that’s going somewhere.” She frowned at the dark. “Where’d a paper-wall like Brask learn that?”

“She grew up at Thessaly Ford.”

“Village people don’t sing it. Village people put up a fence and pray.” Cass took the bottle back. “Somebody taught her. Somebody Tenting. Somebody who trusted her.”

“You sang it three different ways.”

“It’s a song. You don’t sing a song the same way twice. Ground’s not the same twice.”

“But which is the right one?”

Cass looked at her as though she had asked which of the wayhorns was the right wayhorn.

“All of ’em,” she said. “It’s not *written*, Marrow. It doesn’t sit still and wait to be copied. You learn it by walking it with somebody who knows it, and then you know it, and then it’s yours, and it changes a bit, because you do.” She pulled the blanket tighter round her shoulders. Her breath went up white. “Nan’s got a hundred road-songs in her head, and maybe a thousand turns of them. Every route on the heath from here to Thessaly Ford. Your Register hasn’t got one of them. Your Steyne thinks we’re loosening the ground because he can’t find our paper. Never occurs to him we’ve got none. Never occurs to him that’s the *point*.”

Below them the Wing creaked in the cold, a long complaint passed from roof to roof. Ilse thought of the Register, seven levels of it cut down into the rock, every parish in the Reach pressed flat and filed and held. She thought of Hen’s plait lying on the blanket where someone else had put it.

“You paper-walls,” said Cass, softly, not unkindly, watching the wayhorns go, “think if it isn’t written, it isn’t there.”
