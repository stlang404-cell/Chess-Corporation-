# Chapter 19
## Corrow

The crow came into the loft at a quarter to seven on Frost 7 with frost on its wings and a length of string tied to its leg.

Ilse was there because Brask had sent her up with a box of chain-tallies for Mother Nettle to sort, and Cass was there because Cass was always there when she could be, sitting on the rafters with her legs dangling among the perches, talking to the birds in the Margin cant they seemed to prefer. The crow came in through the slot at the far end, beat twice, and landed on Cass’s knee as if it had been aiming for it all the way from the heath.

“Bugger me,” said Mother Nettle, from the floor. She was about a hundred and built like a bundle of kindling tied up in a shawl. “That’s not one of mine. That’s a heath bird. Look at the neck on it.” She squinted up. “Bugger the crows. They’re smarter than you lot, and they know where you sleep.”

Cass had gone very quiet. She untied the string from the crow’s leg with quick fingers and held it up to the grey light from the slot. It was a route-cord, like the ones in her braids: plain brown twine knotted along its length in a pattern of single knots and doubles and one strange looped hitch near the end, and a single red thread worked through it at the last knot.

“Who’s it from?” said Ilse.

“Aunt Bet.” Cass ran the cord through her fingers, knot by knot, the way Teo ran a column of figures, and her lips moved. “Corrow. Ground going. Wagons on chocks. Two days.” Her thumb stopped on the red thread. “Come.”

* * *

Brask was in the Fieldwork shed, sitting on an upturned bucket with her pipe cold in her teeth and a field-book open on her knee. She listened to Cass without looking up, and when Cass had finished she went on looking at the field-book for so long that Ilse began to think she hadn’t heard.

Then she shut it.

“Corrow is eight miles south-east,” she said. “Out past the salt-pools and over the low downs. A Margin settlement under active unravelling. It wants surveying by someone who can tell me what’s happening to it.” She took the pipe out of her mouth. “That’s a field exercise. Three students. Tenter, because she knows the ground. Marrow, because she can draw. Anselm, because he can count and because if I leave him here he’ll bake something for the Quiet ward and set fire to the Second Range.”

“Field-Surveyor,” said Cass. “Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me. Report on my desk by noon on the ninth.” Brask got up off the bucket. She went to the shelf at the back and came down with a wicker crow-basket, the small kind that held one bird. “Take one of Nettle’s. If you need the Register, send to me, not to anyone else. And Marrow.” The one eye found her. “You’re going to watch, measure and write it down. You’re not going to sign anything. Not a sheet, not a scrap, not your name in the dirt.”

“Yes, Field-Surveyor.”

Brask held her look a moment longer than was comfortable, the way she did when she was waiting to see whether a measurement would hold. Then she turned to Cass.

“Tell Ottery Tenter,” she said, “that Odile Brask asks after her.”

Cass stared. “You know my nan?”

“Everyone on the heath knows your nan.” Brask put the pipe back in her teeth. “Go on. You’re losing light.”

* * *

They walked it in a little under four hours, with Cass in front reading the grain and Teo at the back with the crow-basket held out from his body as if it might go off.

It was a hard white day with no wind. The salt-pools had skins of ice on them that sang faintly when a stone skittered across, and past them the low downs had the Margin’s habit of not quite agreeing with themselves. Twice Cass put her palm flat on the frozen turf and changed direction for no reason Ilse could see. Ilse paced and kept her own rough traverse from habit, and by the third mile her numbers and Cass’s route disagreed by nearly a furlong, and Cass was the one who was right.

They came over the last down at two in the afternoon and saw Corrow below them, and Teo said, “Oh,” in a small voice, and stopped.

It lay in a broad shallow bowl of turf and gorse with a stream at the bottom of it and a single standing stone on the rise at the far side, taller than a man and leaning a little, black against the white sky. Eleven wagons were drawn up in a loose ring on the bowl’s floor. They were the high-sided Tenting wagons Ilse had seen at the Ossel Fair, every one of them painted along its boards with route-diagrams in red and ochre and blue, roads that wound and forked and came back on themselves, little suns and moons at the forks, and Ilse’s first thought, before anything else, was that she wanted a closer look at the paintwork.

Her second thought was that the wagons were not where they had been.

Each wagon stood on chocks, wedges of timber jammed under its wheels to hold it. Behind each wagon, trailing away uphill toward the west, lay a line of other chocks. They had been set under the wheels and abandoned, and set again and abandoned again, so that every wagon had a little dotted track of wooden wedges strung out behind it across the turf like the footprints of something that walked very slowly. The longest of the tracks had nine chocks in it. Between the first and the last there was perhaps twenty yards.

The floor of the bowl was sliding downhill toward the stream, and taking the camp with it.

It went at about the pace of a clock’s minute hand, too slow to watch and too quick to ignore. A cooking fire on the near side had walked a yard out of its ring of hearthstones and left a black scar behind it, and its smoke went up crooked. A washing line strung between two wagons had gone so taut that its pegs had popped, and the shirts lay in the turf in a row. A dog lay asleep beside a wheel, and as Ilse watched the ground carried dog and wheel together a hand’s breadth toward the stream, and the dog did not wake.

Her level was making a sound she had not heard it make before. It wasn’t the dropped silence of the Little Wick lane or the shriek of the hollowing at Brine End. It was a low, steady, sliding hum, like a held note with the bow drawn very slowly across it.

“It’s all right,” said Cass. She was looking down at the camp with her jaw set. “It’s slow. Slow’s all right. Slow you can walk off.” She started down the slope. “Come on. Nan’ll want to feed you, and you’ll want to let her. That’s rules.”

* * *

Nan Ottery Tenter was the smallest grown woman Ilse had ever seen, and the loudest.

She came down the steps of the biggest wagon backward, the way old people come down ladders, wrapped in so many shawls that she looked like a heap of washing with a pipe stuck out of the top. Then she turned round, and Ilse saw a face like a walnut, brown and seamed and sharp, with two black eyes in it as bright as a crow’s. She looked at her granddaughter. Then she opened her mouth, and a voice came out of it like a field gate on a dry hinge, rusty and enormous and impossible to ignore.

“Cassia Tenter! Look at the *state* of you! Have they not got soap in the Big House? Have they not got *food*?”

“Hey-ho, Nan.”

“Don’t hey-ho me.” Nan seized Cass by both ears and pulled her face down to her own level, which was a long way, and kissed her hard on the forehead and then on each eye. Then she let go and looked past her at Ilse and Teo and the crow-basket. “And these are the paper-walls.”

“Ilse Marrow. Teo Anselm. My party.” Cass rubbed an ear. “Brask sent us. Field exercise. And Brask says to tell you Odile Brask asks after you.”

Something happened in the walnut face, too quick to read.

“Does she,” said Nan. “Does she now.” She took the pipe out of her mouth and pointed the stem at Ilse. “You. You’re the one that draws.”

“Yes.”

“And him?”

“He counts,” said Ilse, “and cooks.”

Nan looked Teo up and down, slowly, all of him.

“Thank the Road for that,” she said. “At least one of you has sense. Come and eat.”

They ate in the lee of the big wagon round a fire that had to be re-laid twice while they sat at it, because the ground kept carrying it off. Nobody seemed to mind. When the fire had drifted a yard, a boy of ten called Tobin, one of Cass’s cousins, would get up with a shovel and lift the embers back into the hearthstones without being told, the way a Fallowmarch child would put a log back on a grate. The stew was salt mutton and barley and something green and bitter that Cass said was heath-kale, and there was a black flat bread baked on a stone, and the argument started before the first bowl was empty.

It was about bread. Teo, who was from Lisk and could not help himself, said something about leavening, and Nan took him apart for a quarter of an hour with the whole fire joining in. Lisk bread was air in a coat. Lisk bread was so proud of itself it rose to look at its own reflection. Teo defended his city, going redder and redder, and conceded at last that the black bread was *structurally superior for travel*, which made Aunt Bet laugh so hard she had to put her bowl down.

Ilse ate, and the ground slid gently under her, and she was warm for the first time since Frost began.

When the bowls were empty Nan filled her pipe, and Tobin lit it for her with a coal held in a pair of tongs, and she sat back against the wagon wheel and looked at Ilse through the smoke.

“So,” she said. “The Big House sends a girl who draws to look at our ground going. And what’ll you draw, girl?”

“Where it is,” said Ilse. “Where it’s moving to. How fast.”

“And then?”

“And then Brask reads it.”

“And then she puts it in a drawer.” Nan blew smoke. “Paper. You lot and your paper. You think if you draw a thing flat enough it’ll lie down and stay.” She tapped the pipe stem against her teeth. “We don’t keep paper on the heath. Paper says where a thing is. Song says where a thing *goes*.”

“I don’t understand.”

“No. Course you don’t.” Nan looked at Cass. “What’ve they been teaching her up there?”

“Six laws,” said Cass. “All about sitting still.”

“Ha.” Nan settled her shawls, closed her eyes, and without any warning at all began to sing.

It was not a pretty voice. It was the gate on its dry hinge, rusty and huge, and it should not have been able to carry a tune, but it did, the way an old cart carries a load. The song was slow and went in long lines, and Ilse caught perhaps one word in three of the cant, but she caught enough.

*The road to Corrow goes by the high stone when the moon is new,*
*and the low ford when it’s old.*
*Forty paces from the gorse that leans to sunrise.*
*When the rain’s been three days on the down, go by the sheepfold wall,*
*and count your forty from the wall and not the gorse.*
*When the stream’s loud, keep it on your left hand.*
*When the stream’s quiet, it has moved; go up and find it.*

There was more: verses for snow, and for the dry end of Haysel, and for when the high stone itself had walked, which it apparently did every few years. Ilse sat with her bowl going cold in her hands and listened, and somewhere about the fourth verse the hair began to stand up on her arms.

It had distances in it. Forty paces. Three days. It had bearings: sunrise, the left hand, the leaning gorse. It had conditions, *when* and *if* and *unless*, set out as carefully as the clauses of a charter. It was a survey. It was a survey of a route made by people who had looked at a road that would not stay still and, instead of trying to nail it down, had measured the ways it moved and the reasons for them, and written *those* down, in the only ink that wouldn’t dry.

It was a map. It was a map that moved, and it was *right*. She had just walked four hours across this ground and watched Cass turn for no reason and arrive at the right place, and the reason had been in the song all along.

Nan stopped. The fire had drifted again. Tobin got up with his shovel.

“There,” said Nan, opening her eyes. “That’s where Corrow goes. Now write it down flat and see if it stays.”

Ilse could not answer. Teo was looking at her, and she knew her face was doing something she could not stop.

* * *

It was full dark, and the fire had been carried off and brought back four more times, when Nan told them about the old man.

“Came two nights back,” she said. “Late, past moonrise. On foot from the road, with a lantern, very slow, picking his way. College man, by his coat. Old as me nearly, white beard, nice voice. Polite as a parson. He asked would someone show him the way to the Corrow stone, and Tobin took him up.” She jerked her chin toward the rise, where the standing stone was a black finger against the stars. “Gave the boy a silver mark for it.”

“He did,” said Tobin, from the dark. “A whole one.”

“Stood up there a long while by the stone, Tobin says. Talking to it, quiet, like a man settling a horse. Took his gloves off to touch it.” Nan drew on the pipe. “Grey hands, he had. Grey as that stone. Tobin saw ’em in the lantern. Then he put his gloves back on and thanked the boy, and thanked me on his way down, very kind, and went off to the road. And the next night the ground let go.”

Ilse was very cold. It started in her signing hand and went up the arm into her chest and stayed there.

*A gentle old man with grey hands.* She thought: half the old surveyors in the Reach have grey hands. Brask’s own left hand hung at her side like a dead bird. The College was full of white-bearded men in good coats who had signed too much land, and any one of them might come out to the Margin to look at a standing stone. She thought of the Provost’s passage, two days ago, and a kind voice saying that the Tenting had no more to do with the voids than the gulls did. She thought of gloves the colour of a dove’s breast, and of the words *old Sallow frostbite*, and she put that thought down, very carefully, in a drawer at the back of her mind, and shut the drawer.

She did not count back the days. She was a surveyor. She knew how not to take a measurement.

“Did he say why?” she asked, quite ordinarily.

“Said he was looking at the old benchmark. Seeing it was sound.” Nan’s black eyes were on her, bright as a crow’s. “That stone holds the paper for Corrow. Did Cass tell you we’ve got paper?”

Cass hadn’t. Nan sent Tobin into the big wagon, and he came back with a tin box that had once held Lisk tea, and Ilse felt her throat close, because it was the same kind of tin as the one behind the loose brick in Hobbet. Inside, folded in oilcloth, was a charter. It was a College grant, 1190 H., confirming to the Tenter wagon the winter ground of Corrow under the old Heath Treaty, *the bowl, the stream and the rise unto the standing stone*, with a College seal and a Master’s mark and, at the bottom, a smudged brown thumbprint.

“Mine,” said Nan. “Only paper I ever put my thumb to. My mother wouldn’t, and she was right, but I was young and the winters were hard, and the Big House said this way no lord’s surveyor could ever walk us off it.” She laughed her gate-hinge laugh. “Thirteen years it held.”

Ilse unfolded the charter on her knee and read it by the firelight, every line. It was in order. The measurements were sound, the seal was sound, the mark was sound. It was a perfectly good copy of a perfectly good charter, and the land it described was sliding gently downhill into the stream.

She had seen this at Brine End.

She tore a leaf from her field-book and wrote on it, small, by the fire: *Corrow, Tenter winter ground, College grant 1190 H. Parish copy in order. Please ask the Register for the master. I. M.* She rolled it, and Teo held the basket while she tied it to the leg of Mother Nettle’s crow, and Cass carried the bird to the edge of the firelight and threw it up into the dark. It went north-west without circling, straight as a chalk line, toward the fixed stone of the Cape.

The answer came back before the moon was up. The crow dropped out of the black onto the wagon step and stood there fluffing its neck while Ilse untied the paper. Brask’s hand, square and impatient:

*Not in the Register. Not misfiled. Missing. Keep them moving. B.*

Ilse read it twice and handed it to Cass. Cass read it and handed it to her grandmother, who could not read and did not pretend to. Nan held the paper to the fire for a moment, looking at the shape of the words, and then she gave it back.

“Rent’s coming due,” she said.

Cass turned on the step. Her face in the firelight was hard and young and frightened.

“You always say that, Nan. Since I was little. What does it *mean*?”

Nan put her pipe back in her mouth. She looked at her granddaughter for a long moment, and then past her, up the rise, to where the Corrow stone leaned against the stars.

“What it says,” she said.

* * *

They moved the camp that night, because the moon was old and the song said the low ford.

Ilse had imagined a scramble. It was nothing like that. The Tenting had been packed for two days. The horses were put to in a quarter of an hour, and the chocks were knocked out and thrown into the wagon-beds, and the whole camp simply rolled forward off its sliding ground, eleven wagons in file with a lantern hung at each tail, and Nan sitting up on the box of the first with the reins in her small brown fists.

She sang them out. Aunt Bet sang behind her, and Cass beside the second wagon with her hand on the near horse’s neck, and then the whole line had picked it up, the low long lines going back and forth along the column like a rope passed hand to hand. *The low ford when it’s old.* *Forty paces from the gorse.* *When the stream’s quiet it has moved.*

Teo led the third wagon’s horse, a patient grey mare, and counted under the singing, because he could not help it. Ilse walked beside him with a lantern.

The road moved.

She felt it within the first furlong. The track down the bowl and along the stream was a track one moment and a stretch of gorse the next, and the gorse had a path through it that had not been there, and the stream that had been on their left was suddenly ahead of them, in the wrong place, quiet. *When the stream’s quiet, it has moved; go up and find it.* Nan turned the first wagon uphill without a pause, and the line followed, and a hundred yards on there was the stream again, loud, exactly where the song said it would be, on their left hand. Her level hummed and slid and changed its note, over and over, but it changed in a pattern, rising and falling with the land like a voice with a tune.

In the Little Wick lane, when the ground let go, she had thought the world was ending. She had learned everything she knew in a country where land that moved was land that had been wronged. She waited for the fear to come up through the soles of her boots.

It didn’t come.

She walked a road that would not stay where it was put, in the dark, among people singing it, and with every step it went somewhere, and every time it went somewhere the song had already been there. The lantern swung in her hand. The grey mare’s breath smoked. Teo counted, *eleven hundred and forty, eleven hundred and forty-one*, and the hum in her ears rose and fell, and the stars over the Margin rocked very slightly at the edge of the world, and she was not afraid. She was something she didn’t have a word for yet. She was *listening*.

They came to the low ford a little before dawn. The ground there was firm, as firm as the Margin ever was, and when the wagons had been drawn into their ring and the chocks set and the horses rubbed down, the first grey light came up over the downs and showed the stream running quiet and shallow over its stones exactly where it belonged.

Nan climbed down off the box, backward, and stood with her hands in the small of her back.

“There,” she said, to nobody in particular. “Now it can go where it likes.”

Ilse sat down on the wagon step with the lantern still lit in her hand. In her pocket, she knew without looking, her careful traverse of the night’s route made no sense at all. It ran into the stream twice and through a hill once and ended a furlong from where she was sitting.

She didn’t take it out. She sat in the cold dawn and listened to Cass and Aunt Bet arguing about where to put the fire, and to Teo telling Tobin, very seriously, the exact number of paces from Corrow to the ford, and under all of it, in the turf beneath her boots, the long slow hum of the ground, going on.
