# Chapter 20
## The Moot

The Moot-stone had been in the Old Court for three hundred and eleven years, and in all that time it had never once been where it wanted to be.

It sat in the south-east corner of the quadrangle, in a sunken square one rod on a side, edged with brass and filled two feet deep with pale grey sand carted up every autumn from the strand below Brine End. It was a block of black basalt two feet long and a foot each way, three hundredweight or near it, its corners worn soft by three centuries of first-years. At each corner of the bed a brass stud stamped with a benchmark arrow was set into the paving. Everything inside the brass edge was Margin ground in a Held Side courtyard, a little patch of give in the middle of all that certainty, and the stone sat on it like a dog on a hearthrug that is not its own.

By eight on the morning of Frost 10 the whole College was in the Old Court to watch it move.

They had put up benches in tiers on three sides of the bed, with braziers between, and the smoke hung flat in the frosty air at the height of the second tier, so that the leagues on the top rows looked down through a faint blue haze like gods on a painted ceiling. The links sat in front with their instruments in their laps; the Masters sat on a dais at the west side. The brass studs of the meridian line, where they crossed the court, each wore a little cap of frost.

Magistra Ines Calder stood at the front of the dais with a sandglass in one hand and the Moot urn under her other arm. She waited until the noise had died. Then she waited a good while longer, until it had died again.

“First-years,” she said. “The Concordance Moot.”

“The rules are four, and I shall state them once. First. You will survey the stone and its bed from the four corner studs and from nowhere else. Second. You will have one glass to measure and one glass to draft.” She touched the sandglass. “A glass is a quarter of an hour. It does not care how clever you are. Third. You will chart the stone to any position within its latitude, and declare that position by setting your pin at the place your charter says its south-west corner will lie. Its latitude is about a yard in any direction, on its face. You may not chart it on end. You may not chart it out of the bed. You may not chart it into the Provost’s pocket, however much you may wish to.”

Laughter, in the tiers. The Provost smiled gracefully, as a man smiles at a joke he will remember.

“Fourth. At my word, you will attest together. The stone will go where the better map says. That is not my ruling. It is the ground’s. I merely announce it.” She paused. “Before each bout, the winner of the last will release their charter, so that the stone is free for the next. You will learn to let go of a thing before you learn to win one. That is the most useful lesson the Moot has to offer and very few of you will notice it.”

“You will remember from my lectures that the record of greater fidelity prevails, and age prevails only between equals. You may remember the case. *Saffery v. the Parish of Wick*, 1193.” She let the name lie in the cold air for a moment. “A newer survey, better made, superseded a parish map four hundred years old. Fidelity, ladies and gentlemen, not age. Chart well.”

Ilse, on the front bench, sat very still with her instruments in her lap.

She knew the case. She had known it since she was thirteen, though not by that name. In the Fallowmarch they called it the year Fask came. Her father’s name was nowhere in the title; he had only been the man standing at the window. She had not expected to hear it said in that clear Tessary voice, in a courtyard five hundred and sixty miles from the Long Field, as an example of the law working properly. She found that she had closed her right hand round the brass tube of her levelglass hard enough to hurt.

Calder drew the names.

She drew them in pairs, with a pause between the first name and the second that the whole College leaned into. *Grice*, and *Anselm*. *Mercer*, and *Tenter*. A clerk chalked each pair on a board by the dais, and the list grew to twenty-eight, and Ilse’s name was not on it.

There were two names left in the urn. Ilse knew whose the other one was before Calder put her hand in, because she could feel him three benches along, sitting very straight with his notebook closed on his knee.

“Reyne,” said Calder.

She took a long time over the pause. The tiers took it with her.

“And Marrow.”

The noise that went up from the benches was the sound a crowd makes when it has been given exactly what it wanted and is a little ashamed of wanting it. Ilse didn’t look at him. The clerk’s chalk squeaked the last pair onto the bottom of the board, where it would wait for her all day.

* * *

The bouts ran from half past eight until the light went, and Ilse watched every one.

Everyone else watched the duelists, because duelists were people and people were interesting. Ilse watched the stone.

The first thing she saw was that most of the winners did the same thing. They measured the stone exactly where it lay, as carefully as they could, and charted it there, and set their pin against its south-west corner. Calder called it *the standing claim* in a voice that suggested it had bored her every year since she was a girl. A stone charted where it already lay was a record of what was, and a record of what was had no lie in it to strain against, so nine bouts in ten it beat anyone who tried to move the stone any distance at all. Absalom Grice tried to drag it a foot and a half north toward his own side of the bed, with a charter that was all ambition and no arithmetic, and Teo stood his standing claim against him and won without the stone so much as quivering. Teo came off the bed scarlet, sat down beside Ilse and put his head between his knees for a while.

The second thing she saw was smaller.

Before every bout the last winner drew the line through their mark that unsigned their charter, and the stone was free. And every time, before the next pair began to measure, the stone *settled*. Nobody else was looking. A sigh in the sand at its south-west corner, a few grains trickling into the gap that opened along its north-east side. It moved about the thickness of a fingernail, and it moved the same way every time.

At the eleventh bout, on her way to the brazier, she pressed a pin into the sand an inch off the stone’s south-west corner where no one would notice it. By the twentieth bout the gap had closed by the width of a straw.

The stone was going somewhere. It went there a fingernail at a time, in the moments when nobody held it, and then the next first-year measured it where it stood and nailed it down again.

Cass’s bout was the twenty-first, against Piety Mercer.

Piety took the standing claim. She measured with the pursed concentration of a girl who has had a drawing-master since the age of six, and her charter was a pretty thing with a ruled border. Cass did not take the standing claim. She walked once round the brass edge of the bed with her palm held flat a few inches over the sand, as she did on the heath, and then knelt at the south-west corner and put her hand right down into it, and the tiers laughed. Then she measured, badly, and drafted, worse, and set her pin five inches south-west of the stone in a slightly crooked line.

“Ground’s leaning,” she said to Calder, when Calder raised an eyebrow at the pin. “Can’t you feel it?”

“I am not required to feel it, Miss Tenter. I am required to announce it.”

They attested. Ilse felt the two records go on the stone like two hands on a rope, and the stone *moved*. It shuddered in its bed, toward Cass’s pin; sand hissed at its corner; for a long moment it hung there trembling with the tiers gone silent. Then it sank back where it had been, and Piety’s pretty charter held it.

“The record of greater fidelity prevails,” said Calder. “Miss Mercer.”

Cass came off the bed with her face like thunder and threw herself down on the bench between Ilse and Teo.

“Ground wanted to come to me,” she said. “You saw it. It *wanted* to. Her bloody paper held it back.”

“Your paper let it,” said Ilse.

“Eh?”

“You were right. You were right about where it wanted to go. Your charter couldn’t carry it.” Ilse was looking at the stone. “Your south face was a link out. Your corner angles didn’t close. The ground believed you and the map didn’t.”

Cass glared at her. Then she stopped glaring, and looked at the stone, and back at Ilse, slowly.

“Road and rain,” she said. “You’ve got that face on.”

Ilse didn’t answer. She was working it through the way Brask had taught her, measurement first and then the ways she might be wrong.

Kasimir would take the standing claim. He was the best draftsman in the year by a distance she could not make up in a quarter of an hour, and his record of the stone would be so exact that the ground would have nothing to argue with. If she took the standing claim against him she would lose on precision. If she dragged the stone anywhere, she would be telling the ground a lie, and he would beat a lie with the truth.

But the stone did not lie where it lay because the ground had put it there. It lay there because Piety Mercer’s charter had put it there, and Orla Devane’s before that, and someone’s before that, all the way back through three hundred and eleven years of first-years setting it down wherever they had happened to find it. The ground had never been asked. Every time it was let go, for the space of a breath, it tried to tell them. A fingernail at a time.

A standing claim was a perfect record of where a piece of paper had put a stone. It was not a record of the ground.

She thought of Brask’s promise. *No more lies on any College sheet.* She was breaking it every morning in the Antechamber. She asked herself whether a charter of the stone somewhere it had never been was another lie, and turned it over as she would have turned a client’s hedge, and could not make it come out a lie however she held it.

The light began to go a little after four. The clerks lit lanterns round the brass edge of the bed, and the braziers glowed red in the blue dusk. Calder said, “Reyne. Marrow,” and the tiers went quiet all at once.

* * *

Piety Mercer released her charter with an expression of martyred piety, and the stone settled its fingernail’s width, and Ilse was the only one who saw.

Calder turned the glass.

Kasimir went to work at once, and it was beautiful. Ilse watched him out of the edge of her eye, the way you watch a good horse go past. He had a folding rule of black boxwood, brass beam-compasses and a sightvane whose hair-wires caught the lantern light like spider-silk, and he went from stud to stud sighting the stone’s corners and writing the angles in that clear, slanting hand. He measured each face of the block twice. He took the height at all four corners to the hundredth of a link. In the time it took Ilse to kneel at the south-west stud he had sighted two corners from two stations and closed them.

Ilse did not touch the stone.

She laid her levelglass flat on the sand a foot north of it, and watched the bead of quicksilver. It swam, lazily, round and round, as it always did on Margin ground. She moved the glass a foot east. The bead swam. A foot south. It swam. She set it down by the south-west corner, just off the stone, and the bead slowed, and wandered, and came to rest, and trembled there.

Piety’s voice carried from the tiers: “She’s surveying the *sand*.”

Ilse pressed her bare knuckles into the sand along the stone’s south-west side. It was soft there, loose and dry. On the north-east side it was packed hard, a ridge of it, like the bank a boat pushes up against a quay. She lowered her cheek almost to the brass edge and looked along the bed in the lantern light, and saw faint ripple lines in the sand, like a beach when the tide has gone out, all curving round the stone toward its south-west corner. The sand was not still. It had never been still. It had been creeping under the stone for three hundred years, and every year a first-year had stood on its back and told it to stop.

She set three fine pins in the sand along the stone’s south-west edge, each a hair’s breadth from the basalt, and sat back on her heels for a precious minute of the glass, doing nothing, while the tiers laughed.

The gap at the middle pin closed. Very slowly, the stone came to it, until pin and basalt touched.

That was the direction. The bead told her where it ended: three inches south-west of where the stone now lay, and turned by perhaps two degrees, sunwise, as the ripple lines turned. There the bead stopped swimming. There the sand would hold the stone without being made to. It was the lowest place in the bed, if *low* meant what the level meant by it: the place where the ground was most at ease.

Half the glass was gone. She got up and measured the stone.

She could not do it as Kasimir did. But she had nine years of measuring hedges by shuttered lantern, and she knew how to measure a thing quickly and know exactly how wrong she was. She took the stone’s faces with her folding rule and its corners from each of the four studs with her vane, and wrote each measurement down with its error beside it in small figures, as Halloway had drummed into them: *two feet and three-tenths of an inch, give or take a fiftieth*. The sand ran out in the glass as she wrote down the last angle.

“Draft,” said Calder, and turned it.

She drew the stone where it was going to be: three inches south-west and two degrees sunwise, every face to its measured length, every corner closed, the ripple lines drawn in fine dashes round it to show which way the bed ran. At the foot she wrote the bearing and the rate she had seen, small: *settling south-west by about one-fiftieth of an inch in a quarter-hour when unheld*. She didn’t know whether a charter was supposed to say a thing like that. She wrote it anyway, because it was true, and because something at the back of her mind, a voice like Nan’s gate-hinge and Halloway’s stammer at once, said it belonged there.

When the glass ran out she walked to the edge of the bed and set her pin. Red ribbon, in the sand, three inches south-west of the stone’s corner.

Kasimir set his on the far side. Blue ribbon, touching the basalt exactly at its corner.

Somebody at the back called out, “Is that all, Walker?” and Cass told him, loudly and in some detail, where he could put his chain.

Kasimir looked at the red pin.

He looked at it for a long time, and then at Ilse, and she saw him searching it for the trick: the measurement shaved, the angle nudged. He could not find it, because it was not there, and she saw for the first time the faint crease that came between his brows when a sum would not come out. Then he squared his charter on his board.

Calder raised her hand and held it there a long while.

“Attest,” she said.

Ilse set her wren’s foot on the sheet.

It went down true, on its own rule, as it had not gone down on any College paper in a week. And the moment the spur was made she felt the stone take hold of both of them.

It was like having a rope run through her right hand with someone strong hauling on the other end. Kasimir’s record went onto the basalt like iron, laying itself along every face. *Here. Exactly here, to the hundredth of a link. As it is.* Her own record went on beside it, and it didn’t feel like anything she had ever signed. In Agnes Tull’s orchard she had leaned her whole attention into the map and argued the hedge west, persuading, insisting, until the ground gave. There was nothing to argue here. The ground was already on her side of the rope. It had been on her side for three hundred years. All she had done was write down what it had been saying.

Her level rose to a high, hard note. The stone groaned.

It was a deep grinding sound, more felt than heard, like a millstone turning over once in its sleep. Sand hissed up in a little bow-wave along its south-west face and spilled over. The tiers made no noise at all. The black block shifted in its bed, slowly, almost thoughtfully, and slid. One inch. Two. It turned as it went, its long face swinging a degree, two degrees, sunwise, and the sand curled round its corner in the lantern light like water round a moored boat’s bow. Three inches. Its south-west corner came to rest against the red pin with a small, final click of basalt on steel.

The high note in Ilse’s ears let go. Her whole right arm ached to the shoulder.

On the far side of the bed, Kasimir’s charter had gone dead in his hands. She could see it from where she stood: the sheet hung in his fingers like any sheet of paper, holding nothing, and he was looking down at it as if it had died while he held it.

Then the tiers went up like a flock of rooks.

Calder lowered her hand into the noise and waited for it, as she had waited for everything, and in the end it came down to her.

“The record of greater fidelity prevails,” she said. “Miss Marrow.” She turned her head toward Kasimir. Her voice was not unkind. “Fidelity to the ground, Mr. Reyne. Not to the page.”

Cass was on her feet on the bench, whistling through her teeth. Teo was laughing into his hands. On the dais the Masters applauded: Halloway with his three watches jingling, Moy with great slow claps like a man splitting logs, the Provost with the gracious economy of someone applauding a result he has not yet decided how to use.

Master Hallam had stood up.

He applauded with his gloved hands, softly, so the grey kid made almost no sound, and he did not stop when the others stopped. He was looking at her over his half-moon spectacles. It was a long look, kind and attentive and interested, the look of a man who has seen a pupil do something he did not know she could do, and wants to understand it. He went on looking for long enough that Ilse, who was smiling back at him, began to feel her smile set on her face like plaster. Then he nodded to her, very warmly, and sat down.

Ilse turned away from the dais and found Brask.

She was at the very edge of the crowd, under the arch that led toward the Lantern Gate, standing apart as she always stood. The lantern light didn’t quite reach her. Ilse could see only the shape of her, tall and rawboned, leaning on the Long Lie, and the dull gleam of the brass shoe on its end. Their eyes met across the whole roaring court.

Brask took the pipe out of her mouth.

“Hm,” she said, and put it back, and turned, and went away under the arch.

* * *

The tiers emptied slowly into the dark. Ilse stayed by the bed, because the stone was hers now, for a year. She knelt by the brass edge in the last of the light and laid her hand flat on the cold basalt. It sat in its new place as though it had always been there. The sand lay round it smooth and quiet, and when she put the levelglass down beside it the bead did not swim.

She heard him before she saw him. He walked the way he always walked, as if the paving owed him an explanation.

Kasimir stopped on the other side of the bed with his dead charter rolled tight in one hand. In the light of the one lantern the clerks had left he was white to the lips, and very composed.

“How,” he said.

It was not quite a question. She answered it anyway.

“You charted where it was. I charted where it was going.”

“You charted the stone in a position it did not occupy.”

“It does now.”

“It does now because you charted it.” His voice was perfectly level and each word came out complete, like coins being counted onto a table. “You put it somewhere it had not been, and you made the ground agree with you. I have seen you do it before, Miss Marrow. I spent three days on my knees by a hawthorn hedge in Wick Fallow watching the evidence of you doing it.”

“That’s not what this was.”

“Then tell me what it was.”

“The sand moves.” She heard her voice rising and brought it down. “It’s moved all day. It’s moved for three hundred years. Every time somebody let go of the stone it went that way a fingernail, and the next first-year nailed it back. I watched it. I measured the creep. I wrote the rate on the sheet. Every line on that charter is true, Mr. Reyne. Check it. Check it to the hundredth of a link with your beautiful compasses. You won’t find a line I moved.”

“I know.” He said it very quietly. “I have already looked.”

The lantern hissed. In the dark beyond the arch the Lantern Gate’s light burned on its keystone, small and steady.

“That is what I cannot forgive it,” said Kasimir. “It holds. It is better than mine, and I cannot find the line, and everything about it is the thing I came to the Fallowmarch to stop.” He looked down at the stone in its new place, and then at her, and his grey-blue eyes in the lantern light were steady and cold and, under the cold, something she had not expected, which looked a great deal like fear.

“That was not a survey,” he said. “That was a forgery that happened to be true.”
