# Chapter 21
## The Accusation

The summons came on Frost 12, at the end of Morning Survey, in Lisbet Arnaud’s hand.

Lisbet brought it to the Antechamber herself. She waited until Ilse had set her mark on the Wren Stair sheet, crooked, and then held out a folded paper sealed with the plain black wafer of the Board of Errata, and did not quite look at Ilse while she did it. Ilse had never seen the senior proctor look at anything other than straight on.

*Ilse Marrow, Member, first year. You are required to attend the Board of Errata in the Chapter Room at ten o’clock this morning, to hear a complaint laid against you. I. Calder, for the Board.*

Behind her, Teo said, “Marrow,” and nothing else.

She folded it along its creases and went to breakfast, because there was nothing in the summons that said she couldn’t. She knew everyone in the Mess was watching her eat her porridge. She ate all of it.

* * *

The Chapter Room was above the Library of Record, up a stair so narrow that two people could not pass on it. It was long and low and panelled in dark wood, and its windows looked down on the Moot-stone sitting where Ilse had put it two days ago. Behind a long table, three of five chairs were filled. Magistra Calder sat in the centre. On her right was Provost Steyne, with his fingertips together. On her left was Master Halloway, who kept touching his three watches one after another as if to check that time was still going. A clerk sat at a small desk with a ledger open.

Kasimir Reyne stood at the side of the room, by the window, with his hands behind his back.

On the table in front of Calder lay a charter.

Ilse knew it from the door. She would have known it across a field. It was her own sheet of good Lisk rag, foxed at one corner by the vestry damp, with the Tull orchard strip laid out in her own small exact hand: forty-one stations, the west boundary drawn in its new place two rods west of the old, the four apple trees marked with their little circles and their names. *Pearmain. Russet. Codlin. Old Tom.* At the foot, the wren’s foot, three lines and a spur. Beside the charter lay a stiff Court envelope with a red seal broken, and a lens-glass on a cloth, and a brass bowl of water gone cloudy with alum.

“Miss Marrow,” said Calder. “Stand there, if you please.”

She stood there.

Calder read the complaint in her Tessary courtroom voice, without any of her lecture pauses. *That the Member, before her admission, did make and attest a contrary charter of the orchard strip of Agnes Tull in the parish of Wick Fallow in the Fallowmarch, dated Reaping 14, 1203 H.; that the visible mark upon that charter is the witness-mark registered by the Member at Benchmarking in the Old Wall of this College; and that beneath it, traced in blind ink, lies the registered mark of Abelard Quince, surveyor, deceased in Seedtime 1200 H., by which the burden of the charter was transferred without consent to the mark of a dead man. Complaint laid by Kasimir Reyne, Member. The charter obtained from the parish chest of Wick Fallow by order of the Concordance Court, and examined by the Court’s assessor in Tessary, whose report is attached.*

She set the paper down.

“Mr. Reyne. Do you stand by the complaint?”

“I do, Magistra.”

“State the grounds.”

He stated them in complete sentences, without looking at her. He had examined the hedge at Wick Fallow in Reaping and found that it had moved under a recent charter. He had seen Miss Marrow cut her mark at Benchmarking and recognised it. The parish clerk at Hobbet had replied that the charter was in order, and so in Fogmonth he had written to the Court asking that the charter itself be sent for examination. It had arrived on Frost 11. He had compared the visible mark against the Old Wall that afternoon. It was the same mark, stroke for stroke.

“And the second mark?”

“Is shown in the assessor’s report, Magistra. I have also seen it myself.”

Calder nodded to the clerk. The clerk rose, dipped a corner of cloth in the brass bowl, filmed the glass with it, breathed on it twice and laid it over the foot of the charter.

Ilse didn’t need to look. She looked anyway. There, under the wren’s foot, faint and violet and perfectly clear, lay the hook through the ring. Quince’s mark, in her own blind ink, made with her own ground-down nib by shuttered lantern in Agnes Tull’s orchard. It was very good. It was in perfect registration. Even now some small cold part of her looked at it with pride.

Some older, quicker part of her was already going through its pockets for a way out. A second hand. A stolen mark. It found nothing, because there was nothing. She had made this thing so well that it would hang her.

“Miss Marrow,” said Calder. “You are not required to answer this complaint today. The Board sits quarterly. Its next sitting is in Thaw, after the equinox, and your tribunal will be heard then. You may answer now, or you may reserve your answer until the tribunal. Which do you choose?”

Ilse looked at the charter. She looked at Kasimir, who was looking at the window.

“I’ll answer at the tribunal,” she said.

“Very well. Silence is recorded too.” The clerk’s pen scratched. “Until the tribunal you remain a Member, with all a Member’s duties. You will attend your classes and your Morning Survey. You will not leave the College grounds without leave of a Master.” Calder turned a page in front of her. “Master Hallam has written to ask that your copying in the Register continue, since there is as yet no finding against you, and since, I quote, he has no other copyist who can draw a straight line. The Board sees no objection.”

“The College,” said the Provost pleasantly, “prejudges nothing. At the same time, Miss Marrow, the eyes of the Court are upon us, and we must be seen to be thorough.”

“Yes, Provost.”

“One further question,” said Calder. “It is a question the Board is obliged to ask. Mr. Reyne. In the course of your inquiry, did you have any reason to believe that any Master or officer of this College knew of the matter before today?”

Kasimir was silent a moment.

“In Fogmonth,” he said, “I asked Field-Surveyor Brask whether she had any concern about the accuracy of Miss Marrow’s surveys. Miss Marrow is her Fieldwork assistant. The Field-Surveyor declined to answer.”

Ilse went cold to the ends of her fingers.

“Then the Board will hear the Field-Surveyor,” said Calder, and closed her ledger. “Tomorrow, at ten. Miss Marrow, you will attend. That is all.”

On the narrow stair Halloway caught her up, out of breath, his watches jingling. “M-Miss Marrow. The—the error is not the sin. It’s the *unrecorded* error. I only—I wanted to say.” He went off down the stair before she could answer.

When she got back to the Wren Stair there was a folded note pushed under the door, on good cream paper, in a careful old-fashioned hand.

*Courage, my dear. The Board is fairer than it looks, and so, I think, are you.—A. H.*

She sat on the edge of Hen’s stripped bed and held it for a long time.

* * *

Brask came before the Board in her field coat, with the Long Lie in her good hand, and did not sit when Calder offered her the chair.

“Field-Surveyor,” said Calder. “You have heard the complaint against Miss Marrow.”

“I’ve read it.”

“Mr. Reyne states that in Fogmonth he asked you whether you had concerns about her surveys, and that you declined to answer him. Is that so?”

“It is.”

“Why did you decline?”

Brask looked down the table at the three Masters, and then sideways at Ilse, who stood with her hands clasped behind her back so that nobody would see them shake.

“Because the answer was yes,” said Brask, “and it was none of his business.”

The clerk’s pen stopped.

“You knew,” said Calder, “that Miss Marrow was a forger.”

“I knew it on the day of the Walk.” Brask said it flatly, the way she gave a bearing. “Her survey had a stream bank on it that closed by fourteen links where nothing on that heath closes. She’d made it close. Surveyors put down what they find. Forgers put down what ought to be there. I’ve been looking at Walkers’ sheets for nineteen years, Magistra. I know the difference with my eye shut.” She paused. “The one I’ve got left.”

“And you said nothing to the Board.”

“I said nothing to anyone. I passed her. I made her my assistant so I could watch her. In Fogmonth I told her I knew, and I made her a bargain: that she put no more lies on any College sheet, and I’d keep her secret.” Brask leaned a little on the Long Lie. “I judged she’d be more use to the College inside it than in a Court cell. I still judge so. You can write that down too.”

The clerk wrote it down.

The Provost had not moved, but something in the way he held his fingertips had changed. “Field-Surveyor,” he said, very gently, “you understand what you are telling us. That a Master of this College knowingly concealed a felony from the Board, and placed a known forger in a position of trust, in a season when the Court is watching our every step.”

“I’m not a Master,” said Brask. “I’m Field-Surveyor Emerita. This College took the Mastership off me nineteen years ago. It’ll be in your files.”

There was a silence round the table that Ilse could have cut with her knife.

They sent the two of them out into the corridor for a quarter of an hour. Brask stood at the window with her back to Ilse, and Ilse stood against the panelling and could not think of a single word that would be any use. Then they went back in, and the Provost gave the sentence himself, because a suspension was the Provost’s to give.

Odile Brask was suspended from teaching, and from all duties of the College, pending the tribunal of Miss Marrow in Thaw. She was barred from the College grounds from sundown that day. Her Fieldwork classes would be taken by a fourth-year proctor until other arrangements could be made.

Brask heard it without moving.

“I’ve been barred from better places,” she said, and turned, and went out, and the Long Lie knocked once on each step of the narrow stair all the way down.

* * *

Ilse found her in the Fieldwork shed an hour later, packing.

Brask had never kept much in the shed that was her own. Into a crate on the bench she was putting field-books in order of date: nineteen years of them, cloth-bound and swollen with damp. The chains hung from their pegs round the walls, cleaned and tallowed. The bucket in the north corner rang under its drip every eleven seconds, and Ilse thought, stupidly, that the fourth-year proctor would not know to empty it.

She didn’t say anything. She took the next field-book from the shelf, checked its date and handed it over. Brask took it without looking and put it in the crate.

They did that for a while.

“This is my fault,” Ilse said at last.

“Your fault’s your own business, Marrow. Mine’s mine.” Brask put in another book. “I chose. I knew what I was choosing on the day of the Walk, and I chose it again in Fogmonth, and I’d choose it today if they gave me the morning back. Don’t you take that off me. It’s about the only thing I’ve done in nineteen years I didn’t have to ask permission for.”

“They’ve barred you from the grounds.”

“I live in Verity Cross. It’s two miles.” She squared the books in the crate. “I’ll sit in my cottage and drink bad tea, and nobody will ask me to be grateful for broth. Somebody empty that bucket, or the proctor will drown.”

Ilse emptied the bucket out of the door into the frozen gorse. When she came back Brask was running the oldest of the chains through her good hand, link by link, its brass fifty-link tally worn smooth as a coin.

Ilse’s mouth was open. Everything was in it, crowding up behind her teeth: the violet lines, the Antechamber, Hen’s hands, the skewed marks she had been setting on every sheet since Frost 3 in breach of the one promise she had ever made this woman. She drew breath.

“Whatever it is,” said Brask, without turning round, “don’t.”

Ilse stopped.

“I’m off these grounds at sundown, and I can see on your face that you’ve something heavy to give me.” Brask hung the chain back on its peg. “I’m not carrying anything out of that gate I can’t put down again. Not today. You hold it. You’re young, and you’ve got two good hands.”

The bucket began to drip into itself again. Eleven seconds. Ilse closed her mouth.

“That stone,” said Brask, after a while. “At the Moot. You wrote the creep on the sheet.”

“Yes.”

“Rate and bearing.”

“Yes. Was that wrong?”

“Did it hold?”

“Yes.”

“Then don’t ask me stupid questions.” Brask lifted the crate off the bench and settled it on her hip, the stilled grey hand hooked under it like a bracket. At the door she stopped and looked back round the shed, at the chains on their pegs and the drip and the bench, the way a person looks round a room for a thing they have forgotten and know they will not find.

“You’ll do the Long Measure without me,” she said. “Good. You’d have leaned.”

She went out. Ilse stood among the hanging chains and listened to the Long Lie going away along the frozen path, its brass shoe ringing on the stones, until she couldn’t hear it any more.

* * *

It snowed on Frost 14.

It began in the dark before First Bell and did not stop. By the time Ilse came out of the Antechamber with the Wren Stair signed, crooked, for the day, the Old Court was white from wall to wall and the snow was still coming down, large and soft and slow, out of a sky the colour of pewter. It fell without any wind at all. It settled on the benchmark arrows cut into the corners of the buildings and filled them in. It lay along the brass studs of the meridian line, so that for a while each one showed as a small dark eye in the white, looking up, and then one by one the eyes closed. In the south-east corner the Moot-stone wore a thick white cap and sat in its bed under the snow, still where she had put it.

Kasimir was crossing the court from the Library of Record, with his collar up and a satchel under his arm, and his footprints went behind him straight as a chain-line.

She hadn’t meant to stop him. She found she had walked out into his path, and that he had seen her and had not changed his course by so much as a link.

He stopped a yard off. Snow had caught in his hair.

“Miss Marrow.”

“You wrote to your mother.”

“I wrote to the Concordance Court,” said Kasimir, “which is the proper authority for the examination of a charter held in a parish chest. My mother is its Chancellor. I did not write to her, and she did not reply.” His breath went up white. “I am aware that you will not believe that.”

“You went through the parish chest at Hobbet. You had them send my hedge to Tessary in an envelope so some Court assessor could put a lens on it.”

“Yes.”

“Do you know what you’ve done to Brask?”

“I know what the Board has done to Field-Surveyor Brask.” Something moved in his face and was put away. “I did not lie to them. I was asked a question and I answered it. If you wished me to lie for her you should have asked me before Calder did. I would have refused, but you would at least have asked.”

“That’s the thing about you.” Her voice was shaking, and she let it. “That’s the thing I can’t get past. You think the law is a—a fence round the weak. You said so, in the Library. Law protects the weak from the powerful. You believe it. You’d swear on the Atlas to it.”

“I would.”

“Do you know *Saffery v. the Parish of Wick*?”

He went very still.

“Calder taught it,” said Ilse. “At the Moot she held it up for all of us like a bloody saint’s relic. A newer survey, better made, supersedes a parish map four hundred years old. Fidelity, not age. Do you know what it was about?”

“A boundary in the Fallowmarch. Lord Saffery’s surveyor re-charted the parish of Wick Fallow in 1192. The parish appealed on grounds of precedence and lost.” He said it as he would have said it in an examination, and then she saw him hear what he had said. “Wick Fallow.”

“Corwin Fask,” said Ilse. “He came through in the autumn of 1192 with Lord Saffery’s money and a fine leather case and two chainmen, and he walked the whole parish in nine days, and he was very good. He was much better than the old parish map. That was the whole point. He found a place on the boundary of my father’s field where the latitude would bear it, and he charted the line sixty yards in. Sixty yards of frontage. The best soil we had. And the well.” She heard her own voice going on, flat and steady now, as if she were reading out a field-book. “I was twelve. It happened at night. The fence-line came out of the ground with a noise like a tree going down, and the well-head ground sideways on its stones, and my father stood at the window with a lamp in his hand saying *no* over and over, like that would hold it. In the morning our well was on Saffery land. Legally. Attested. Filed, stamped, and better made.”

Kasimir said nothing. The snow came down between them.

“He appealed. The vestry lent the appeal its name and not a penny more, so he spent every mark we had on it. Your Court heard it in Tessary and found for Saffery, and it went into the books as a model of the law working, and Calder teaches it to first-years. Then he went to work as a hired hand on Saffery’s land, which used to be his field. He drew water from his own well for another man’s cattle. He got smaller every year. He died of a fever in 1195 and there wasn’t much of him left for it to take.” Her right hand was aching from the knuckles to the wrist, the deep grinding ache she thought of as the bill. “The money I spent on the coach to come here was the money I’d saved to buy it back. Forty-four marks. Sixteen short.”

“Miss Marrow —”

“You think I don’t know what a forged boundary costs?” She was very close to him now. She didn’t remember moving. “I know exactly what it costs. I’ve watched it cost. Fask’s was *legal*.”

He had gone white again, as white as he had been at the Moot, and she saw that it had gone into him. She saw it go in and land somewhere and stay. For a moment, standing in the snow with his satchel under his arm, he looked as though someone had pulled a chain out of his hands.

And then he held. She watched him do it. She had watched him do it with the dead charter in his fingers at the Moot. Whatever had gone into him, he set his feet on it.

“Then the law was wrong,” he said. His voice was low and not quite steady. “In that case, the law was wrong, and the Court that upheld it was wrong, and I am—I am sorry for it. More than I can say standing here. But you have told me what was done to your father by a man with a better pen than his, and your answer is to be the woman with the better pen.” He took a breath. “You have moved I do not know how many boundaries in the dark, on nobody’s authority but your own. You were right about the widow’s orchard. I do not doubt it. Were you right every time? Who checked you? What happens when the next one with your gift decides that *she* knows where a lord’s hedge ought to be, and the lord pays better than the widow? You put a dead man under your mark so that you would not carry what you did. You do not get to choose who carries. Nobody does. That is what law is *for*.” His hand had come up, without his meaning it to, and he put it down again. “Then the law must be fixed. Not ignored. If everyone with a grievance and a pen —”

“Most people with a grievance,” said Ilse, “don’t have a pen, Mr. Reyne.”

It stopped him. She saw it stop him, the way a chain stops when it comes taut against a stake.

They stood a yard apart in the falling snow. The breakfast bell went, muffled, as if through cloth.

“My father had a pen,” Ilse said. “Fask had a better one. That’s all the law ever was in the Fallowmarch. Whoever could afford the better pen.”

Kasimir looked at her. Snow had settled on his shoulders and he had not brushed it off. She thought he was going to say something else. She thought, for one moment, standing there with her hand aching and her face wet with snow, that he was going to say he would withdraw it.

“I am sorry about your father,” he said at last, very quietly. “I mean that. I will remember it.” He shifted the satchel under his arm. “The complaint stands.”

He stepped round her, carefully, as if she might break or go off, and walked on toward the Ranges.

Ilse didn’t turn to watch him. She stood where she was in the middle of the Old Court and looked down at the line of his footprints coming toward her across the white, straight as a chain-line from the Library door, and stopping where she stood, and going on. The snow was already filling them. In a quarter of an hour they would be gone, and the court would be smooth again from wall to wall, as if nobody had ever crossed it, and nothing in it would be anywhere but where it had been put.
