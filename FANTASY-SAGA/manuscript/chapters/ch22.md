# Chapter 22
## Errata Night

The crow found her on the Wren Stair at four in the afternoon, when the light was already going. It came in through the casement Cass had left open, walked twice round the table as if it owned the lease, and held out its leg with the air of a creditor.

The note was a strip of brown wrapping paper rolled small. Brask’s hand was square and unforgiving, and she had not wasted a word on greeting.

*Confess something useful. Not the obvious thing. They’ve heard the obvious thing. — B.*

Ilse read it twice. The crow got half a biscuit, which it took to the windowsill and dismantled with contempt.

“Well?” said Cass, from the floor, retying the cords in her braids by feel. “What does the old wolf say?”

“To confess something useful.”

“Bold, from a woman who’s not allowed past the Cross.”

Brask had been barred from the College grounds for eight days. Every morning since, Ilse had walked past the padlocked Fieldwork shed and felt the absence like a step missing on a stair she knew in the dark. She put the note in her coat, in the inside pocket where she kept the things she did not want Sorry to eat.

Sorry was asleep on Hen’s bed, which was made up tight and square the way Hen had always left it, and which nobody had slept in since Frost 2. Nobody had had the heart to move it.

“Are you going to?” Teo was at the little mirror by the door, trying to make his choir collar sit flat. It was a band of dark felt with a brass pin, and on him it looked like a bootlace on a haystack. “Confess something. You don’t have to. Most people just drink and shout ‘Recorded’ at their friends, and Master Moy says the choir is excused because we’re singing, though I don’t think that’s actually in the statutes.”

“I don’t know,” said Ilse.

“That means yes,” said Cass.

“It means I don’t know.”

“From you,” said Cass, “that means yes.”

* * *

The Mess on Errata Night was not the Mess. Ilse had eaten in it every day for three months: the long tables scarred with four hundred years of compass points, the star-chart ceiling gone brown with smoke so that the constellations looked like stains on an old map. Tonight someone had taken all that away and left candles in its place.

They stood in their hundreds down every table, fat tallow and thin wax side by side, in saucers and inkpots and the necks of bottles. They crowded the window ledges three deep. They had been fixed with their own drippings along the beams, so that for the first time the ceiling was lit from below, and the painted stars hung there clear and gold. The flames leaned together whenever the doors opened and righted themselves when they closed, the way a field of barley does. Through the tall western windows she could see the Drift Wing going out over the dark heath, its windows lit. In the Old Court the snow fell straight down. Out there, past the Lantern Gate, it came down in slow loops and spirals, each flake on its own private road.

The air smelled of hot cider and cloves and wet wool, and under it, faintly, of Archivist Damask’s vinegar.

There was no seating plan tonight. Masters sat among the links; Ilse saw Mother Nettle wedged between two pale Tessary boys, telling them something that had made both of them go red to the hairline. Teo went to the choir benches below the dais, where the Sounders’ Choir stood in their felt collars like a row of large, solemn birds. Cass towed Ilse to the end of a table near the windows, with a jug in reach.

“Hen should be here,” said Cass, pouring. She said it to the jug.

“Yes.”

“She’d have confessed something stupid first thing, to get it going. That she counts stairs in her sleep.” Cass pushed a cup across. “She’d have *liked* this.”

Ilse wrapped her hands around the cup. The cider tasted of orange peel and pepper. Her right hand ached in the cold, the old ache of the bill, which she had once sent down a dead man’s thread and now carried, honestly, herself.

At the high table Hallam sat with his grey-gloved hands folded around a cup he wasn’t drinking from, listening to Master Halloway with his head on one side and an expression of tender patience. Provost Steyne sat at the centre in silver and black, Calder beside him, very upright. At the far end of the dais was the space where, Teo had told her, Brask had stood every Errata Night for nineteen years, refusing a chair, her unlit pipe in her teeth. Nobody had put anything in the space.

Kasimir was three tables over with his party. He had been not looking at her, with great precision, for eight days.

* * *

At the first stroke of the Bell of Hours the doors were shut, and the candles all leaned and settled, and Lisbet Arnaud climbed onto the dais carrying a slate.

It was an ordinary schoolroom slate in a frame of old black wood, with a sponge tied to its corner by a string. Lisbet propped it on an easel, chalked *Frost 22, 1203* at the top, and turned to the hall.

“The Slate is open,” she said. “Anything said tonight goes on the Slate and nowhere else. The Slate is washed at midnight. By the custom of the College, what is washed is not repeated, not entered, not minuted and not held against anyone in any court or any Board, and anyone who tries can take it up with me.” She folded her arms. “Masters included.”

There was a cheer. Ilse had read about the Slate and not believed in it; a forger learns early that no paper forgets. But Lisbet Arnaud did not look like someone who expected to be inconvenienced.

“By custom,” said Lisbet, “the Provost begins.”

Steyne rose beautifully, as if rising were a small kindness he was doing the room. “My dear colleagues. I confess—” He smiled, and let the pause do its work. “I confess that the new chairs in the Refectory were a mistake.”

The hall roared. The chairs had arrived in Gleaning from some Tessary benefactor, so slippery with varnish that you slid off them if you laughed, and everybody had been sliding off them for two months.

“Recorded!” said three hundred people, and Lisbet chalked a stroke on the Slate.

“That’s a paper-wall confession,” said Cass, under the noise. “Hold on. Here’s a real one.”

Halloway was on his feet, holding all three of his watches the way some men hold a hat, pink to the ears.

“I. Ah.” He looked at the watches, as if they might help. “I c-c-confess. In the spring of eleven eighty-nine I surveyed a sheep-fold at Callow Edge, in the Ammonite Hills, for my third-year practical. Dry-stone, irregular heptagon, perimeter four chains and sixty-one links. I closed my traverse with a misclosure of one link in two thousand and attested it, and it was passed.” His stammer had gone entirely. His voice had dropped half an octave and steadied, the way it always did in Mensuration when he reached the numbers. “On the fourth leg I carried a seven instead of a one. My closure was a coincidence. Two errors cancelling. The north wall of that fold has stood eight and a quarter inches out of true for fourteen years, and the sheep do not know, and the farmer does not know, and the Register does not know.” He swallowed. “Every summer I take the coach to Callow Edge and stand by that wall and look at it. I have never told anyone. I would like it to be recorded.”

The hall was quiet for a moment. Then Damask, from somewhere among the second-years, said in his thin delighted whisper, carrying perfectly, “Oh, *Pim*,” and the hall answered, “*Recorded*,” not loud this time but low and warm, three hundred voices at once, like a hand on a shoulder.

Halloway sat down very fast. Calder, beside him, patted his arm without looking at him.

“He takes the coach,” said Cass, wondering.

“Of course he takes the coach,” said Ilse. Her eyes were stinging, and she blamed the smoke. *Error is not sin. Unrecorded error is.* He had said it to first-years every week for fourteen years, with his sheep-fold standing out of true behind him the whole time.

* * *

After that they came quickly, the way a dam goes once the first stone shifts.

A league confessed that he had passed Concordance by learning Calder’s face. “Recorded,” said the hall, and Calder said “Also noted,” in a voice like a dropped portcullis. A pale first-year from Lisk confessed that he had been homesick every single day since the Walk and was homesick now, and half the links in the hall shouted “Recorded!” with the fervour of the guilty, and someone passed him a jug.

Orla Devane stood on her bench, which was not done, and held up one hand for quiet, which she got, because Orla could get quiet from a hen-house.

“I confess,” she said, “that I have been reading Piety Mercer’s letters.”

There was a short shriek from Kasimir’s table. Piety Mercer had come half out of her seat, scarlet.

“Since Gleaning,” said Orla. “All of them. Her mother’s, her aunt’s, and the young man from the Lisk canal board, who writes every week.” She put her free hand on her heart. “I confess it as an error. Because they are the dullest letters I have ever read, and I kept reading them, every week, in hope. That is the error. Hope. Recorded?”

“*Recorded!*” Piety said something that would not have been permitted on any other night, and Lisbet, chalking, said, “Also recorded, Miss Mercer, thank you.” Ilse saw Kasimir put a hand over his eyes. She thought his shoulders were shaking. She looked away before she could be sure.

Mother Nettle confessed, in language that made the Provost study the ceiling, that she fed his message-crow better than he fed his clerks, and would continue. Master Moy, without rising, confessed in a bass voice that came up through the benches that he had excused his choir from confession every year on a statute he had made up. “*Recorded*,” said the choir, with feeling, and Teo, in the middle of the second row, grinned at his feet.

Then the choir sang the old *Meridian Hymn*. The true sounders, out in the Drift, sang lower than hearing; Ilse had felt one through the soles of her boots on the day of the Walk. In a crowded hall on a winter night the choir did something like it: twenty men and women standing quite still, mouths barely open, and a note so low it was less a sound than a change in the room. The cider in her cup shivered into rings. The candle flames trembled in their hundreds, all at once, like a field when the wind turns. She felt it in her breastbone and behind her ears where the level lived, and for the length of the hymn the whole building, Held Side and Drift Wing both, seemed to be holding the same note. Someone had propped the far doors open, and she imagined the sound going down the corridor to the infirmary, and Hen lying there with her grey hand on the blanket. She did not know if Hen could feel it. She was gripping the cup hard enough to hurt.

When it ended, nobody said anything for a moment, which on Errata Night was a kind of applause.

* * *

Cass went next. From her face, she had not expected it either. She stood up, the cords in her braids swinging, and Ilse watched three hundred people remember all at once that there was a Tenting girl in the Big House, and arrange their faces.

“Hey-ho,” said Cass.

A few links, Walkers mostly, called it back. “Hey-ho.”

“My nan sent me here to learn what the paper-walls know and bring it home,” said Cass. “That’s what I told everyone, anyhow. Learn it, carry it back, don’t stay. Don’t sign what you can’t carry.” She stopped. Her hands had gone into fists at her sides, and she looked down at them as though they had been put there by someone else. “So I confess. I love the Big House. I love the stupid stairs and the stupid Survey and the bloody bell and you lot, most of you, and I don’t know what I’m going to tell my nan. May the Road forgive me.”

The hall came apart. They roared it, “*RECORDED*,” and stamped so that the candles jumped in their saucers. The Walkers were on their feet. Cass stood there with her chin up and her eyes too bright, then sat down hard beside Ilse and said, very fast, “Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to look at me.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You’re doing it now.”

Ilse filled Cass’s cup. Neither of them said anything else. Under the table, for a moment, Cass’s knee knocked against hers and stayed there.

Then Teo stood up from the choir benches.

He did it the way he did everything that frightened him, all at once, as if launched. In the candlelight, with his collar crooked, he looked like an apologetic oak. He cleared his throat twice.

“I confess,” said Teo, “that I’m afraid of heights.”

There was an instant of perfect silence, and then the whole hall, with one voice and enormous affection, said, “*We know.*”

Teo went crimson. “Yes. All right. That’s fair. The thing with the Observatory ladder was a reasonable response to a ladder. Thank you.” He did not sit down. Ilse saw him look, quickly, toward the open doors and the corridor to the infirmary. “And I confess,” he said, in his small brave voice, the short-sentence voice, “that there’s a girl from the Salt Steps whose laugh is the best sound in this building. Better than the choir. Sorry, Master Moy. I never told her. That was an error. I’m going to correct it when she wakes up.”

Nobody shouted that time. The hall said “Recorded” the way people in Hobbet said the Ninefold’s Grace at a graveside, whether they believed in it or not. Teo sat down. Two of the basses put their arms round his shoulders and he let them.

Cass had her face in her cup. Ilse took Brask’s note out of her pocket, unfolded it, and read it again by the light of the nearest twenty candles. *Not the obvious thing. They’ve heard the obvious thing.*

She thought of the accusation in the Board’s files, in Kasimir’s beautiful handwriting, and the Tull charter with a dead man’s ghost under her wren’s foot. Everyone in this room had heard. Piety Mercer had seen to that within a day. Whatever Ilse said tonight would change nothing. The Slate would be washed at midnight. The tribunal would sit after the equinox. The charter would still have her mark on it in the morning.

That, she thought, was the point. It was the only confession she would ever make that couldn’t buy her anything.

She stood up.

She felt it go along the tables like a crack along ice: heads turning, a cup set down too hard. Then three hundred people being quiet on purpose, which is louder than silence.

On the dais, Steyne’s face had acquired a careful nothing. Hallam had lowered his cup and was watching her over his spectacles, so gently and so worriedly that she nearly sat down again. Three tables away, Kasimir Reyne had finally looked at her. His notebook lay closed on the table, under his hand.

“I’ve lied on paper,” said Ilse. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. “For years.” She considered the hall, the candles, the painted stars. She thought of Agnes Tull’s Pearmain coming home across the dark. She thought of nine years of hedges. “Well.”

Three hundred people worked out what she had said. Someone at the back laughed, a single startled bark, and then a few more, and it went through the room not as mockery but as something rueful, the laugh you give when someone has said the thing nobody else would.

And then the College answered her.

“*Recorded.*”

It came from every table: the links and the leagues, the choir benches, Mother Nettle, Orla Devane, the homesick boy from Lisk, Halloway with his three watches. It came from the high table, from Calder crisply and from Steyne after a moment, smoothly. It came from Hallam, warmly, his gloved hand lifted a little from the cloth as if in blessing. It was not louder than it had been for the chairs or for Orla’s letters. It was exactly the same, and that was what undid her. The ritual took what she had said and put it with everything else, the sheep-fold and the chairs and the homesick boy, the way the ground takes rain. It did not make an exception of her. It did not make an example of her. It recorded her.

She sat down. Her knees were not entirely reliable. Lisbet chalked a stroke on the Slate, just one, the same size as all the others.

It was two heartbeats after the hall had finished, when the noise was turning back into talk and the jugs were moving again, that she heard it. It came from three tables over, from a young man sitting very straight with his hand flat on a closed notebook, and it was said not to the hall but, as far as she could tell, to the candle in front of him.

“Recorded,” said Kasimir Reyne.

He did not look at her. Orla, beside him, turned and studied him, then looked across at Ilse and lifted her cup very slightly before she drank.

* * *

At midnight Lisbet Arnaud untied the sponge from the corner of the Slate, dipped it in a basin of water that one of the links held up for her, and washed the Slate clean.

They all stood for it. The sponge went across in long strokes, and the chalk dissolved under it: the date, and the tally beneath, a hundred and nine strokes in rows of five. Grey water ran down the black face into the basin. Ilse watched her own stroke go with the others and could not tell which one it had been. Then the Slate was black and shining, and Lisbet held it up, and the College gave the toast that ended every Errata Night.

“To what is.”

“*And what isn’t, yet.*”

* * *

It was snowing harder when they crossed under the Lantern Gate. The lantern hissed in the flakes, and past it the snow went back to its looping fall, so that walking under the arch was like walking from one weather into another. The Second Range was full of singing, much of it bad.

Teo was quiet all the way up the Wren Stair. Cass went ahead two steps at a time, humming the *Meridian Hymn* in a register no choir would have owned. Inside, Sorry had moved from Hen’s bed to the warm hearth. It opened one amber eye at them and shut it again.

Cass lit the lamp. Teo sat on the end of his bed, took off his choir collar and folded it, and sat with it in his lap.

“Marrow,” he said.

Ilse was hanging her father’s coat on its nail. He only called her that when he was worried. She turned around.

Teo held out his right hand, palm up, into the lamplight.

For a moment she didn’t see it. His hands were what they always were, enormous and gentle and burned in three places from the stove. Then he tipped the hand toward the lamp, and she saw.

The tips of his first two fingers had gone grey. It was very faint. It started at the edge of each nail and faded to nothing before the first knuckle, like a cloud’s shadow on a hillside. She crossed the room, took his hand and pressed her thumb on the pad of his forefinger. It was cooler than the rest of him, cooler than it ought to have been after a hot hall and a long climb. Under her thumb the flesh was faintly, wrongly firm, like a wax seal that has not quite set.

“When?” she said.

“Four days ago. At Survey. I thought it was the cold.” He was looking at her face, not his hand. “It hasn’t got worse since. I’ve been signing off-register like you showed us, every single morning, I swear I have, I skew it by a hair to the left, I’ve never once—”

“I know you have.”

“Then why—”

“The old sheets.” She heard her own voice, flat and exact, the voice she used for closing a traverse. It was the only voice she could trust just then. “Everything you signed before Frost 4. Since Reaping. Every morning, every sheet. The trick stops new lines taking. It doesn’t cut the old ones. They’re still filed. They’re still pulling.” She did the sum because it was there to be done. Sixty-odd mornings, four sheets a morning on the busy days. “You’re carrying sixty days of it, and it’s not going anywhere.”

“Oh,” said Teo. He looked down at his hand in hers. “Right. That makes sense, actually. That’s quite elegant, in an awful sort of way, like a pie that’s gone off in a very regular pattern.”

Cass had come to stand behind Ilse. She didn’t say anything. She reached past Ilse’s shoulder and put two fingers on the grey, as Ilse had, and took them away again, and swore once, very quietly, by Road and rain.

“It’s slowed,” said Ilse. “You said yourself. Hen went faster. Hen signed extra, every day, for weeks.” She stopped. “You’re slower. That’s time.”

“How much time?”

She thought of Hen’s face against the white pillow, and Wil Ashdown on his bench staring at nothing, and she didn’t know, and she would not lie to him about it. Not with the Slate just washed.

“I don’t know,” she said. “So we’re going to be quick.”

Teo nodded slowly. He looked at his fingers for a while in the lamplight. Then he folded them gently into his palm, as if he were putting something away for later, and looked up at her with his round, tired, decent face.

“I meant it, you know,” he said. “About her laugh. I’d like to be there when she does it. Properly there. Not on a bench, looking at nothing.”

“You’ll be there,” said Cass.

“Properly,” said Ilse.

She was still holding his hand. She didn’t let go. Outside the window the snow came down in its slow spirals over the Drift Wing, every flake on its own road, and on the hearth Sorry sighed in its sleep and stretched one grey paw toward the warmth. Down in the Old Court, very faintly, someone was still singing.
