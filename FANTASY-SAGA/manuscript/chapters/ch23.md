# Chapter 23
## The Infirmary Ledger

Hen had been moved to the bed by the window, which Dr. Orle said was for the light and which Ilse suspected was so that the nurses could see her from the door without having to walk the length of the ward.

It was the third day of Deepwinter and the light was thin as skimmed milk. It came in across the blanket and lay on Hen’s right hand, which rested on top of the covers exactly where it had been put. The grey had reached the second knuckle of every finger now. It had a sheen, like pewter that has been handled, and the nails had gone the colour of old candle-ends.

“Teo came,” said Hen. “He brought a pie.”

“Did you eat it?”

“Yes.”

“Was it good?”

Hen considered this. Her eyes were the same blue they had always been, and they were open, and they rested on Ilse’s face with perfect attention, the way a level rests on a table. That was the worst of it. Hen was not vague. She was not asleep. She was simply there, all of her, with nothing moving.

“It was a pie,” said Hen.

Ilse had brought a chain. She had told herself it was for Hen to count, because Hen had always counted things, stairs and links and the seconds between lightning and thunder, and it had seemed like something Hen’s hands might remember even if Hen didn’t. She laid it on the blanket, twenty links of it, brass tallies and all. Hen looked at it.

“Twenty,” she said. She did not touch it.

“Yes.”

“That’s a fifth.”

“Yes.” Ilse waited. Once, Hen would have said, *A fifth of a chain, a fifth of a cake, a fifth of the way to dinner*, and laughed at herself, the big ridiculous laugh that came out of her like a gull out of a cliff. “Hen. Do you want me to read to you?”

“If you like.”

She read from a book of Salt Steps tide-tables that Cass had found in the library, on the grounds that it was the only thing Hen had ever said she missed. She read the high waters for Saltcombe and Tollhaven for the whole month of Seedtime, in a level voice, and Hen listened to every number, and nothing in her face changed at all. At the end Ilse closed the book and sat with it on her knees, and found that she had been holding her own right hand in her left, under the cover, pressing the thumb into the old chain-scar on her palm until it hurt.

“Miss Marrow.” Dr. Orle stood at the end of the bed in her shirtsleeves, drying her hands on a towel. “When you’ve a minute.”

* * *

Orle’s office was a cupboard with a window, and the window looked onto the coal-yard. There was a desk, two chairs, a skeleton hung on a stand by its skull with a scarf round its neck against the draught, and more paper than Ilse had seen outside the Register. Orle shut the door, went to the desk, unlocked the bottom drawer with a key from a string round her neck, and took out a ledger bound in green cloth.

“Sit,” she said. “Don’t touch Mr. Bones; he comes apart.”

Ilse sat.

“After your visit in Frost,” said Orle, “I went back. I’ve the infirmary day-books for forty years in the cellar, and the mice have had some, but not much. I wanted to know when this started.” She opened the ledger on the desk between them and turned it round. “Officially it started this autumn, it’s seasonal and it’s fatigue. Here’s my unofficial.”

It was a doctor’s ledger: columns ruled in red, a neat, fast, slanting hand. Name. Year. Party. Date of first presentation. Signs. Hand.

*Ashdown, W. Link (1st yr). Seedtime 9, 1201. Listless, flat affect, sleeps poorly. Grey to first joint, R. index and second.*

Ilse looked at it for a long time.

“He was a first-year,” she said.

“He was a link in his first spring. He’d passed every paper going.” Orle tapped the entry. “They’ve kept him on the rolls as a chain because nobody has had the stomach to strike him off. That’s the College for you. Kind in the cheapest possible way.”

Under Wil Ashdown there were two more for 1201, in Haysel and Frost. There were four for 1202. Then 1203 began to fill the page: Gleaning, Gleaning, Fogmonth, Fogmonth, and *Solberg, H. Link. Frost 2, 1203. Grey to second joint R. hand within the week. Flat. Cooperative. Says she cannot remember why things were funny.*

“And before 1201?” said Ilse.

“Nothing. Not one.” Orle sat back. Her chair creaked. “Oh, there’s Drift-fatigue in the old books, plenty of it. Field parties back from the deep heath, dizzy, sleeping fourteen hours, seeing their grandmothers on the stairs. That’s real. It passes in a week. It doesn’t go grey, and it certainly doesn’t go grey on the signing hand first, every single time.” She put one blunt finger on the column headed *Hand*. Every entry read R, except one, a left-handed girl from Tessary, which read L. “Fatigue doesn’t choose a hand, Miss Marrow. I said so to you in Frost and I’ll say it to anyone. Burden does.”

“Have you said so to anyone?”

Orle’s mouth went thin. She reached into the drawer again and took out a single folded sheet of heavy cream paper and slid it across.

*The Provost thanks Dr. Orle for her diligence in this distressing season, and asks that further correspondence on the matter of the seasonal fatigue be routed through his office, so that the College may speak with one voice.*

It was signed with a flourish and sealed with the Provost’s seal, a little meridian line crossed by a quill. It was the politest threat Ilse had ever read, and she had read the letters Lord Saffery’s steward sent to tenants.

“One voice,” said Orle. “His.” She took the sheet back and locked it away. “I’ve stopped writing to Tessary. I haven’t stopped writing.” She looked at Ilse for a moment with her sharp, tired, decent eyes. “You know something about this. I don’t know what and I’m not going to ask, because I’ve a feeling you’d have to tell me something you oughtn’t. But if you find anything I can put in front of a physician who isn’t afraid of the Provost, you bring it to me.”

“Seedtime 1201,” said Ilse. “You’re sure of the date?”

“I wrote it myself. I was there.”

She walked back through the ward past Hen’s bed. Hen was looking at the window. The chain lay on the blanket where Ilse had left it, twenty links, untouched, and Ilse picked it up and coiled it and put it in her pocket and did not say goodbye, because Hen would only have said *goodbye* back, perfectly, in exactly the right place.

* * *

She had seen the watermark change in Fogmonth, in the Register, at the copying desk on Level Two. It had been a small thing then, the sort of thing she noticed the way other people noticed weather. The old Morning Survey folios, the bundles from the 1190s and before, had a wheat-sheaf in the paper when you held it to the lamp: a fat little bound sheaf with a sickle across it, the mark of a mill at Ambry Head that had made College paper for a century. The folios after a certain point had a bridge of three arches instead. Coyle had said Master Hallam had changed the supplier, cheaper, from Lisk.

She had not, at the time, looked at the date the bridge began. She had not had a reason to.

The next morning, after Survey, she went down to Level One before the copying hour with her lamp, and found the Morning Survey bundles for 1201 on their shelf in the Antechamber stacks, each month tied with tape and docketed. She untied Thaw. She held the sheets to the lamp one after another.

Wheat-sheaf. Wheat-sheaf. Wheat-sheaf, all through Thaw, the last of the old Ambry Head stock. She untied Seedtime.

Seedtime 1: the bridge.

She stood in the stacks with the sheet against the lamp glass, and the three arches stood in the paper as clear as if they had been drawn there. Seedtime 1, 1201. The first morning of the new paper, two hundred students signing it before breakfast. Eight days later Wil Ashdown had walked into the infirmary with two grey fingers.

“Miss Marrow?”

Merrin Coyle stood at the end of the stack with a box of new folios in his arms and an expression of polite alarm. He was a narrow, soft-spoken man with ink-stained cuffs, and he always looked faintly as though he had been caught at something, even when he was the one catching.

“Good morning, Mr. Coyle.” She did not hurry to put the sheet down. Hurrying was the first thing that got you caught. “I was checking a docket. Master Hallam wanted the 1201 Survey dates for the copy-index.”

That was a lie, and a good one, because Hallam had asked her to index the Survey bundles back to 1195 and simply hadn’t said which year first.

“Ah. Yes. Of course.” Coyle set the box on a shelf. His eyes went to the sheet in her hand, the lamp behind it. “The new stock.”

“The bridge mark. From Lisk, you said.”

“Yes. A mill at Lisk. Pennick’s.” He wiped his hands on his coat, which made the ink on his cuffs worse. “It was the year of the Assembly’s audit. The Provost’s office cut everyone’s allowance, every department, by a fifth. Master Hallam was very put out about it. He said cheap paper was a false economy and the Provost would have his economies whether they were sensible or not.” He gave a small, unhappy laugh. “He signed for the new stock under protest. I remember. It was the only time I’ve heard him raise his voice.”

“So it was the Provost’s order.”

“Well. The Provost’s budget. Master Hallam’s signature. These things are—” He made a vague shape in the air. “Why do you ask?”

“The new paper takes ink differently,” said Ilse. “I’ve noticed it when I’m copying. I wondered why.”

“Does it?” said Coyle. Something went across his face then, too quick to read, and he picked up the box of folios again as though he needed something to hold. “I wouldn’t know. I only file them. You’d have to ask the Provost’s office, Miss Marrow, I’m sure I don’t know anything about paper.” He went off down the stack with the box. At the corner he dropped a pen, and stooped for it, and did not look back.

She retied the bundles exactly as she had found them, the tape crossing at the same angle, the knot on the same side. Then she went up to breakfast and found she couldn’t eat it.

* * *

The summons came the following afternoon. It was on a card, thick cream board edged in gilt, and the ink was a deep glossy black that smelled faintly of cloves.

*The Provost would be glad of a word with Miss Marrow at five o’clock today, in his study.*

“Glad,” said Cass, reading it over her shoulder. “I bet he’d be glad.”

“He found out in a day,” said Ilse. “I asked Coyle about the paper yesterday morning.”

“Coyle’s a mouse.”

“Mice run somewhere.”

The Provost’s study was on the first floor of the Provost’s House at the east end of the Old Court, where the Held Side was oldest and the level in Ilse’s ears was so steady it was almost a sound. A clerk showed her up a stair carpeted in red, past portraits of Provosts in oils, each one more serene than the last. The study itself was very warm. There were carpets on the carpets, Lisk work in blues and golds, and her boots, which she had cleaned, felt like an insult to them.

In the middle of the room, on a table of its own, was the Meridian.

It was a model of the whole College, perhaps nine feet from end to end, carved in pale wood and painted. The Old Court was there in honey-coloured lime, every window cut, the Bell of Hours a thimble of real brass in its tower. The Lantern Gate had a chip of amber on its keystone where the lantern should be. Beyond it the Drift Wing ran out across a painted heath along its causeway: the three Ranges, the Observatory of the Tern with a dome of blown glass no bigger than a hen’s egg, the long bent arm of the Second Range where the Wren Stair was. Every piece of the Wing was mounted on brass pins set into slots in the baseboard, and Ilse saw, bending, that the slots were long enough to let each section slide.

“A clerk adjusts it at the end of every month,” said Steyne, behind her. “From the Morning Survey filings. So that the model is always true. I find it soothing.” He had come in by another door, silent on the carpets. “Do sit down, Miss Marrow. Will you take a glass of something? No? Tea, then.”

He poured it himself, from a silver pot, into cups so thin she could see the shadow of the tea through them. He was elegant in the way a well-made instrument is elegant: nothing extra, everything polished. His hair was silver and his hands were pink and perfectly smooth. She noticed his hands because she always noticed hands now. There was not a trace of grey on them, not at the nails, not anywhere. Hands that had not signed much of anything in a long time.

“I’ll be frank with you,” he said, sitting opposite her, “because I think you would prefer it, and because I think we understand one another better than either of us might like. You are in a difficult position.”

“Yes, Provost.”

“Mr. Reyne’s accusation is a serious one, and I am told it is well founded. The Board sits after the equinox. You know what the Board can do. Expulsion, at the least. And once you are not a Member, the Tull matter goes back to the Concordance Court, where, I need hardly tell you, the Chancellor is Mr. Reyne’s mother.” He sipped his tea. “She is not a sentimental woman.”

“No, Provost.”

“And yet.” He set the cup in its saucer without a sound. “The offence predates your membership. There is a respectable argument that the Board has no standing to hear it at all, and that the Court’s own referral was irregularly made. There is a respectable argument that the matter might be deferred, and deferred again, until it is of no interest to anyone. These things,” said the Provost gently, “can be managed.”

Ilse said nothing. She held the thin cup and looked at the model of the Meridian, at the brass pins and the long slots that let the Wing slide.

“Field-Surveyor Brask’s situation,” Steyne went on, “could likewise be revisited. She has served the College for forty years. It would be a great pity for it to end over a misplaced loyalty to a student.”

That one went in under the ribs, and she knew he had meant it to. She kept her face still.

“What would the College want,” she said, “in return?”

He smiled, and it was a real smile, a little rueful, the smile of a man who disliked crudeness and had been forced into it. “So little. Dr. Orle is a fine physician and a very anxious correspondent. She has been upset this winter, and I am told you have been visiting her often. I would ask you not to upset her further. And I would ask you to keep to your copying in the Register and leave the paper supply to the bursary.” He spread his smooth pink hands. “That is all.”

“The paper supply.”

“I understand you have been asking about it. You have a good eye. It does you credit. But you are a first-year with an accusation of forgery over her head, and you are going about the Register asking questions about the College’s accounts, and people will draw conclusions, Miss Marrow, and none of them will be kind to you.” He leaned forward a little. “Do you know what the Court would give for a scandal in the Register? Lady Reyne has wanted the Register under Court authority for fifteen years. Every rumour, every complaint, every anxious letter from an infirmary is a stone in her hand. This College has stood for twelve hundred years because it has kept its own house in order and kept its own counsel. Its survival depends on calm.” He sat back. “The College’s survival depends on calm, Miss Marrow. I am asking you to help me keep it.”

There were students in his infirmary with grey hands. There was a girl from the Salt Steps who could not remember why things were funny. Ilse looked at the Provost and thought, quite coldly, that he knew about them, every one, and had written a letter to Orle about speaking with one voice.

She had lied to magistrates and parish clerks and a Concordance assessor. She knew how to do this.

“I’ll think about it, Provost,” she said, and let her voice catch a little on the last word, the way a frightened first-year’s would. “Thank you. For Field-Surveyor Brask. I hadn’t thought there was anything anyone could do.”

“Of course you hadn’t.” He rose, and so did she. He walked her to the door himself, his hand not quite on her shoulder. “Think about it. Don’t take too long.” At the door he paused. “And Miss Marrow? Your Errata Night confession was very affecting. I hope you will not feel obliged to make it again anywhere less forgiving.”

What was washed was not repeated. He had repeated it, pleasantly, on his own doorstep, so that she would know he could.

* * *

“The Provost,” said Cass. “Road and rain.”

She was sitting cross-legged on the table in the Wren Stair with her skinning knife in one hand and a whetstone in the other, and she had been sharpening the same edge for some minutes, in long, unhurried strokes. Sorry sat by her knee and watched the blade go back and forth as though it were a mouse.

“He knew I’d asked about the paper within a day,” said Ilse. “He told Orle to stop writing. He offered to make the accusation go away, and Brask’s suspension, if I stopped. He knows exactly what’s in that infirmary, and he’s keeping it quiet.”

“And the paper changed the year his office cut the money,” said Cass. “Seedtime first. And the first one went grey on the ninth.” She tested the edge on her thumbnail. “Seems tidy.”

“It’s tidy.”

Teo was on his bed with his back against the wall, his knees up, his big hands loose between them. He had pulled the cuff of his right sleeve down over his fingers, which he had taken to doing in company, and which Ilse had pretended not to see. Now he pushed it back and looked at his fingertips by the lamp. The grey was no worse than it had been on Errata Night. Ilse had checked it every morning, and so, she knew, had he.

“I don’t know,” said Teo.

“What don’t you know?” said Cass. “He threatened her. In his nice warm room with his nice silver teapot.”

“Yes. He did. He’s horrible.” Teo frowned at his hand. “It’s only — I’m sorry. It’s that I’ve met men like him. In Lisk. The canal board’s full of them. They’re all ‘we’ and ‘calm’ and ‘the good of the city,’ and if a lock-gate cracks they’ll paint it before they’ll mend it, because mending it means admitting it cracked. They’ll hide anything.” He looked up. “But they don’t *build* things. They haven’t got the patience. Whoever did this sat down and drew a ghost-line under every mark on every sheet, every morning, for three years. Thousands of them. In perfect registration. Ilse said so herself — perfect. That’s someone who’s very patient and very good with a pen.” He shook his head slowly. “The Provost’s a coward, not a — whatever this is.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. The whetstone had stopped.

“Cowards hire people,” said Cass at last. “Paper-walls do. Pay someone to carry it. Bearing for hire, isn’t it?”

“Maybe,” said Teo. “Maybe that’s it.” He didn’t sound sure.

Ilse turned it over in her mind like a sheet held up to the lamp, looking for the second line under the first. Teo was right that the work was patient. It was also right that Steyne had threatened her the day after she asked about the paper, and that he had written to Orle, and that he had offered to buy her silence with the only two things she wanted. A man who had nothing to hide didn’t offer to buy anything. It was the first rule of the trade. Nobody bribes the honest witness.

“It doesn’t have to be his hand,” she said. “It only has to be his order. He wants it quiet. Somebody’s doing it and he wants it quiet. That’s enough to start.”

“Start how?” said Cass.

“Damask’s Palimpsest class comes to raking light this month. If there’s more on those sheets than I can see with a damp lens, he’s the one who can show me how to find it.” She looked at Teo’s hand. “And we keep signing skew. All of us. Every morning.”

“Every morning,” said Teo, and pulled his cuff back down.

Sorry had lost interest in the knife. It had found the Provost’s summons card on the table, where Ilse had dropped it, and was investigating the edge of it with its nose. Before anyone could stop it, it took the corner in its mouth, sat up on its haunches like a squirrel, and began to eat the card from the gilt edge inward, with small decided bites, the glossy black ink first.

“Look at that,” said Cass, with deep satisfaction. “Even the cat’s against him.”

Ilse let it eat. It got as far as *glad of a word* before it stopped, sneezed at the cloves, and went to sleep on the remainder with its ears folded flat, and she sat for a long time watching it and thinking about patience, and about pens.
