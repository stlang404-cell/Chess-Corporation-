# Chapter 24
## Raking Light

Archivist Damask began the lesson by putting out every light in the room.

The Palimpsest workroom was under the Library of Record, down a stair so worn in the middle that each step was a shallow bowl. It smelled of vinegar and old glue, and its shutters were lined with felt. When the last lamp was out the dark was total, and twelve first-years sat in it breathing, and somebody’s stomach made a long thoughtful noise, and Orla Devane said “Absalom,” and Absalom said “It wasn’t,” and then a match scraped and there was Damask.

He was very small in the light of one candle, with his brass-armed lenses pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyebrows. He laid a single sheet of yellowed paper on the bare oak table.

“What do you see?” he whispered.

“A deed,” said Piety Mercer, who sat nearest. “A conveyance of a mill-leat in the parish of Thorne, dated 841.”

“Yes! Yes. Very good. That is what it *says*.” Damask beamed at her. Then he bent, slowly, the candle in his fist, and brought the flame down until it was nearly touching the table, at the far edge of the sheet, so that its light did not fall on the paper but skimmed across it, flat and low, like the last of the sun across a ploughed field in winter.

The sheet changed.

Ilse had cut paper and soaked it and scraped it all her life. She had not known it had a landscape. Under the raking light every fibre stood up and threw a shadow. The surface of the deed was not flat at all. It was a country of tiny ridges and furrows, and the words of the conveyance lay on it like hedgerows on a hillside. Under those words, and across them, and between them, lay others: shallow trenches where an older pen had pressed, and broad smooth hollows where someone, long ago, had scraped the first writing away with a knife and pumice and written the mill-leat over the top. A whole second document lay there under the first, the way an old field-system lies under a new one, visible only at dusk, when the low sun shows every bank.

“Oh,” said Damask, with enormous tenderness. “Oh! Look. *Look.*”

They crowded round. Even Piety leaned in.

“It was a boundary,” said Damask. “Before it was a mill. A field boundary, between two brothers. One of them died, and the other one scraped him off the deed and sold the water.” He moved the candle a hair, and a name came up out of the shadows, faint as breath: *Edwy*. “There you are, Edwy. There you are, my dear. Nine hundred years under a mill-leat, and here you are.” He looked round at them all, his little lenses catching the flame. “This is what I want you to understand. Nothing written is ever wholly gone. A pen is a plough. It bruises the tooth of the paper, and the bruise remains when the ink has faded or been scraped or never shown at all. Light from above shows you what a sheet wants you to see. Light from the side shows you what it did.”

He gave them each a candle and a reused sheet and set them to it. Kasimir Reyne found three layers on his in ten minutes and began, inevitably, to list them in his notebook. Ilse found a scraped-out sum on hers, a tithe-collector who had got his total wrong and tried again twice. She looked at it for a long time and did not see the sum at all.

At the end, while the others were filing out, she lingered by the teaching stack, putting her sheet back.

“Archivist. If a line had been drawn in an ink that doesn’t show — not scraped off, but never visible in the first place — would raking light find it?”

Damask turned to her with an expression of pure delight. “A dry stylus, you mean? Or one of the vanishing inks? Oh, yes, yes. If anything, better. Scraping smooths the tooth. A pen that has never been seen has never been scraped.” He tipped his head like a bird. “Have you something you would like me to look at, Miss Marrow?”

“No,” said Ilse. “I was only wondering.”

“Wonder,” said Damask, “is the whole of my discipline,” and patted her arm, and shuffled off to unshutter the windows.

* * *

She took the sheet that evening from the Frost bundle in the Antechamber stacks, while Coyle was at supper.

It was the Wren Stair and Long Gallery folio for the first day of Frost. She chose it with care. It came before the off-register signing began, it had been superseded by forty-odd mornings since, and it carried four marks at the foot of the drawing: Teo’s careful round anchor, Cass’s knot, her own wren’s foot, and Hen’s. Hen’s mark was a little salt-pan rake with five teeth. It was one of the last sheets Hen had ever signed.

She waited until Teo was asleep and Cass was snoring in the way she always denied, and Sorry had settled in the crook of her knees. Then she slid out of bed, laid the folio flat on the floorboards in the middle of the room, and set a candle on the boards beside it, as low as it would go, with the flame at the paper’s edge.

At first there was only the drawing: the Wren Stair, the turn at the landing, the Long Gallery running away northward, in their party’s four hands, Hen’s neatest. She moved the candle an inch, and the paper turned into country.

There were the marks, standing up in the light like stones in a field. Under each of them, as she had known there would be, lay the ghost: a second line exactly under the first, in perfect registration, a bruise in the paper where a fine pen had laid down blind ink before any of them had ever touched it. She had seen these through her damp-lens. What the lens had been too blunt to show her was where they went.

They did not stop at the marks.

From beneath each signature a single hair-fine line ran out across the foot of the sheet, so faint that under ordinary light there was nothing there at all. Hen’s ran from the rake. Teo’s ran from the anchor, and Cass’s from the knot, and hers, she saw with a lurch, from the spur of the wren’s foot. The four lines did not run straight. They curved toward one another across the margin, the way streams curve on a map, and a little below the scale-bar Teo’s joined Hen’s, and a little after that Cass’s joined them, and then hers, so that what had been four lines became, by the time it reached the bottom corner of the sheet, one. It was a little deeper there, a little more bruised, as if more weight had run down it. At the very corner, at the edge of the paper, it ended in a tiny tick: a registration mark, the kind a printer uses to lay one sheet exactly over another.

Ilse sat back on her heels on the cold floor.

She had seen the shape before, on every map of every coast in the Reach. A river comes down to the sea and splits, and splits again, and spreads into a fan of channels, a delta. This was a delta running the wrong way. It did not spread. It gathered. It took many mouths and drew them inland into a single stream, and the stream ran off the edge of the paper toward somewhere else.

A ghost-line was a thread. She had always thought of it that way: a thread from your hand to the land, and, if you were clever, a second thread to a dead man’s hand, so that the weight went to him instead of you. One mark, one thread, one place for the burden to go. She had never in her life drawn a ghost that went anywhere but to a mark.

These did not go to a mark. They went to an edge, and to a tick that meant *lay the next sheet here*.

“Somewhere,” she said aloud, very quietly, “there’s a sheet this sits on.”

On the bed, Sorry lifted its head.

She thought of two hundred students signing every morning: two hundred threads on fifty sheets, every one gathering in its corner into one channel, every channel running off its edge toward the same place. A root. She thought of a word Quince had used once, drunk and boasting of a chapter-house he had ghosted in his youth so that the dean’s burden ran into a dozen dead canons at once. He had drawn it for her in spilled ale on the tabletop, all the little lines running into one. *You want a conduit, girl, for a big job. Run it all through the one pipe.*

A conduit.

She looked at the thickest of the four lines, the one that ran from Hen’s little rake. It was deeper than the others. Of course it was. Hen had signed extra.

She did not sleep much. In the morning she put the folio between the pages of Halloway’s *Elements of Error*, which was the dullest and heaviest book she owned and which nobody, including Halloway, had ever opened for pleasure, and she put the book on the shelf above her bed with the others.

* * *

The note came at noon, by a link who handed it over as if it might go off.

*My dear Miss Marrow — I have been sent a seed-cake from Lisk of quite unreasonable size, and I cannot possibly face it alone. Would you rescue me? My study, four o’clock. Do say yes. — A. H.*

She read it on the Wren Stair and stood holding it for some time.

She could not go to the Provost. She could not go to Brask, barred in her cottage at Verity Cross; and a crow could not carry what she had found. Orle had no power. Damask would be delighted and then tell everyone. She had Teo and Cass and Sorry and a sheet of paper in a book, and Hen had been lying by the window for six weeks being perfectly polite.

And there was Hallam. Master of the Register, which was where the sheets were kept. He had signed for the new paper under protest. He had put her in the Register as a copyist when nobody else in the College would have trusted a Walker with a dust-cloth. He had looked at her across the Mess on Errata Night as if he were afraid for her. He was the one person in this place with authority who had been kind to her before he had any reason to be.

She went.

* * *

Hallam’s study lay behind the Register Antechamber, through a door at the back of the stacks that Ilse had walked past every morning for three months without seeing open. It was oak, old and dark, with an iron latch, and when Hallam opened it to her knock the warmth came out to meet her like a dog.

“There you are,” he said. “Come in, come in. Mind the step, it’s been there since the Ninefold and it has never once moved, and still everyone trips on it.”

It was a small room and it was full: a sea-coal fire in a deep grate, two shabby chairs, books from floor to ceiling and in leaning stacks on the floor with cups on top of them. Maps were pinned over the books: the Salt Steps, the Ammonite Hills, a hand-drawn sheet of the Sallow coast, faded nearly white, with its edges soft as cloth. On the desk, among the papers, sat a paperweight carved from sounder-ivory, a smooth pale oval the size of a hen’s egg, with growth-rings in it like the grain in a shell. There was a smell of woodsmoke and paper and, faintly, of caraway.

The seed-cake was on a plate by the fire. It was, as promised, unreasonable.

“My sister’s girl,” said Hallam, cutting it. “She lives in Lisk and is convinced I don’t eat. She’s quite right. Sit, my dear.”

He poured tea for her, and then took his own cup in both hands and sat back in the other chair with it held against his chest. His grey kid gloves were soft with age at the fingertips. He held the cup the way a man holds a bird he has found in the road.

“These old hands don’t like the winter,” he said, seeing her look. “The Sallow left me a souvenir. Never mind them.” He smiled at her over his spectacles. “How is Miss Solberg?”

“The same.”

“I sat with her last week, for an hour. I don’t know whether she minded. She was very polite to me.” He looked into his tea. “That is the cruelty of it. The politeness. One would almost rather they wept.” He was quiet for a moment. “And Mr. Anselm? I heard him on Errata Night. A brave young man. I hope he tells her.”

“So do I.”

“And you,” said Hallam gently. “Are you sleeping?”

“Not much.”

“No. I shouldn’t think so.” He set his cup down on a stack of books. “I won’t speak of the Board unless you want me to. I’ll only say that when the time comes, I shall tell them what I know of your hand: that it is the best I have seen in a first-year in twenty years, and has never once given me a false line.”

She had not expected that. She drank her tea to give her face something to do.

They talked. It was easy to talk to him. He knew the Fallowmarch; he had walked it as a young man re-signing the old parish benchmarks, and he remembered the church at Hobbet with its leaning spire, and laughed when she told him it leaned more now. He told her about the night on the Sallow when Odile Brask had carried a lamp across a standing-rain bog for two hours to find a lost chainman, singing to keep the hollowings off. “The bravest woman I ever knew,” he said. “Still is. Broken, a little, but the break shows you the grain.” He talked of the Reach the way old sailors talked of ships, as *she*.

“People forget she has to be held,” he said, looking at the fire. “They think the ground under Tessary is just there, the way the sky is there. It isn’t. Someone holds her. Every morning, two hundred children walk out onto the Drift Wing with chains in their hands and hold her a little, and they think it’s a chore.” He smiled. “Every map is an act of love, my dear. We hold her still so that the children of Hobbet can find their way home.”

Ilse put her cup down.

“Magister,” she said. “I need to tell you something about the Morning Survey sheets.”

She had planned it on the walk down, the way she planned a traverse: which stations to take, which to leave out. She told him there were lines under the marks. She told him she had seen them under raking light, after Archivist Damask’s class, on an old sheet from the stacks. She told him they lay in perfect registration under each signature and ran off to the corner of the sheet and joined there. She did not say *damp-lens* or *blind ink* or *Quince*. She did not say *I know what a ghost-line is because I have drawn a hundred of them*. She said, “I think someone is tracing under our marks. I think that’s why the students are going grey. I’ve heard that’s how — forgers do it. To move the weight.”

Hallam did not interrupt her once. He listened with his gloved hands folded in his lap and his head bent, and as she went on his face grew very still and very grave, and when she had finished he took his spectacles off and rubbed his eyes with the back of his glove.

“There is a seventh Law,” he said at last, quietly. “You will have noticed I don’t teach it. We don’t, at the College. For the same reason one doesn’t show children where the knives are kept.” He put the spectacles back on. “Transfer. Yes. It can be done. It is the vilest thing that can be done with a pen, and you are telling me it has been done here, in my Register, under my nose, to my students.” His voice did not rise. It went lower. “Thank you. Thank you for bringing it to me and not to the Mess.”

“The Provost—”

“Aurelian is frightened,” said Hallam. “He has been frightened for three years. Frightened men want things to be calm. Leave Aurelian to me.” He leaned toward her. “Have you shown anyone else? Told anyone?”

She thought of Teo asleep on the Wren Stair with his grey fingertips, and Cass snoring, and Orla Devane lifting her cup across the Mess. They were hers to protect. The habit of nine years closed over them like water.

“No,” she said.

“Not Dr. Orle? Not young Mr. Reyne?”

“No one.”

“Good,” said Hallam. “That was wise. And the sheet itself?”

“I put it back,” said Ilse. “In its bundle.”

“Then that’s where I shall find it.” He sat back, suddenly very old and very tired. “I’m going to look into this myself, my dear, and at once. It’s my Register. Whatever has been done in it is my failure before it is anyone’s crime, and I shall mend it.” He held her eyes. “But I must ask you something hard. Tell no one. Not your friends. Not the infirmary. Not for my sake, for the students’. If two hundred young people refuse to sign tomorrow morning, the Drift Wing will be out on the heath by noon, and some of them will be in it. You know the story of the Lost Wing. Panic,” said Aurek Hallam gently, “kills more than any illness. Let me find who has done this. Quietly. And then we will make it right.”

“Yes,” said Ilse. “All right.”

“Thank you.” He breathed out slowly and smiled at her, and it was the smile of a man who has been handed something heavy and is glad, all the same, to be the one holding it. “Now. You’ve hardly touched that cake, and I shall be eating it until Thaw if you don’t help. Have the corner piece. It has the most caraway.”

She had the corner piece. He wrapped two more slices for her friends, “whoever they are,” and at the step that had never moved since the Ninefold he put out his gloved hand to steady her elbow, and she did not trip.

* * *

She walked back across the Old Court feeling lighter than she had in six weeks, and noticed it the way you notice a tooth stop aching: carefully, not trusting it. She had put some of the weight down into hands able to carry it. That was what the College was supposed to be for.

“You told a Master,” said Cass, when she got in. She was sitting on the windowsill eating one of the slices of cake. “About the lines. You just told him.”

“Not how I know. Not about you. Just that they’re there.”

“Big House looks after Big House, Ilse. You know that. That’s all it does.”

“Not him,” said Ilse.

Teo, who had eaten his slice in two bites, said, “I think it’s good someone grown-up knows. I’m sorry. I just do.” He hesitated. “It’s very good cake.”

Cass looked at the cake in her hand, and at Ilse, and shrugged, and finished it.

Before she went to bed, Ilse took Halloway’s *Elements of Error* down from the shelf, opened it at page two hundred and nine, and looked at the folio lying flat between the pages. Then she closed the book, put it back exactly in its place, square to the shelf-edge, and blew out the candle.

* * *

She woke in the dark before First Bell because something was walking on her face.

It was Sorry, treading round and round on her pillow, trembling under her palm, its fur standing out in a bristling halo. It made a small noise she had never heard it make, a thin creak like a door. She lit the candle.

Sorry leapt from the pillow to the top of the wardrobe in one bound and crouched there, staring down at the room, its outline not quite steady, as if the candlelight could not decide where it ended. Its amber eyes were enormous.

The wardrobe had moved.

That was not new. Every morning it stood a foot or so out from the wall, and one of them pushed it back. But this morning it stood well into the room, turned at an angle, its doors facing the window, and the floorboards showed four long pale scrapes in the dust.

Ilse got out of bed. She went to the shelf above her bed and took down Halloway’s *Elements of Error*. It was where she had put it, square to the shelf-edge. She opened it at page two hundred and nine.

There was nothing between the pages.

She went through the book page by page, then shook it over the bed, then did the same with every other book on the shelf, quietly and very thoroughly, because the first rule of losing something is to be certain it is lost. The door was shut. The latch was down. Teo was asleep on his back with one arm over his face, and Cass was curled toward the wall, and Hen’s bed was square and empty, and nothing else was out of place at all.

Coyle counted his bundles. She had seen him do it, every week, with his lips moving. Coyle had seen her in the Seedtime stacks with a sheet against the lamp, and the Provost had known about it within a day.

She took her folding rule from her coat on the nail and measured from the wall to the back corner of the wardrobe. Two feet, eleven inches and a half: four links and a little more, nearly three times its usual drift. She measured the angle against the line of the floorboards with her thumb and a scrap of paper. Then she took her field-book out of the coat and wrote it down, the date, the distance, the angle, *sheet gone from book, door latched, no other disturbance*, in her smallest, most careful hand, because an error unrecorded was a sin and this, whatever else it was, was an error in her measurements of the world. When she had finished she put the book away, and lifted Sorry down from the wardrobe and held it against her chest until it stopped shaking, and then she went and woke the others.
