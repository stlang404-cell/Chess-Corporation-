# Chapter 25
## Kasimir’s Ledger

A week after the sheet went missing, Hallam had sent her a note.

*My dear — Inquiries proceed. They are delicate and they are slow, and I would ask you for patience, which I know is a great deal to ask of anyone your age. Rest assured that you are not forgotten. Burn this. — A. H.*

She had burned it, and thought it a strange thing to be grateful for being told to wait. She had not told him the sheet was gone. She had told him it was in its bundle, and a lie, once laid down, is a station you have to keep measuring from.

It was Candlemonth now, the twentieth day, the snow gone to grey slush in the Old Court, and nothing had changed. Hen lay in her bed by the window. Teo’s fingertips were no worse and no better. Every morning the three of them signed the Survey skew, a sixteenth high of the rule and a little sunwise, and every morning the Wren Stair held, a little wobbly, like a table with a folded letter under one leg. Coyle would not meet her eye in the stacks. The Provost had not summoned her again. He did not need to.

She climbed to the crow-loft at sunset with a letter for Brask in her pocket, as she did every week.

The loft was at the top of the old tower above the Library of Record, up a stair and two ladders, a long low room under the roof slates. It was open to the west through a row of arches, and the whole of the Drift was framed in them like a painting. Tonight the sun was going down into the sea behind the Tern Stack, and its light came in flat and red along the floor. Out over the heath, three miles off, a single hill stood under its own private rain, a grey column of it falling and falling out of a clear sky, lit pink from the side, the way it had stood since Fogmonth.

The crows were on their perches in their dozens, grey-necked and black-backed, muttering. Along the sill of every arch they had built their little cairns: stacks of pebbles three and four high, no bigger than a fist, each one set exactly on a point where the hold was strongest. Mother Nettle never let anyone move them. *They know where the world is*, she said, *which is more than you lot do.*

“Brask again?” said Mother Nettle, from her stool. She was a heap of shawls with a face in the middle of them like a walnut, and she was mending a jess with her teeth.

“Brask again.”

She took the letter, rolled it with two twists of her crooked fingers, and whistled a crow down onto her wrist. “Go on, Tansy. Down to the Cross, to the cross old woman.” The crow dropped away into the red light. Mother Nettle wiped her fingers on her shawls. “And here’s the other one.”

Ilse turned. The trapdoor had lifted, and a fair head was coming up through it, and then a grey-blue coat with the snow still wet on its shoulders.

Kasimir Reyne climbed into the loft, closed the trap behind him, stood up, and hit his head on a beam.

Mother Nettle cackled, a long rattling noise like a stick dragged along railings, and it set the crows off, so that the whole loft was full of wings, and Kasimir stood with his hand pressed to his skull and his eyes shut.

“Bugger the crows,” said Mother Nettle happily. “They’re smarter than you lot. *They* duck.”

“Good evening, Mother Nettle,” said Kasimir, with dignity. He opened his eyes. “Good evening, Miss Marrow.”

“Mr. Reyne.”

“I would like ten minutes of your time.” He lowered his hand and examined it for blood, and found none, and seemed faintly disappointed. “I would like them somewhere that neither my mother’s Court nor your Provost’s office can overhear. I followed you up here because it is the only place in the College that I am reasonably confident has neither.”

“Crows don’t tell,” said Mother Nettle. “And I’m deaf when I want.” She went back to her jess.

Ilse looked at him. They had not spoken since the Old Court in the snow, when she had told him about her father and the Long Field and he had said *then the law must be fixed* and walked away, and left his accusation in the Board’s files where it still lay.

“You filed an accusation of forgery against me,” she said. “The Board hears it in five weeks.”

“Yes.”

“And now you want ten minutes.”

“Yes. The accusation is true. This is also true.” He took out the small black notebook with its corners worn round that she had seen in his hand since the night at Widow Tull’s hedge. “I am capable of holding two true things at once, Miss Marrow. I had hoped you might be.”

* * *

He sat on an upturned crate under the arches with the notebook open on his knee, and she sat on another, and the red light lay across the pages between them.

“I was in the Fallowmarch last summer on a Concordance inspection of the voids,” he said. “What you don’t know is that I was there at my mother’s request, and her interest is not in hedges. Since the spring of last year there have been unravellings in the Held Reach. The College calls them Drift incursions, or Margin mischief, and says they are nothing to concern the Court. My mother believes the College is losing its grip on the Reach and is concealing it. She asked me to find out whether she was right.” His mouth tightened. “I would like to be clear that I know what she will do with the answer.”

“Take the Register.”

“If she can.” He looked down at the page. “I have been keeping a list.”

The front of the notebook was errors observed, as she had always known it was: small entries in his perfect hand, dated, each with its correction. *Fogmonth 4: Grice’s chain read 99 links at a tally; recalibrated. Fogmonth 11: my own levelglass reading transposed (4.2 for 2.4); corrected.* Then, toward the back, the entries stopped and a table began. It was ruled in fine ink across two pages, and the columns were headed *Place*, *Date of void*, *Nature*, and then, in a separate block on the facing page, *Register warrant*, *Signed*, *Dates of travel*, *Route*.

He had seven places.

*Hythe Corner, Salt Steps. Blossom 1202. Harbour wall unknit, 9 rods.*
*Little Sallow. Fogmonth 1202. Well displaced 22 yds.*
*Marlow, Fallowmarch. Seedtime 1203. Drainage ditch wandering; about 3 chains.*
*Fennock, Fallowmarch. Greening 1203. Gatepost and 2 rods of fence migrating.*
*Little Wick lane, Wick Fallow. Reaping 15, 1203. Lane unravelled, ford dispersed, milestone sunk.*
*Brine End. Fogmonth 9, 1203. Well displaced 40 yds; two cottages slid.*
*Corrow. Frost 7, 1203. Camp ground let go entire.*

“Where do warrants come from?” said Ilse. Her voice sounded quite ordinary.

“When a Master travels on College business, the Register issues a warrant. It pays for post-horses and coach seats on the Great Meridian Road and the coast roads, and the posting houses enter it in their ledgers so the College can be billed at the quarter.” He touched the facing page. “Assessors inspect posting-house ledgers for road tax, and I hold an assessor’s commission. It took me since Reaping, nine posting houses, and one very long evening in which Orla Devane charmed the bursar’s clerk while I read his daybook upside down.” He did not smile, but something in his face nearly did. “She would like it known that she does not regret it.”

Ilse read down the right-hand page.

Every void had a warrant beside it. Every warrant was a Register tour of inspection, and every one was signed in the same hand, which Kasimir had copied so exactly that she could see the slope of the capitals and the little hook on the final letter: *A. Hallam, Mag. Reg.* And every tour had brought him within a morning’s ride of the place, a day or three before the ground let go.

*Reaping 8–21, 1203. The Cape to the Fallowmarch and return. Stages: Verity Cross, Tessary, Hobbet (two nights).*

“Brine End,” said Ilse. “He was in the Salt Steps that week.”

“His warrant says the Salt Steps, which are north. The posting house at Verity Cross says that on the first morning he hired a gig, drove it south, and brought it back at dusk muddy to the hubs.”

“I was in Hobbet that week,” said Kasimir. “I was there for the voids. I did not see him. I saw you.”

Out over the heath the hill stood under its rain. The light on it was going from pink to grey.

“No,” said Ilse.

“Miss Marrow—”

“No. Listen.” She put her hand flat on the page. “Dates are the easiest thing in the world to make agree. I’ve done it. You take a hundred tours and a hundred voids and pick the seven that line up. Hallam travels all the time. It’s his office. Of course he’s near when something happens.” She heard herself talking faster and could not stop. “And it’s the Provost who’s been covering this. The Provost wrote to Dr. Orle to stop her writing to Tessary. The Provost had me in his study six weeks ago and offered to make your accusation disappear if I stopped asking about the paper supply. The paper changed in 1201 when the Provost’s office cut the money. The Provost—”

“The Provost,” said Kasimir, “has left Cape Verity three times in two years, all three to sit in the Assembly at Tessary, on dates that are printed in the Assembly calendar and that match none of these. And the Provost does not sign travel warrants. These are in Hallam’s hand.” His voice was very level. “There were eleven Register tours in that period. Seven of them were followed by voids. I did not choose seven out of a hundred. I chose seven out of eleven. The other four were in the Ammonite Hills and Lisk, where the ground is oldest and most deeply held, and where, I would suggest, a void is very much harder to make.”

She said nothing.

“I may be wrong,” said Kasimir. “I have written down every way I can think of in which I might be wrong. It is on the next page. It is a long page.” He closed the notebook on his finger. “But I came to you because you are the only person in this College who would understand the evidence that would prove me right, if it exists. In Fogmonth I found you in the Antechamber at midnight, with something in your hand you did not want me to see, and you were more frightened than I have ever seen anyone. You are not a person who frightens easily. I have spent three months trying to work out what a forger would be afraid of in a room full of honest paper.” He looked at her. “What did you find?”

Mother Nettle had stopped mending. Ilse looked at the columns in his beautiful hand and thought of the old milestone, HOBBET II, standing up to its shoulders in the lane.

“Not here,” she said. “It needs the dark. Can you get yesterday’s Survey sheet for your party out of the Antechamber without anyone seeing?”

“I file my party’s sheets myself,” said Kasimir. “I always have. Nobody else does it properly.”

“Of course you do,” said Ilse.

* * *

The Fieldwork shed had been padlocked by order of the Board since Frost, but the order said nothing about the back door, which had never had a lock, only a wedge, and Ilse had been Brask’s assistant long enough to know where the wedge was kept.

Inside it was cold and smelled of damp canvas and linseed oil and, very faintly, of Brask’s pipe. The chains hung on their pegs where Ilse had coiled them before the shed was shut. The Long Lie’s peg by the door stood empty, and Ilse found she could not look at it.

She shuttered the one window and lit a single candle and set it on the bench, and Kasimir laid the sheet down beside it: the Survey folio for the Observatory stair and the Tern Passage, dated Candlemonth 19, signed in four places at the foot. Absalom’s laborious cross. Piety’s spiky little steeple. Orla’s flourish like a cat’s tail. And his own, a beautifully drawn R with a level line through it, the most exact mark on the sheet.

“Watch the foot of the sheet,” said Ilse, and brought the candle down to the bench until the flame was level with the paper, and let the light rake across it.

She heard him stop breathing.

It was all there, as it had been on the Wren Stair folio five weeks before: the paper standing up into country, the knot bruised under each mark, and from each knot the hair-fine line running beneath the rule toward the binding. Then, in the blank margin, four streams bending toward each other, Absalom’s into Orla’s, Piety’s into them, and then his, until one channel ran out to the edge and its little registration tick.

“Each line is in blind ink,” said Ilse quietly. “Gall and alum and a little quicksilver. It dries to nothing. It’s laid on the sheet before anyone signs it, a knot shaped to sit under your mark, because you’re all taught to sign in the same box, and every one of you does it the same way every morning. When you set your mark on it with intent, you take the hold, and you take the burden that goes with it. And then the ghost takes the burden from somewhere else and runs it down that line into you.” She moved the candle a little, and the channel in the margin deepened. “On an ordinary forgery the ghost runs to one mark. A dead man’s, usually. This doesn’t run to a mark. It runs off the sheet. There’s another sheet somewhere that all these lines sit on. A root. A conduit. Every sheet in the Antechamber since Seedtime 1201 is feeding into it, and it’s feeding back.”

Kasimir did not say anything for a long time.

Then he bent until his face was a hand’s breadth from the paper and looked at his own mark with the ghost under it, running away to the margin. He reached out one finger toward the knot under his R and stopped short. His face had gone the colour of the paper.

“Every morning,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Since Reaping. I have signed every morning since Reaping. I have never missed one. I have signed extra when Absalom was ill.” He straightened and put both hands flat on the bench and looked at them, then turned them over, the long clean fingers, the nails. There was no grey on them. She watched him look for it anyway, at every fingertip in turn, the way a man pats his pockets for a key he knows he has dropped.

“There’s nothing,” she said. “You’d see it at the nail first. Your capacity must be high.”

“That is what they said about my father.”

She waited.

“Lord Matthias Reyne,” said Kasimir, to his hands, “bore for the Court for thirty years, and everyone said his capacity was boundless. Then he could not change his mind about which coat to wear, and then he could not climb the stairs. At fifty he went to stand in the chapel at Reyne House, and he is standing there still.” He was quite steady. “They keep him dusted.”

The candle hissed.

“I write perfect charters,” said Kasimir, “because a perfect charter does not strain. If the record is exactly true, the land does not pull against it, and I do not carry. That is why I check every link. That is why I keep this.” He touched the notebook in his coat. “Every error is a place where the land might one day pull against me. I have spent my whole life making sure there is nothing in the world that can pull.” He looked at the folio. “And every morning for five months, someone has been running a line under my hand and feeding me weight. Quietly. Without asking. The one thing I have spent my life—” He stopped. “Forgive me. I am not usually like this.”

“I know,” said Ilse. “I’ve watched you.”

He gave a short laugh with no breath in it.

“You can stop it taking any more,” she said. “Not what’s already there. That stays filed. But new lines. Sign off-register. Set your mark a sixteenth of an inch high of the rule and turn it a little sunwise, so it doesn’t sit on the knot. That’s enough. We’ve been doing it since Frost. The stretch wobbles a little, but it holds.”

Kasimir stared at her. “Deliberately sign out of true.”

“Yes.”

“On a College sheet.”

“Yes.”

“That is—” He searched for the word, and she watched him find it and decide, with visible pain, to say it. “That is a lie.”

“It’s a small one,” said Ilse. “On a College sheet. Welcome.”

He looked at her for a long moment in the candlelight, and then, unexpectedly, he put his hand over his eyes the way he had on Errata Night, and his shoulders shook once, and when he took the hand away he was nearly smiling.

“Recorded,” he said.

* * *

Afterward they sat on the bench with the candle between them, and for a while neither said anything. The wind had got up off the sea and was worrying at the roof, and in the rafters a loose slate ticked.

“The Provost could have given the order,” said Ilse at last. She knew as she said it that she was saying it to hear how it sounded. “He could have told someone in the Register what to do.”

“Who?” said Kasimir, quite gently.

She did not answer.

Who orders the paper for the Survey folios, and signs for it, under a protest nobody heard but his own clerk. Who insists, kindly, always kindly, that every sheet be signed in the Antechamber and nowhere else. Who knows the seventh Law and does not teach it, *for the same reason one doesn’t show children where the knives are kept*, and made the whole lecture hall laugh when Kasimir asked about contrary records. Who travels. Who wears grey kid gloves at all times, for frostbite. A College gentleman, out of the Meridian, all the way from the Cape; ever so polite. Asked the way to the old milestone. A gentle old man with grey hands, Nan Ottery Tenter had said in her smoky wagon at Corrow, polite as a parson. Asked the way to the Corrow stone.

The pieces went into place one after another, each one with a small exact sound, like links going into a canvas bag. She had always loved that sound.

“He gave me tea,” she said.

Kasimir turned his head.

“Seed-cake. From his sister’s girl in Lisk. He gave me the corner piece because it had the most caraway.” She could hear her voice and it sounded, still, quite ordinary, which was odd, because nothing else was. “He said he’d speak for my hand at the Board. He said Brask was the bravest woman he ever knew. He said every map is an act of love.” She swallowed. “He sat with Hen. He told me that. He said she was very polite to him.”

“Miss Marrow.”

“I told him.” She looked at the sheet under the candle. “In Deepwinter. I found the root lines on one of our old folios and I didn’t know who else to take it to, so I took it to him. I told him there were lines under the marks. I told him they ran to the margin and joined. He asked me if I’d told anyone else and I said no. He asked me where the sheet was and I said I’d put it back.” She laughed, or something did. “I lied to him to protect my friends from *him*, and I didn’t even know that was what I was doing. And that night someone came into our room while we were asleep and took it out of my Mensuration book and shut the door behind them and put the latch down.”

The slate ticked in the rafters. The wind leaned on the shed.

She thought, without wanting to, of how kind he had been. That was the part she could not make go anywhere. It had not been a performance. She had spent her whole life watching people perform kindness for her, magistrates and stewards and the Provost with his silver pot, and she knew the shape of it, and that afternoon by the fire had not had that shape. He had been glad of her company. He had been sorry for Hen. He had meant every word about the Reach and the children of Hobbet, and he had walked up the Little Wick lane in his kid gloves and let it go, with Tam Brewin coming down it singing.

The kindness had been real. That was the worst of it. The man was this, and the kindness had been real, both at once.

Two true things.

Kasimir was watching her. He had not moved closer and he did not touch her, and she was grateful for both. After a while he took his notebook out of his coat and opened it at the page with the long list of every way he might be wrong, and looked at it for a moment, and then turned to a clean page and uncapped his pen.

“What are you writing?” she said.

“The date,” said Kasimir, “and what you have just told me.” He wrote it in his small perfect hand, and blotted it, and looked up at her across the candle. “He asked whether you had told anyone else. You said no. He took the sheet. He has written you a note since, I imagine, telling you to be patient.”

“Burn this,” said Ilse. “It said burn this.”

“Then he has stopped asking himself whether you understand what you found.” He closed the notebook. “He will not ask again.”

Ilse looked at the empty peg by the door where the Long Lie had stood for nineteen years, and at the sheet under the candle with its four small rivers running the wrong way into the dark, and at her own right hand, the signing hand, resting on her knee.

“No,” she said. “He knows I know.”
