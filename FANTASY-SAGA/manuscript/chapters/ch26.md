# Chapter 26
## What the Sallow Gave Back

The cart road from the College to Verity Cross was two miles of frozen ruts, and Kasimir Reyne walked every one of them as though he had been asked to assess it.

He placed his boots on the crowns of the ruts rather than in the troughs, where the ice was thinner. It was the walk of a man who had been taught as a boy that the ground owed him an explanation. Ilse had walked these ruts all autumn behind Brask with an armful of chains, and she let her feet find their own way, and twice in the first mile she had to wait for him.

“You might have brought a lantern,” he said, the second time.

“You might have brought one. You’re the one with the coat that costs more than my parish.”

“Lanterns on the cart road after Evening Bell are logged at the porter’s lodge.” He came level with her. Frost had got into his hair where it escaped his hat. “I did not think either of us wanted the porter to write down that the Chancellor’s son and the accused forger had gone out together to call upon the suspended Field-Surveyor.”

“Put like that it sounds like a song.”

“It sounds like a conspiracy, Miss Marrow, because it is one.”

She had no answer to that. The road went over the shoulder of the down, and Verity Cross lay below them, a scatter of roofs and three lit windows, with the sea a sound to the north. Two days since the crow-loft, since Kasimir had opened his notebook of errors on a perch-board spattered with crow-lime and shown her seven dates and seven travel warrants and one name. She had done her Morning Survey since and signed her sheets off-register, and sat in the second row while Aurek Hallam lectured on the Law of Witness with his gloved hands folded on the lectern. He had smiled at her once, kindly, over his half-moon spectacles, and she had smiled back. It turned out that a forger’s face was the last thing to go.

Brask’s cottage was the last on the cliff lane, low and whitewashed, with a crooked chimney and a yard full of lobster pots that had never seen a lobster. A light showed under the door. Ilse knocked twice, and the door opened before her hand had come down from the second knock.

Brask filled the frame. She had the Long Lie in her good hand, held crosswise like a bar, and her white hair stood up as if she had been running her fingers through it. Her eye went to Ilse, and then past Ilse to Kasimir, and stayed there.

“Marrow,” she said. “And the Chancellor’s boy. Is this an arrest?”

“No, Field-Surveyor,” said Kasimir.

“Pity.” She stood aside. “Wipe your feet. The floor’s the only thing in here I’ve attested.”

That was a lie, and a joke, and Ilse could not remember ever hearing Brask make both at once. She wiped her feet.

* * *

The cottage was one room and a loft, with books on every flat surface and several that weren’t flat: books propping a table leg, books stacked into a stool, a book face-down on the cold hearth with a cairn-crow feather in it for a marker. Brask had a spirit-lamp going on the table and a chipped brown teapot beside it, and she poured three cups of something that might once have been introduced to tea but had not stayed long in its company.

“Drink it,” said Brask. “It’s warm. That’s its only virtue.”

Kasimir drank without a flicker. Ilse thought better of him for that.

Then he laid his notebook open between the teapot and the lamp, and in complete, careful sentences he told Brask what he had told Ilse in the crow-loft. A list of voids, kept since his summer in the Fallowmarch. The Register’s day-books of travel warrants, which any member might read because nobody had ever thought to forbid it. Brine End, Fogmonth 10. Corrow, Frost 7. The Little Wick lane at Wick Fallow, Reaping 15. A sheep-drove at Marlow, a ford at Fennock, two more. Before each one, by a day or by three, a warrant for an inspection tour, and each warrant bore the same signature.

He did not say the name aloud. He turned the notebook round and let Brask read it.

Brask read the whole page, and then she put her good forefinger on the first line and went down the dates again the way she went down a field-book, looking for the place where the numbers would stop agreeing. They didn’t stop. At the bottom she laid her hand flat on the table, next to the stilled grey one, and left it there.

“Marrow,” she said. “You’ve something to show me, or you wouldn’t be here. You’d have sent him on his own and let me throw him in the sea.”

Ilse had brought that morning’s Morning Survey sheet in the lining of her father’s coat. She unfolded it on the table, lit a candle-end at the lamp and set it down on the boards so low that its flame stood level with the paper, and slid it slowly across.

On the sheet the Wren Stair and the Long Gallery lay in her own fair hand: three chains forty-one links, the angle of the landing, the bearing of the turned stair. At the foot was her wren’s foot, set a hair off its proper place. Under the grazing flame the paper rose into hills and valleys too faint to see by daylight, and among the valleys ran a fine pale violet line. It came in from the left edge, ran under where her mark ought to have been, missed it by the width of her deliberate skew, and went on off the right edge toward the binding. It didn’t branch. It *gathered*, like every such line on every sheet she had looked at, toward a single place.

“That is blind ink,” said Brask.

“Gall and alum and quicksilver. It’s printed on the sheets before we ever see them. Every sheet since 1201.”

“A ghost.”

“A ghost-line, under every mark in the College, running to one root.” Ilse moved the candle back. The violet line sank into the paper and was gone. “I don’t know where the root is. I know where it isn’t. It isn’t in the Antechamber. It isn’t anywhere a student can go.”

“And the Quiet.”

“Wil Ashdown went grey in Seedtime 1201. Dr. Orle has it in her ledger. Hen. Two in the second year. Teo’s fingertips.” She made herself say the last part. “I told him, Field-Surveyor. In Deepwinter I sat in his study and told him there were ghost-lines on the Morning Survey sheets. He gave me tea and thanked me and asked me to keep it quiet for the students’ sake. The next morning the sheet I’d filched was gone from my room.”

Brask didn’t move. Ilse watched the good hand on the table, because it was the only part of Brask telling her anything, and saw the fingers draw in against the wood until the nails were white.

“Gloves,” said Brask.

Neither of them answered. She wasn’t asking.

“He started with the gloves three winters back,” said Brask. “Old Sallow frostbite.” She made a sound that was not a laugh. “We came home off the Sallow in Highsun. There was no frost. I lay in the infirmary six weeks with my eye in a basin and he sat by me every day with his hands as pink as a baby’s. I thought, twenty years is a long time for frostbite to wait. And then I thought, he carried you twenty leagues, Odile. Let the man have his gloves.”

“You couldn’t have known,” said Kasimir.

Brask turned her eye on him. It was not a kind look. It was the look she gave a student who had said something soft where something measured was wanted.

“I can always have known, boy. That’s the job.” She looked down at her hand, and she opened it, deliberately, finger by finger, and laid it flat again. “I knew the wrong thing. That’s worse. I knew he came back certain.”

“Certain of what?” said Ilse.

“Of everything.” Brask reached past the teapot for her clay pipe and held it without filling it. “You never knew him before. Hallam at thirty was the best doubter I ever worked with. Give him a closed traverse and he’d find you three ways it might be wrong and a fourth you hadn’t thought of, and he’d be *happy* about it. He used to call the First Atlas the finest guess anyone ever made. He was my second on the Sallow because he argued with me. I wanted someone who’d argue.”

She turned the pipe in her fingers.

“Four didn’t come back. I lost the eye. Somewhere in the last of it, when he had me on his back and we were walking out on a bearing he’d taken off a cairn-crow, something happened to Aurek that I didn’t see, because I was mostly dead at the time. When I woke in the infirmary he was sitting there with his pink hands, and he was certain. The Sallow had given him back to me, and I was so grateful to have anyone given back that I didn’t count what was missing.” She set down the pipe. “He sat in front of the Board and he didn’t say I was mad. He didn’t say I was right either. He said the Drift was a danger to everything we loved and the College’s duty was to hold. He was never wrong about anything again. They made him Master of the Register six years later.”

Ilse thought about Hallam in the Antechamber, saying *every map is an act of love, my dear,* and about how she had believed him, and still did. That was the worst of it. She could not find the line he had moved, because she didn’t think there was one. He believed every word.

“Certainty’s a symptom,” she said slowly. “Of burden. That’s what Orle says. The mind goes rigid before the hand does.”

“Sometimes,” said Brask. “And sometimes a man watches four friends go into the ground like stones dropped down a well and decides he’ll never let anything move again, and he doesn’t need burden to do it. He does it with his whole heart. That’s the kind I’d be frightened of.” Her eye was dry and very bright. “I am, as it happens.”

Kasimir said, carefully, “So am I.”

“Good.” Brask pushed the teapot back to the middle of the table. “Then we’re all sensible. Drink your tea.”

* * *

The tea had gone cold, which did not make it worse. Brask made more, and while it stewed she walked the one room with the Long Lie, three paces and turn, the brass shoe ringing on the flags, while Ilse and Kasimir laid out what they had.

What they had was a notebook of dates, some sheets with violet under them, a doctor’s ledger and the word of a dead man’s apprentice.

“And no root,” said Kasimir. “We cannot point to the place the lines run. We can say only that they run.”

“Your mother would make something of it, boy,” said Brask. “You’ve thought of taking it to her.”

Kasimir did not deny it. He sat very straight with his cold hands round the cup.

“I have thought of it,” he said. “My mother has wanted the Register under the Court since before I was born. If I give her a Master of the Register who has been ghosting students, she will have it within the year. That would be convenient, which is not the same thing as just, and the Board would see only the convenience. A suspended Field-Surveyor. An accused forger. The Chancellor’s son.” He set the cup down. “We are the three least credible witnesses in the College. If I sat on the Board I would not believe us.”

“You wouldn’t believe us anyway,” said Ilse. “You don’t believe anything you haven’t measured.”

“That is why I would not believe us.”

Brask snorted. She stopped walking.

“Forty years,” she said. “He’s been the kindest man in that building for forty years. He held my hand in the infirmary, and he’s given his own money to every Walker who came through the Gate with holes in their boots. There are masters who’ll hear the word ghost and look at Marrow, because she knows how, and then at me, because I hate them, and then at you, because of your mother, and they’ll go to bed and sleep like lambs. You won’t undo forty years with a candle held sideways.” She thumped the Long Lie on the floor. “So. What does he want?”

“To hold the Reach,” said Ilse.

“That’s what he says. What does he *need?*”

“Signatures,” said Ilse. “Marks on sheets with his lines under them. Two hundred students a morning, a sliver each. That’s been enough for three years and it isn’t enough any more, or he wouldn’t be pruning parishes. The voids are getting closer together. Brine End and Corrow were three weeks apart.”

“So he needs more,” said Kasimir.

“He needs a great deal more, all at once.” Ilse looked at Brask. “Where does the whole College sign one sheet?”

Brask had already turned to the window, toward the Cape, as though she could see the Lantern Gate from here with its one light on the keystone.

“The Equinox Charter,” she said. “Thaw 22. Every member of the College under the arch at dawn. Three hundred marks on one folio, and the Master of the Register prepares the folio. He’s done it for thirty-odd years. Nobody watches him do it, because nobody ever has.” Her voice was quite level. “If I wanted to bind a whole College at once, that’s where I’d do it.”

Ilse felt it in her signing hand first, a cold ache across the knuckles. Hen had gone Quiet on a sliver a morning. She tried to think what a full measure would do, poured into three hundred people in a single dawn, and her mind wouldn’t draft it.

“The Long Measure comes first,” said Kasimir. He had a pencil out. “Thaw 21. The winning party’s route charter is presented at the Gate and becomes the basis of the Equinox Charter. That is the custom. He prepares the folio, but he prepares it from the winning charter.”

“He’ll have his own folio ready whatever happens,” said Brask. “But if the winning charter is good enough, he has to be seen to use it, or explain why not, in front of three hundred people and the Provost.”

“Four weeks,” said Ilse.

“Twenty-nine days,” said Kasimir, without looking up.

“Thank you, Mr. Reyne.”

“You are welcome, Miss Marrow.”

Brask looked from one of them to the other, and then she sat down heavily and put her face in her good hand for a moment, and when she took it away she was only tired.

“You’ll do it without me,” she said. “I’m not allowed past the porter’s lodge, thanks to you, boy.”

“Yes,” said Kasimir. “I am sorry for it.”

“Are you.”

He considered. “I am sorry that it was necessary. I am not sorry that I told the truth.”

“Hm.” Brask poured him more tea, which was perhaps a punishment. “That’s the first sensible thing anyone named Reyne has said in my hearing. Drink it, and go home, both of you, before the porter wonders. And Marrow.”

Ilse stopped with her coat half on.

“Be civil to him,” said Brask. “If he’s had your sheet out of your room, he’s watching to see what you do. Do nothing. Give him a student. Can you do that?”

“I’ve been doing it for two days.”

“I know. I can see it on you.” She looked at Ilse for a long moment, and her voice did something Ilse had not heard it do before. It went down. “It’s a hard thing to look at the kindest man you know and see what he’s become. I’ve had half an hour to do it in. You’ve had two days. Neither of us should have had to. Go on.”

* * *

They didn’t go straight to the Ranges. Neither of them suggested it. When they came in by the postern, Kasimir turned not toward the Lantern Gate but toward the bell-tower.

“There is a ladder from the bell-chamber to the leads above the Library of Record,” he said. “My cousin Lisbet showed me. She is a proctor and believes that rules are for other people’s cousins.”

“Is it logged at the porter’s lodge?”

“Nothing on a roof is logged, Miss Marrow. That is the principal charm of roofs.”

The leads were a flat valley between two slopes of slate, with a parapet that looked west. Ilse climbed out onto them and stood up, and the whole sky came down on her.

The air had been scrubbed with frost. Fallowmarch skies had always had a hedge at the bottom. Here there was nothing at the bottom but the Margin and then the Drift, and over the Drift the stars were wrong. They were the right stars in the right places, but at the rim of the world, out past the Wing, the low ones wavered. They didn’t twinkle. They slid gently back and forth, a finger’s width, the way a reflection moves in a pond when a fish turns under it. The horizon out there was not sitting still, and the stars rocked on it like lamps on a boat.

“I come up here,” said Kasimir, beside her, “to look at that.”

“It’s horrible.”

“Yes.” He leaned his forearms on the parapet. “I find it calming. I cannot say why.”

They stood there for a while. Below them the Drift Wing breathed out over the heath, and one window in the Third Range slid a little and stopped.

“My father,” said Kasimir, “was fifty when he stilled.”

He had told her this in the crow-loft, in four sentences, like an entry read out. She waited to see if there would be a fifth.

“He bore for the Court. For duty, which is a kind of hire in which the wages are thanked rather than paid. Every boundary in the Lisk reclamations carries his mark. When the Ambry rose in ’93 and the levees strained, he felt it in his knees. I was eleven. He could not climb the stairs to say goodnight, and so I came down. Then he could not turn his head to see me in the doorway. Then he stood in the chapel at Reyne House, where he had asked to stand.” His voice was perfectly level. “I visited him every day for a year and then I stopped, because I found that I was talking to him as though he could hear me, and I could not bear to find out whether he could.”

The stars at the horizon rocked on their pond.

“That’s why you’re so good,” said Ilse. “Your charters. That’s why they’re flawless.”

“If a record is exactly true, the land does not pull against it. No strain. No burden.” He turned his hands over on the parapet and looked at them. “I have attested ninety-four records since I came to the College and I feel almost none of them. I have made myself very good, Miss Marrow, so that nothing will ever have a reason to hold on to me.” He laughed a little, without much in it. “And then I found my mark on a sheet with a violet line under it, and it was not my fault, and I could not have prevented it, and it was in me anyway. My hands are quite pink. I look at them every morning. I am terrified of being still.”

It was the plainest thing she had ever heard him say. She looked at his hands, long and clean and steady, and at her own, small and ink-stained, and found that she wanted to put one of hers on one of his, which was ridiculous. She kept them on the cold stone.

“I’m frightened of being found out,” she said. “That’s the obvious one. You found me out.”

“I did.”

“That’s not what I mean, though.” It was hard to measure something you had never let anyone look at. “Quince used to say a good lie is ninety-nine parts truth. A good lie’s just a true thing with one line moved. And I’ve started to think—” Out on the heath a light that might have been a wayhorn’s eye, or a lantern, or nothing, came and went. “I might be made the same way. Everything true about me except one line, and the line’s the bit that holds the rest up. If you took it out I don’t know there’d be anyone there. I’m frightened that I’m hollow. That it’s forgery all the way down.”

He didn’t say *you’re not*, which was what anyone else would have said. He was quiet for long enough that she thought he wasn’t going to answer, and she was obscurely grateful.

Then he said, “Ilse.”

She turned her head. He was looking at her, not at the horizon, and in the starlight his face had lost all its careful assessment and was only young and tired and very close. She could see that he did not know what he was going to say next. She had never seen that in him before, and it undid her more than anything he could have said.

Neither of them moved. A gull went over, very high, crying once. Out past the Wing the stars swayed and settled.

“It was a good forgery,” he said at last, quite low. “The Tull hedge. I measured it for three days. I could not find the line you had moved. I had to find the dead man’s mark instead.” He looked down at his hands. “You were not hollow when you drew it. Nobody hollow could have drawn it so well.”

It was not what either of them had been about to say. Ilse knew it, and knew that he knew it, and found that it would do, for now. She was smiling in the dark like a fool.

“That,” she said, “is the most Reyne compliment anyone has ever paid me.”

“I have been told I give very few.”

“You should be careful. It’ll go to my head.”

“I do not think,” said Kasimir Reyne, “that anything has ever gone to your head, Miss Marrow, without first being made to produce its papers.”

She laughed out loud, and the sound went off the leads and away over the Margin, and below them in the Second Range a window opened and someone shouted at them to shut up, some of us have Morning Survey. They ducked below the parapet like children and stayed there, backs against the cold slates and shoulders not quite touching, until the window shut again, and then a while longer, while overhead the stars stood fixed and true, and away at the edge of the world they rocked.
