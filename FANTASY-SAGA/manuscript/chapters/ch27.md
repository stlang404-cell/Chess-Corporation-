# Chapter 27
## Registration Guides

Mother Nettle had agreed to the plan with an enthusiasm that worried everyone except Cass.

“A crow from Tessary,” she said, rubbing her knuckly hands together. “For the Registrar-Adjunct personal, and won’t give it up to nobody else, and pecks. Oh, he’ll come. That one’d climb a burning ladder for a sealed letter.” She cackled. “Bugger me, I’ve not had this much fun since the Provost’s hat.”

“What happened to the Provost’s hat?” said Teo.

“Crows,” said Mother Nettle, with satisfaction.

It was Thaw 12, 1204 by the College clocks and still Candlemonth by the weather. The crow-loft ran the length of the roof-space at the top of the old tower, perch-boards in tiers and a hundred-odd cairn-crows muttering on them like a congregation waiting for a dull sermon. Along the sills and under the perches the crows had built their small stacks of pebbles, as they did wherever they slept. Ilse had counted them once: a hundred and twelve tiny cairns, each true to its shelf, the most honestly held objects in the College.

She sat at the far end of the long table with a lantern turned low and Sorry along her forearm. Teo stood by the hatch, not looking down through it. Cass sat on a crate beside it, flicking a sling-stone up and catching it.

At a quarter to nine the ladder creaked.

Merrin Coyle came up through the hatch head first, his ink-stained cuffs turned back and his anxious face lit from below by his lamp. He said, “Mother Nettle, I was told—” and then he saw Teo, and Cass, and Ilse, and he stopped with his shoulders out of the hatch like a man in a bath.

Cass stood up, let the hatch-cover fall shut behind him with a bang that sent forty crows into the air, and sat on it.

“Hey-ho, Master Coyle,” she said. “Mind the step.”

* * *

He didn’t try to run. He looked at Cass sitting on the hatch, and at Teo, who was very large and looked sorry about it, and then he came the rest of the way up and set his lamp down among the message-tubes. His hands were shaking. He folded them to stop it.

“Is this to do with Miss Marrow’s inquiry?” he said. “Because I only keep the folios—”

“It’s to do with the folios,” said Ilse. “Sit down, Master Coyle. Nobody’s going to hurt you. Teo, does he look hurt?”

“He looks cold,” said Teo. “I’ve got tea. It’s in a jar, which isn’t ideal, but jars are better than you’d think—”

Coyle sat and took the tea. Mother Nettle had retreated down the loft and was pretending to mend a nesting box with her head on one side like one of her birds.

Ilse unrolled a stolen Morning Survey sheet and drew a lit candle-end across it, low, so that the grazing light made the paper into a country of tiny hills. The violet line came up beneath her skewed wren’s foot the way a fish comes up under ice.

Coyle’s face told her everything before he spoke. It wasn’t surprise. It was the face of a man meeting an old acquaintance somewhere he had hoped never to.

“The guides,” he said.

“What guides?”

“The registration guides.” He swallowed. “I print them. Every sheet. There’s a second plate. Master Hallam gave it to me in 1201, when we changed to the Lisk paper, and the ink with it. It’s a guide-print. It keeps the students’ drafts square to the Register sheets, so the filing will be true. He said—” Coyle stopped. He looked at the candle. “I asked why blind ink. He said, so as not to distract the students, my dear Merrin. They have enough to think about at First Bell.”

“Did you believe him?” said Ilse.

“Yes.”

She waited.

“For a year,” said Coyle. “Perhaps more. They use guide-prints in the Lisk counting-houses for ledgers; there’s nothing strange in it. But these don’t sit where a guide would sit. They sit under the marks. Always under the marks, and nowhere else. I told myself it was for the filing.” He looked up. “You know what it is. That’s why you’ve been looking at them.”

“I know what it is.”

“Then tell me.” His voice cracked. “Please. I’ve been afraid to know for two years, and I’m so tired of being afraid of it.”

So she told him what blind ink was for, in hedge country, and what a ghost-line did when it lay in perfect registration beneath another person’s mark. She did not tell him how she knew. He didn’t ask. Halfway through he put his face in his hands.

When she had finished, Teo sat down on the bench beside him, which made the bench complain, and laid his own hands on the table in the lamplight. The tips of his fingers were the colour of a slate roof after rain, fading to pink at the second knuckle.

“I’m not angry with you,” Teo said. “I want to be clear about that, because if I were you I’d be expecting somebody to be. I’ve just been signing my stretch every morning, very neatly. I’m quite proud of my mark.” He looked at the grey. “Hen signed more than I did. She can’t remember why things are funny.”

Coyle made a noise like something tearing.

“The Equinox folio,” he said. “Oh, Ninefold. The Equinox folio.”

“Tell us,” said Ilse.

“Other years I prepare it. I size the sheet and rule the margins, and he draws the frame on the morning, from the Long Measure charter.” Coyle was speaking fast now, before his courage closed. “This year he took it blank, on Candlemonth the third, and locked himself in his study with it three nights running. I saw his hands once, last Frost, when he took a glove off to wipe his spectacles. Grey to the wrist. Not like Master Anselm’s. Like stone. When he came out in Candlemonth he was grey in the face as well. He sat down on the Antechamber steps and I brought him water, and he patted my hand and said I was a good man.”

“The frame,” said Ilse. “The under-drawing. Did he do it himself?”

“He won’t let me near it. He won’t let me near the other one either.”

“What other one?”

“The master sheet. In his study, in the press by the window, under glass. I’ve only seen it from the door. He calls it the Conduit.” Coyle looked at the lamp. “He’s never been short with me in eleven years except once, the day I moved the sounder-ivory paperweight on his desk to dust under it. He took it out of my hand and put it back exactly where it was and said, *Please don’t,* in a voice I didn’t know he had.”

Ilse filed that, not knowing yet where it went.

“Why?” said Cass. She hadn’t moved from the hatch. “That’s what I want to know. The paper-walls do a lot of things for money and a lot for spite. Why’s a kind old man drain the Big House dry?”

Coyle was quiet so long that Ilse thought he would refuse. Then he said, very low, “I carry the lamp for him down to Seven, sometimes. He doesn’t let me past the grille. He goes in to the Zero Sheets with his own instruments and measures them, and he talks while he does it. I don’t think he knows he does.” Coyle wet his lips. “He says, a hair a season. He says, two hairs, this winter. He says she’s coming apart at the centre and the Provost won’t let him say so.” He looked round at the three of them. “The Zero Sheets are softening. He’s not stealing anything. He’s trying to save Tessary.”

At the far end of the loft Mother Nettle had stopped pretending to mend the nesting box.

Ilse understood that she had been wrong about the size of the thing. She had thought she was looking at a crime, a matter for a Board. It was a crime laid on top of the world. Underneath Hallam’s violet lines there was the Zero Stone, and a million people who had never heard of the Morning Survey, and the centre of the map going soft.

Sorry got up off her arm and walked across the table and sat down in front of Coyle and looked up at him with its amber eyes, and after a moment the Registrar-Adjunct put out one ink-stained finger and stroked it between its too-big ears.

“I’ll help,” he said, to the ossel. “I expect I’ll be very bad at it.”

* * *

The Errata Society met in the Mess kitchen on Thaw 14 after the last scullion had gone up to bed, under the copper pans hanging like bells, with Teo at the board making onion tart because, he said, he could not think about the end of the world without something in the oven. Kasimir sat at the end of the scrubbed table in his good coat as though he were sitting a Patent examination. He had come in by the scullery door, which no one had told him about.

“Brask’s answered,” said Cass. One of Nettle’s grey-necks had come in at the window and walked the length of the table eating crumbs. Cass shook a curl of paper out of its message-tube, read it, snorted, and passed it to Ilse.

It was four lines in Brask’s cramped, sloping hand.

*Coyle’s right. I’d bet my other eye on it. Aurek would never do this for less than the world.*

*Win. Draw it so it can’t be ghosted. If you don’t know how, you have a week. Find out.*

*Don’t lean on each other so hard you fall over.*

*Burn this. B.*

It went round the table and into the range.

“A week,” said Teo. “A week and a day. I’ve counted. I know you knew that, I just like to say it.”

“Before we come to how,” said Kasimir, “I think we must speak of whether.”

They all looked at him. He sat very straight.

“I do not like where my thinking goes,” he said, “and I would rather say it here than under the Gate. If Coyle is right, the First Atlas is failing. Tessary holds a million people. Master Hallam has found a way to hold them, at a cost. The cost is unjust. It is also smaller than the alternative.” He laid his hands flat on the table. “I am not saying that we should let him. I am saying that if we stop him, we must be able to say what happens to Tessary afterward, and I cannot.”

The range ticked. Teo had stopped rolling pastry.

“It’s about three hundred,” Teo said. “Us. The whole College. Against a million. If you just do the sum—” He stopped. He looked at his fingertips on the pastry board, slate-grey against the flour. “I hate that I can do the sum. I want you to know I hate it. My mam in Lisk would be in the million, near enough. My little brothers.”

“Do it for Corrow, then,” said Cass.

She hadn’t raised her voice. Her face was quite still.

“Do it for Brine End. Do it for the lane at your place, Ilse, with the carter in the ditch. He’s been doing your sum on us for three years, Teo. Edge for the middle. The middle’s worth more, so the edge goes. My nan’s wagon had a charter. Paper-wall charter, treaty of 1190, signed and filed. He walked up to the Corrow stone polite as a parson and asked the way, and next night the ground let go under the children. And there’s always a middle. That’s the thing about middles. You hold Tessary ten years by eating three hundred of us, and what do you eat in year eleven?”

Nobody answered.

“It don’t stop,” said Cass. “It only gets nearer.”

Kasimir inclined his head to her, gravely, as he did to Calder when she cited a precedent he had not known.

“Yes,” he said. “I think that is the answer to the argument. I wanted to hear someone make it who was not me.”

Ilse had been silent through all of it. She thought of Hallam on the Antechamber steps, grey in the face, patting Coyle’s hand. She thought of him at forty-four with pink hands, sitting by Brask’s bed every day for six weeks. She thought of Hen.

“He’s right that she needs holding,” she said. “Tessary. I believe Coyle. I believe Brask. The centre’s coming soft, and if nobody does anything it’ll come apart, and that’s a million people waking up to find their street’s wandered off.” She looked down the table at each of them. “Then we find another way to hold her. We don’t pay for it with Hen.”

“What other way?” said Kasimir, not unkindly.

“I don’t know.”

“That,” said Kasimir, “is a very honest answer and a very poor plan.”

“People have done more in a week and a day,” said Teo. “Not many. But some.”

So they made a plan, with the smell of onions and butter coming up out of the range, and Kasimir wrote it in his notebook of errors because he said that was where it belonged. It had three parts.

One. They had to win the Long Measure, so that their route charter was the one presented under the Gate and the one the Equinox Charter must be drawn from, in front of everyone.

Two. Their charter had to be drawn so that a ghost couldn’t take beneath it. Ilse knew a dozen ways to put a ghost under a mark and none to keep one out, except the off-register skew, and a skew on a whole charter would be a lie the size of the Drift Wing. The Wing would wander on it like the Lost Wing of 902. A ghost was patient. It only needed a line to lie under.

Three. On the morning, while the College stood under the Gate, Cass and Kasimir would go into Hallam’s study with Coyle and take the Conduit out of its press: Cass because she could move unseen, Kasimir because he could say *I am the Chancellor’s son* in a voice that would make a proctor think twice.

“Then there’s the other thing,” said Kasimir. He turned back a page. “The articles of the Long Measure. A party is four. It says so in the fourth article. Miss Solberg is in the infirmary.”

“So we’re three,” said Cass. “So what?”

“A party of three may compete under the short-party penalty. One hour added to its time.” Kasimir looked up. “I would join you if I could. If I leave Orla and Piety and Absalom, I will have bought your hour with theirs. And Absalom cannot read a sightvane without help.”

“You’re a terrible snob,” said Cass.

“I am an accurate snob.”

“An hour,” said Ilse. She did the sum without wanting to, and it was not a good sum. Last year’s winning party had come home in a little under twenty hours, and Kasimir’s party would expect to beat that, because Kasimir was Kasimir. “We’d have to beat everyone else by an hour and then some.”

“Then we beat everyone by an hour and then some,” said Teo, and shut the oven door.

* * *

On Thaw 18 she took Hen a twist of salt caramels, because Hen had once eaten nine in an evening and claimed it was medicinal. Hen sat up in the third bed by the infirmary window with her grey hands on the coverlet, her hair brushed too flat by someone who was not Hen. She said, “Thank you,” and put the caramels on the side table unopened.

Ilse told her they would run the Long Measure as three, because the articles said four and Hen was a lazy cow lying about in bed. Two months ago Hen would have thrown a pillow at her. Now she looked at the window for a while.

“Three is a bad number for a chain,” she said. “Someone has to hold both ends.”

“I know.”

“Teo will want to hold both ends. Don’t let him. He’ll pull too hard.” Hen’s flat voice didn’t change at all. “Cass should lead. Cass can feel which way it goes.”

Ilse took Hen’s grey hand, which was cool as a stone in shade, and put a caramel in it and folded the fingers over. At the door she looked back. Hen was looking down at her own closed fist, as though she had found a thing in it she couldn’t remember asking for.

* * *

The Lantern Count began at Evening Bell on Thaw 20, the eve of the Long Measure, with the whole of the College standing in the Old Court in the cold holding unlit lanterns.

Three hundred people stood on the honey-coloured flags in coats and scarves: masters in gowns, proctors with staves, Halloway with all three watches out at once, Mother Nettle with a crow on each shoulder. The brass studs of the meridian line ran through the crowd, and people stood carefully on either side of it, so that there was a narrow empty lane down the court where nobody stood at all.

A proctor on a ladder lit a taper from the keystone lamp and handed it down to the Provost, and Steyne lit the first lantern and passed the flame to the masters. Hallam lit his own and turned and lit the next, which belonged to a frightened first-year from the Ammonite Hills, and said a word that made her laugh. Then the light went out through the crowd hand to hand until the Old Court was full of small flames. Ilse lit hers from Teo’s, and Teo lit his from Cass’s, and Cass had lit hers from Orla Devane, who had said “Don’t set your hair on fire, Tenter,” and been told “Don’t set your mouth on fire, Devane,” and both had looked pleased.

Across the court Hallam’s eyes found her over his spectacles. He lifted his gloved hand. Ilse lifted hers and smiled at him with her forger’s face, and he smiled back with his kind one, and then the line began to move and he was lost behind lanterns.

They went out under the Gate two by two, the Provost first, and onto the causeway into the First Range. As each pair passed under the keystone they began to count.

Teo had explained it at supper with gravy on his chin. The Wing from the Gate to the far end of the Third Range was one thousand three hundred and six paces at the founders’ stride, and everyone counted their own aloud, so that everyone was on a different number and all the numbers went down the Wing together.

“One,” said Ilse, under the keystone. “Two. Three.”

Behind her Teo said, “One,” and Cass, “One,” and ahead of her the Provost was already at forty, and the numbers rose around her like water in a lock.

The First Range was nearly stable, and the counting there was crisp. Then the causeway kinked into the Second Range, and the floor took on its faint give, like a plank laid over a ditch. Three hundred voices were counting now, each on its own step. It wasn’t a chant, because nobody was together. It was more like rain: every drop falling in its own place, and all of them together one steady sound that filled the corridors.

“Two hundred and twelve,” said Ilse. “Two hundred and thirteen.”

The level was steadier than she had ever felt it in the Wing.

She noticed it the way you notice that a pain has stopped. Every morning at First Bell the Long Gallery shifted under their chain and the bead in the levelglass swam, and they wrestled it back onto the sheet and signed. Tonight there wasn’t a drop of ink on the whole procession, and the Wing was quiet around them. It wasn’t still. It moved under the counting the way a sleeping dog moves under a hand, settling, as though it liked being walked.

At the turn of the Wren Stair Ilse looked back from the window and saw the procession behind her: a line of small lights strung along the whole length of the Wing, back over the kink of the causeway to the Gate, where the keystone lamp burned over the last of the leagues still filing through. The line bent where the Wing bent. It swayed a little where the Wing swayed. It was like looking down from a hill on a road at night, a road made of nothing but people walking it.

“Five hundred and eight,” said Cass, behind her, and then, very softly, under the count, so that only Ilse could hear it, she began to hum.

It was the Corrow song, from the night the whole camp had sung their wagons to the firm place. *The road to Corrow goes by the high stone when the moon is new.* Cass was only humming the tune between her numbers, probably without knowing she did it.

“Five hundred and nine,” said Ilse, and missed a step, and had to catch it up.

Something was nagging at her, the forger’s itch at the back of the neck that told her when a sheet was wrong. But nothing here was wrong. That was the trouble. She looked at the lanterns going ahead of her into the Third Range, swaying with the floor, held by nothing but people counting their way along. She thought of Nan Ottery in the firelight with her pipe. *Paper says where a thing is. Song says where a thing goes.*

She thought of the Ladder, in the dark, with the room turning under them and Halloway’s delight. She couldn’t think why she had thought of the Ladder.

“Six hundred,” said Teo, at her shoulder, in his soft voice, and added, “I’m sorry, I know you know, I just like the round ones.”

The thought went, as names go when you reach for them too fast. Ilse let it. She counted. They went on under the glass dome of the Observatory, where the lanterns filled the curve of the dome with small gold reflections. A thousand. A thousand and one. At the far end of the Wing, where the last window looked out over the black Margin, the line turned back on itself, and now the counts going out and the counts coming home passed each other in the corridors, numbers rising and numbers falling, voices brushing past in the lamplight.

On the way back, somewhere in the Second Range, Kasimir passed her going the other way, counting in his clear exact voice, “Nine hundred and forty, nine hundred and forty-one.” His eyes met hers over his lantern. He didn’t break his count, and neither did she. But he lifted the lantern an inch as he went by, the way a man on a road at night lifts his light to someone coming the other way, to say that he has seen them, and that the road is clear.
