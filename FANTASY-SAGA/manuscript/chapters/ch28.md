# Chapter 28
## The Long Measure

At a quarter to six on the morning of Thaw 21, forty-seven students stood under the Lantern Gate with chains on their shoulders, and Magistra Ines Calder read them the articles of the Long Measure as though each one had personally offended her.

“The course,” she said, “is the Tern Stack. Three leagues west by south-west. You will survey your route out and your route back by instrument, closing each leg. You will set and attest a station-pin at each closing, so that the route holds behind you for your return.” She paused, long enough for a gull to cross the arch. “At the Stack, one member of each party will re-attest the beacon cairn. The cairn has been re-attested every year since 211. I do not intend it to fail in mine.”

“Accuracy first, time second. Your charters will be judged at this Gate. The winning charter will be the basis of tomorrow’s Equinox Charter.” Calder lowered the paper and looked along the parties over the top of it. “Twelve parties. One under the short-party penalty, which adds an hour to its time, by the fourth article, as in *Penwarden’s Party v. the Proctors*, 1131. Miss Marrow.”

“Magistra.”

“You understand the penalty.”

“Yes, Magistra.”

“And you are content to run as three.”

“No, Magistra,” said Ilse. “But we’re running.”

Calder wrote something down, and Ilse would have given a good deal to know what.

The keystone lamp burned overhead and the sky behind the Drift Wing had gone the colour of a bruise healing. Ilse had the plane-table board strapped to her back and the levelglass on a cord round her neck, and inside her coat Sorry was a small warm weight that purred whenever she breathed in. It had refused to stay in the room. It had sat in the doorway and made a sound like a kettle until she gave in. Cass had the sightvane and the route-cords. Teo had the chain over his shoulder and a face the colour of porridge.

“I’m fine,” he said, before she could ask. “I had two breakfasts. I’m mainly fine.”

Kasimir’s party stood three places down: Orla yawning, Absalom Grice bouncing on his toes, Piety Mercer with her lips moving in prayer or arithmetic, and Kasimir, still and combed, counting the links of his chain by touch. He didn’t look at her. She hadn’t expected him to.

Then the masters came down the line to wish the parties well, and Hallam was among them.

He went from party to party with his gloved hands clasped behind him and a word for each. He told Absalom Grice to look before he leapt, and Absalom laughed far too loudly. He came to Ilse’s party last and looked at the three of them over his half-moon spectacles with real tenderness, and Ilse made her face into the face of a student being told a kind thing by a kind master.

“Three,” said Hallam. “Three brave Walkers and a penalty. Miss Solberg would be proud of you.” He laid a gloved hand on Teo’s arm, briefly, and then on Cass’s, and Cass stood it with only a slight tightening round her eyes. Then he turned to Ilse, and his voice dropped, so that it was for her alone.

“Come home safe, my dear.”

“I mean to, Master Hallam.”

He patted her shoulder and went back to stand with the Provost. Ilse had spent nine years learning to hear the second line under a sentence, and she listened now with everything she had, and could not tell whether there was one. He might have been wishing her well. He might have been telling her he knew. He might have been doing both at once, with his whole heart, and that was the possibility that frightened her most.

The Bell of Hours struck six. Calder said, “Go,” and forty-seven students went out under the lantern into the Margin.

As they came off the causeway onto the heath Ilse looked back once. On the low rise beyond the College wall, outside the grounds, where nobody could say she had trespassed, a tall figure stood against the grey sky with one hand on a staff. Brask didn’t wave. She lifted the Long Lie a foot off the ground and set it down again, once, the way she did in Fieldwork when someone had finally closed a traverse.

* * *

They did the Margin fast, because the Margin was theirs.

Cass led, because Hen had said so, and Teo had said “Of course Cass leads, Cass can feel it, I mostly feel my breakfast.” Cass went ahead with the sightvane and the forward end of the chain, reading the grain off the gorse and picking ground that would stay put long enough to measure. Teo held the back end and called the links. Ilse drafted at every station, and they ran each leg in the quick sequence they had learned on the Walk: chain, bearing, level, draft, close, attest the pin, go. A station-pin was an iron spike with a charter the size of a playing card wired to it, and when Ilse set her wren’s foot to each one the ground round the spike went firm for a yard and she felt a small, distinct tug in her signing hand. Twelve pins by the edge of the Margin. Twelve small tugs.

By nine they were into the near Drift, and the land began to have opinions.

The first was the standing rain. It stood on a long low hill like a grey curtain hung from nothing, falling on the hill and only on the hill, out of an otherwise clean sky, its edge as sharp as a wall. Cass put her hand into it up to the wrist and it came out dripping while her elbow stayed dry.

“It’s been raining there since Candlemonth,” she said. “Uncle Pell says. There’s a frog in there the size of a dog.”

“There is not,” said Teo.

“Size of a small dog.”

They chained through it, because going round would have cost a mile. Inside, the light was green and the noise was everything, and Ilse drafted under her coat with Sorry complaining against her ribs. She closed the leg at fourteen chains and sixty links with an error of two links, wrote the error down, and was absurdly proud of it. They came out into sunshine with water running out of their boots, and behind them the rain went on falling on its hill as if they had never been there.

The second was the skein.

They came over a rise and saw it. A stream came down off the downs in an ordinary bed of stones, and at the bottom of the slope, instead of going on, it rose. It went up off the ground in a smooth glassy arch, twenty feet high at the crown, and came down again forty yards farther on, and ran away across the heath in its bed as though nothing had happened. Under the arch the grass was dry. Through the water, as through a window, the sky showed rippled and green.

In the arch there were eels. They were long and silver and thin as a boot-lace, and they hung in the moving water with their heads into the current, and every now and then one of them slipped out of the top of the arch into the open air and swam a yard of nothing, flicking, before it fell back in.

Nobody said anything for a while. Ilse found that her pencil was shaking very slightly above the board, and not with fear.

“Skein-eels,” said Cass softly. “Nan says if one touches you, you’ll never drown.”

“Is that true?” said Teo.

“No idea. Nobody I know ever got touched.”

They measured the skein, because Ilse could not bear to leave it off the sheet. She took the height of the arch from two stations and noted beside it, *the stream rises here, 20 ft at crown, and falls again at 2 chains; this is the bed of a stream, not its course.* She looked at that for a moment afterward. She didn’t know why it pleased her so much.

The third was the fold.

They walked into it in the early afternoon without seeing it. One step the air was the thin cold of the Thaw; the next it was warm as a bread-oven, so suddenly that Teo yelped. The fold lay in a hollow of heath a hundred yards across, a pocket of summer that had got lost, and in it the gorse was in flower and the air was full of moths, small and pale and powdery, turning in slow drifts over the yellow gorse the way snow turns in a draught. When Ilse breathed out, the nearest moths tumbled away on her breath and came back.

“Fold-moths,” said Cass. “They don’t leave. They’re born in it and they die in it. Whole lives in one warm room.”

“That’s very sad,” said Teo.

“Is it?” said Cass, and looked round at the flowers and the moths and the gold light, and didn’t seem to think so.

* * *

The hollowings found them at the glasswrack.

The glasswrack fields lay in a long shallow valley a league short of the sea, where the wind came straight in off the water. The glasswrack grew in spirals out of pale sand, crystal stems twisted like barley-sugar, and in the wind they rang like a hundred wet fingers drawn round the rims of a hundred glasses, so that the whole valley hummed a chord that never resolved. It was very beautiful and very hard to think in.

She was setting the fortieth pin when the level dropped out of her ears.

It went the way it had gone at Brine End: not fading but cut, a held note stopped mid-breath. She looked up. Across the valley, beyond the ringing spirals, the air had gone soft in a patch the size of a cottage. It wouldn’t come into focus. The glasswrack under it had stopped ringing. The patch slid toward them slowly, as unhurried as a cloud shadow, and behind it the sand slumped in a long shallow furrow.

Beyond it there was another, and another, farther back along the routes the other parties had taken. Ilse could see their station-pins from here, iron points catching the low sun, and around every pin the air was blurring.

“Road and rain,” said Cass. “Look at them all.”

“The pins,” said Ilse. “Hundreds of fresh pins all down the course. All new attestations. That’s what draws them.”

“I’ve never seen so many,” said Teo. His voice had gone small. “Nobody’s ever seen so many. Have they? Marrow. Have they?”

Away to the north someone was shouting. One of the Ammonite parties was running back the way it had come, and an empty board lay on the sand behind them with a blur sliding over it.

The nearest hollowing was a hundred yards off and closing.

“Cass,” said Ilse.

“I see it.” Cass had her head up like a dog scenting. “Ground there,” she said, and pointed with her chin. To their left the floor of the valley was moving, a long tongue of loose pale sand sliding gently seaward with the glasswrack riding on it, ringing as it went. “Get on the moving bit. They don’t like it. Nan says.”

“Nan says a lot of things,” said Teo.

“And she’s alive at seventy-nine, ain’t she? Move.”

They picked up the chain and the board and went, half-running, onto the sliding sand. It was like stepping onto a barge. The ground went out from under their feet and came back, carrying them, and Ilse staggered and caught Teo’s arm and felt the sand flow seaward under her boots at about the pace of a slow walk. The glasswrack spirals went by them ringing.

Behind them the hollowing reached the edge of the moving ground and stopped.

It drifted a little along the edge of the slide and back, like a moth against a lit window, unwilling. The sand under it was still. The sand under her was moving. It would not cross. After a while it turned and slid back toward the line of pins, where the ground stood fixed and freshly signed.

Ilse watched it go with her heart going hard. A thought came and went too fast to catch, like the one in the Lantern Count. It had something to do with the skein. *This is the bed of a stream, not its course.* She reached for it and it went.

“Marrow,” said Teo. “Marrow, we’re going quite fast toward the sea.”

They got off the slide at the far end of the valley, and Ilse took a bearing back to the fortieth pin and closed the leg across the moving ground with a note that said *bed moving seaward, about 1 chain in 3 minutes*, and they went on.

* * *

They came to the Tern Stack at nightfall, first of the twelve.

It stood in the shallows a furlong off the last of the land, where the Drift ran out into the sea. They went out along a spit of shingle in the last of the light with the tide hissing on both sides. Close to, the Stack was a black tower of rock a hundred and forty feet high, streaked white by a thousand years of birds and cut with ledges where the terns slept. On its flat top, against the last green of the western sky, Ilse could just make out the beacon cairn: a stack of dressed stone with an iron fire-basket on it, unlit.

Teo stood at the foot of it and looked up and didn’t say anything at all.

A notice was nailed to a post at the foot of the climb under oiled cloth. Ilse read it by lantern. *The cap of the Stack was fractured in the Frost storms. Part of the summit is hollow. Climb with care. Do not stand on the seaward side of the cairn.*

“Hollow,” said Cass. “Lovely. Which part?”

“Doesn’t say.”

“’Course it doesn’t.” Cass looked up at the Stack and flexed her hands. “Right. I’ll go. I’m lightest after you, and you’re the one with the board, and I can read the rock—”

“You can’t sound it,” said Teo.

They both looked at him.

His face in the lantern light was grey and wet and absolutely set. He had put down the chain. His hands were shaking, and he put them in his armpits to stop them.

“The cap’s cracked,” he said. “Somebody has to know where it’s hollow before they put their weight on it. You can read which way the rock leans, Cass, but you can’t hear what’s inside it. I can. That’s my whole—that’s the only thing I’m actually for.” He swallowed. “It’s a hundred and forty feet. I worked it out on the way. Call it a hundred and eighty handholds, if they’re a good foot apart, which they won’t be, because nothing ever is. I’m going to count them. Don’t talk to me unless it’s about rock.”

“Teo,” said Ilse.

“I know.” He looked at her. “I know, Ilse. I’m going anyway.”

He had never called her Ilse when he was frightened. He called her Marrow when he was frightened. She understood that he had gone past frightened into somewhere else, and she didn’t argue.

There was a horn of rock at the foot of the Stack, worn smooth by a century of ropes, and Ilse took two turns of their line round it and braced the line across her back, so that if Teo fell the rock would take his weight and not her. Cass tied the other end round Teo’s chest in a Tenting knot with a twist in it. Teo put the levelglass and a folded folio in his coat and a hooded lantern on his belt, and Sorry put its head out of Ilse’s coat and made a small sound at him.

“Thank you,” said Teo to the ossel, very seriously, and put his hands on the rock.

“One,” he said.

He went up slowly. That was the first thing. He didn’t rush, and he didn’t freeze; he went up one hold at a time, and at every hold he stopped and knocked on the rock above him with his knuckles, the way he had knocked on the walls of the Ladder in the dark, and listened. Then he said the number and moved.

“Eleven. Twelve.”

“Lean left,” Cass called up. She was standing back on the shingle with her head tipped right back, reading the face of the Stack the way she read a heath. “Rock’s sound there. The grain goes in, not out. Left, Teo. That’s it.”

“Thirteen.”

Ilse paid out the line. It went up through her hands rough and wet and alive, and she felt every one of his movements down it, the pauses while he knocked and listened. The light went. The lantern on his belt became a single point of gold moving up the black rock, and the stars came out above it, and the tide came in round the spit with a long hush.

“Forty-four. Forty-five.”

“Big ledge above you. Terns on it. Don’t mind them.”

“I mind them a great deal.” A burst of furious wings and screaming above, white shapes boiling out into the dark. Teo’s light stopped. The line went rigid in Ilse’s hands, and her heart stopped with it. Then: “Forty-six.”

It went on longer than Ilse would have believed a thing could go on. Her arms burned and went numb and burned again, and her hands bled where the line ran. She noticed only the gold point of the lantern and the numbers coming down out of the dark. Ninety. A hundred and two. Teo’s voice had gone hoarse and very steady, the voice of a man reciting something he knew by heart. Once he stopped for so long that Cass cupped her hands and shouted “Teo!” and the answer came back, perfectly calm, “I’m sounding. Shut up,” and Cass laughed out loud with tears on her face.

“A hundred and sixty. A hundred and sixty-one.”

“You’re nearly at the cap. Go right of the crack. Right, Teo, the grain goes back on itself left of it—”

“I can hear it. It’s hollow on the left. Like a pie with no filling.” A pause. “Sorry. That wasn’t rock.”

“A hundred and seventy-eight,” said Teo, from very far above them. “A hundred and seventy-nine.”

Then the gold point went over the edge and was gone, and the line went slack in Ilse’s hands, and from the top of the Tern Stack, a hundred and forty feet up in the dark, came a sound that it took her a moment to recognise. It was Teo, laughing.

They waited. Up there he would be sounding the cap, measuring the cairn by lantern, drafting with his big careful hands, setting the stones of the beacon in their true places on the page. Then he would sign.

She felt it when he did. Everyone on the spit must have felt it, because Cass made a small noise. It was like a bell struck once, far off: the level in her ears rang, clear and certain, and the black Stack above them steadied as though it had taken a breath and decided to keep it.

Then the beacon lit.

It went up all at once, a bloom of orange in the iron basket, and the whole Tern Stack leapt out of the dark: the white streaks on its sides, the ledges, the terns wheeling round it in the firelight like sparks. Far out on the black water a road of orange light ran away toward the west. Ilse stood with the line in her bleeding hands and looked at it, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and her friend had made it.

“He did it,” Cass was saying, over and over, “he bloody did it, the great soft lump, he did it,” and Sorry had climbed out of Ilse’s coat entirely and was sitting on her shoulder with its ears straight up, staring at the fire with its amber eyes.

Coming down took him an hour. He counted that too.

When his boots touched the shingle he stood there for a while with his forehead against the rock. Then he turned round, and he was grinning in a way Ilse had never seen him grin, the whole of his round face gone loose with it, and he held out the folded charter to her with both hands, the way you hold out something hot.

“Two hundred and three,” he said. “Up. I lied about the hundred and eighty. Two hundred and three handholds, and I sounded every one, and the cap’s hollow on the left like I said, and I signed it on the right. My hands are shaking. I don’t mind. I don’t mind at all.” He looked up at the fire. “I don’t like heights. I want to be clear. I still don’t like them.”

Cass flung her arms round his neck and nearly pulled him over. Ilse took the charter, and checked it by lantern, because she was a surveyor, and it was perfect. Then she put her bleeding hand on his arm and left it there, and he put his big hand over it, and neither of them said anything.

* * *

Kasimir’s party came down the spit forty minutes later, at a run, with their lanterns swinging.

They had seen the beacon. Absalom was red and blown, Orla wry, Piety white and furious. Kasimir stopped at the foot of the Stack and looked up at the fire, and then at the three of them standing under it.

He looked at Ilse for a long moment. Then he inclined his head to her, very slightly, the way one surveyor acknowledges another across a boundary they will both sign.

Ilse nodded back.

“Hey-ho, Reyne,” said Cass. “Mind the left side of the cap. It’s hollow. Teo heard it.”

Something moved in Kasimir’s face. “Thank you,” he said, to Teo, not to Cass, and meant it, and turned to rig his own line.

They couldn’t wait; they had an hour to make up. Teo, who had climbed the Tern Stack and could apparently now do anything, picked up the pack without being asked, and they went back along the shingle with the beacon throwing their three long shadows out ahead of them over the glasswrack.

At the landward end of the spit Ilse put her eye to the sightvane to take the back-bearing on their last pin, and it wasn’t there. The bearing ran out to empty sand. She swung the vane and found the pin, still upright, catching the beacon light on its iron head, eleven yards south of where she had set it, and beyond it the long ridge they had crossed that afternoon was sliding, very slowly, the whole black length of it, as patient as a tide. Cass had already seen. She hitched the route-cords at her belt, said, “Ground’s going home without us,” and began to walk faster.
