# Chapter 29
## The Open Map

By midnight they had re-chained the same leg three times, and it had moved three times, and Ilse had stopped being angry with it and started taking notes.

The ridge they had crossed that afternoon was going somewhere: south and a little west, toward the sea, at a pace she could see if she watched a single tuft of heather against a single star. Their pins went with it. Each one she reached she found carried off its mark and straining in the sand, the spike leaning, the card fluttering, the ground round it held hard while everything else flowed past. Each one pulled at her signing hand like a child pulling at a sleeve.

And the hollowings were everywhere.

They hung over the ridge in the dark, twenty or more, patches of blur that put out the stars behind them, clustered round the strained pins the way wasps cluster on a split plum. The level in Ilse’s ears kept going out and coming back like a lamp guttering.

“Leave the pins,” said Cass. “They’re eating ’em. We set ’em, and they come.”

“If we leave them we can’t close the route.”

“If we stay with ’em we can’t close nothing ever again.”

Ilse looked at her board, where the return leg wandered across the sheet in three attempts like a drunk man’s signature, and thought of the penalty hour, and of Hallam under the Gate with his own folio ready.

Then Teo said, “Listen.”

From beyond the ridge to the north came a girl’s voice, screaming, and under it a man’s shout cut short. Lanterns showed there, low down in a hollow between this ridge and the next. They weren’t moving forward. They were swinging, the way lanterns swing when the people holding them are trying to keep their feet.

“That’s Reyne’s lot,” said Cass. “They passed us an hour back on the straight line. I saw his light.”

Ilse could see the whole of it laid out like a sheet. If they went north, the penalty hour became three, and their charter came in late and broken, and Hallam drew his folio from someone else’s map. If they went on, perhaps they won.

Teo had already picked up the chain.

“Road and rain,” said Cass. “’Course we’re going. What are you stood there for?”

* * *

The fold was a long trough between two ridges, and both ridges were moving, and not the same way.

They came down into it sliding. The near ridge went south and the far one north, and the ground between them twisted like a wrung cloth, heaving one way and then back. In the middle of it, on a patch of turf coming apart at the edges, Kasimir’s party were holding on.

Orla Devane had a lantern in each hand and her feet braced wide. Absalom Grice lay on his back beside her with his face the colour of tallow and his left leg wrong below the knee, bent in a way legs don’t bend. Piety Mercer was on her knees beside him, and it was Piety who had been screaming, and she was screaming still.

“—*your* fault! Your Margin tricks, your filthy wagon songs, you’ve sung the ground loose under us, you—”

“Shut up, Piety,” said Orla, without heat. “Hello, Marrow. We seem to be in a spot.”

Kasimir knelt at the centre of the turf with his board across his knees and a lantern on the ground beside him. He was drafting. As Ilse slid down beside him he finished a sheet, set his mark to it, and pressed it flat on the turf with his palm, and she felt it take. The ground under them stiffened, held, for the space of two breaths.

Then it tore. She heard it, a sound like a wet sheet ripping, and the turf lurched, and Kasimir made a small sound through his teeth and doubled over his right hand.

“Again,” he said. His voice was shredded. “It is the latitude. It is enormous here. I chart it to its present position and it is not there by the time I have signed. I have done it nine times.”

“Stop,” said Ilse.

“If I stop, the ground goes out from under Absalom.” He was already ruling a fresh sheet. His right hand shook so badly that the rule skidded. In the lantern light the skin across his knuckles had gone a strange dull colour, like pewter under dust. “It is very simple, Miss Marrow. I must be faster than it.”

Above the fold the hollowings were gathering.

They came over the lip of the near ridge, one and then three and then more, sliding down toward the turf where Kasimir’s nine torn charters lay. Teo said, “Oh, no,” very quietly. Cass stood and opened her mouth to sing, the low Tenting song Brask had sung at Brine End, and her voice came out cracked and stopped. She tried again. Nothing came but breath. Ilse had seldom seen Cass afraid.

The nearest hollowing slid down the slope toward Absalom’s broken leg.

Cass grabbed Ilse’s coat. She tore it open, and Sorry tumbled out into the lantern light, small and grey, ears flat. And Cass Tenter, who believed it was wrong to describe a living thing into whatever you wanted, pointed at it and roared.

“That’s a *dog!* That’s the biggest, ugliest, fiercest dog that ever walked the Margin! That’s a dog with teeth like a gin-trap and a bark like the Bell of Hours, and it eats hollowings, and it *hates* them!”

Sorry flickered, and swelled, and stood up.

It was the size of a wolfhound and it seemed much bigger. Its coat stood out in a grey ruff like a thornbush and its jaws were wide and wet and full of teeth, and in its great shaggy head its eyes were still Sorry’s, pale amber and appalled. It stood over Absalom on legs like fenceposts. Then it lowered its head, and it barked, a sound that went off the walls of the fold like a shot, and it charged.

It hurled itself across the shifting sand at the hollowing, barking and barking, and the hollowing pulled back from the moving, roaring thing as the other had from the sliding sand. It slid away up the slope, and the dog went after it to the lip of the ridge and stood there bellowing at the dark.

Then it came back down to Ilse and stopped in front of her, and it shrank like a bellows emptying. In three heartbeats it was a small grey shape of no particular kind, all ears and eyes, curled in the sand. Ilse picked it up. It was asleep before her hands closed round it, breathing fast and shallow, and it was shivering.

“Sorry,” said Cass, hoarse. “Sorry, Sorry, I know. I know you hate it.”

Ilse buttoned the ossel back inside her coat. On the ridge there were more hollowings than before.

* * *

Kasimir set his mark to the tenth charter, and it held for two breaths, and it tore.

This time Ilse watched the ground rather than the sheet. She saw the turf hold, rigid, while the trough heaved around it, the sand piling against the held patch like water against a sluice gate. She saw the tear begin where the strain was greatest, and the ground break free and swing on, south, as it had been going to all along. And she saw the hollowings on the ridge lean toward the tear, all at once, like flowers toward a window.

His charter said the turf lay *here*. It was a beautiful charter. It was exact to a hair.

And it was true for perhaps a second. A second after he signed, the turf was a foot south of where the charter said, and then two feet, and then swinging back north past the place and beyond it, and for all that time the charter was wrong. Not much wrong. Wrong the way a stopped clock is wrong: a flawless record of an instant, held up against a thing that was never still.

*A record’s hold is proportional to its accuracy and completeness.* Hallam had taught them that in the Antechamber with his gloved hands on the lectern. The Law of Fidelity. Kasimir’s charters were accurate, but they weren’t complete. They left out the one thing about this ground that was most true about it. They left out that it moved.

The land pulled against them because they were wrong about it. The burden ran up Kasimir’s hand because they were wrong. The hollowings came to the fighting the way flies come to a wound.

Ilse knelt in the heaving sand and felt the whole of it come together in her head, piece by piece, like the pieces of a traverse closing.

The Ladder. The room turning under them in the dark, the moment she had stopped fighting the drift and measured its rate instead, and Halloway’s joy over the rate annotation. They had recorded the drift instead of correcting it, and the room had held.

The Moot. The stone grinding three inches toward her mark, because she had charted it where the sand wanted it to lie. *Fidelity to the ground, Mr. Reyne, not to the page.*

Nan Ottery in the firelight at Corrow, her pipe going. *Paper says where a thing is. Song says where a thing goes.* And Cass humming the Corrow song in the Lantern Count: *the road goes by the high stone when the moon is new, and the low ford when it’s old.* That wasn’t superstition. That was a survey of a road that moved, true at the new moon and true at the old, and the Tenting had walked by it for generations and never been lost.

Brask at her cold hearth. *The map is a promise, not a cage. Know which one you’re making.*

And her own pencil that afternoon, beside the skein: *this is the bed of a stream, not its course.* Beside the slide in the glasswrack: *bed moving seaward, about one chain in three minutes.* She had drawn two open things already today without knowing, and they were the two best lines on her sheet.

On the Walk, half a year ago, she had come to a stream bank that wouldn’t close by fourteen links, and she had corrected it, because she was a forger and forgers made things neat. Brask had laid a fingernail on that bank. *Forgers correct. Surveyors record.*

What was the truest thing you could say about this ground? That it lay here? It didn’t. That it lay nowhere? It did; it lay *somewhere*, and the somewhere had edges. It swung, and it swung between limits. Its latitude was enormous, but it wasn’t infinite. Latitude was only that: the range of states the land could be in.

So you didn’t chart a state. You charted the range.

You said: *this ground lies between here and here, and moves thus.* And every word of it would be true, at every instant, for as long as the ground kept doing what it was doing. A record like that would have nothing in it to be wrong. It would be more faithful than any point charter ever drawn, Kasimir’s included, because it was complete. By the Law of Fidelity it would hold. By the Law of Precedence it would beat any narrower record laid against it. And by the Law of Burden it would cost almost nothing to carry, because the land would have nothing to pull against. You wouldn’t be holding it still. You would only be telling the truth about how it moved.

It wasn’t a new law. It wasn’t a trick. It was a law she had been taught in her first month, followed all the way to the end.

“Kasimir,” she said.

He looked up from an eleventh sheet, half ruled.

“Stop charting where it is,” said Ilse. “Chart where it goes.”

* * *

She was not good at explaining. She was good at drawing, and she drew it for him on the back of his ruined tenth charter: two lines for the limits of the swing, an arrow between them, a number beside the arrow. She watched him understand it, and hate it, and go on looking anyway.

“That is not a charter,” he said. “A charter says where a thing is.”

“It does say where a thing is. It says all of where.”

“It is a—” He stopped. She could see him searching for the legal word and not finding one. “It has no fixed line in it.”

“There’s no fixed line in the ground.” She put her finger on the swinging limits. “I can feel the give. I can feel how far it’ll go before it can’t, the same way I felt how far a hedge would go. I can’t draw it fine enough. Not here, not to a hair. You can.” She looked at his hand, at the pewter dulling his knuckles. “You said it on the roof. If a record is exactly true, the land doesn’t pull against it. So make it exactly true.”

He looked at her in the swinging light. Then he wiped his rule on his sleeve and turned to a clean sheet.

They did it together, all seven of them, in the bottom of the moving fold, with the hollowings hanging on the ridge above.

Teo lay flat on the sand with his ear pressed to it, sounding, and counted the swing aloud, the way he had counted the Tern Stack. “Forty-one,” he said, “from the north limit to the south. Forty-one, and back in forty-three, it’s not even, it’s slower going north, it’s pulling uphill.” Cass knelt with her palms flat and read the grain, which way the swing leaned and where it bent. “Not straight,” she said. “It curves. Like a gate on a hinge. Hinge is east.” Ilse shut her eyes and felt for the give, the way she had felt for it in Widow Tull’s orchard with her palm on a wet sheet, and found the place at each end of the swing where the ground would go no farther, where it came up against its own latitude like a gate against its post. She put Orla there, at the north limit, with a lantern held high, and after a moment’s hesitation she put Piety at the south.

“Me?” said Piety. Her face was blotched with crying.

“You’ve got a lantern and two legs,” said Ilse. “Stand where I tell you and don’t move unless the ground does.”

Piety stood.

Kasimir took the limits. He sighted on Orla’s lantern and on Piety’s at every swing, again and again until the numbers stopped changing, and he drew the way she had always envied, without a wasted line. He put down the north bound and the south bound and the hinge, and the curve of the swing between them, and in the margin, in his beautiful exact hand, the rate: *forty-one seconds north to south, forty-three south to north, about a hinge bearing east by north.* It wasn’t a picture of a place. It was a picture of a movement, the first she had ever seen drawn true.

He held out the pencil to her.

They signed it one after the other on the open sheet, her wren’s foot and his mark beside it, and pressed it down on the turf between them, and Ilse felt it take.

It wasn’t like any attestation she had ever made. There was no tug in her signing hand. There was no pull. It was like setting down something heavy on a table built to hold it. The level in her ears, which had been guttering all night, came back clear and low and steady.

And the ground stopped fighting.

It didn’t stop moving. It went on swinging, north and south, on its long hinge. But the heave went out of it. The wrenching twist that had been tearing the fold apart eased, and the turf under Absalom stopped lurching and began, instead, to sway. Slow and gentle, forty-one seconds and forty-three, like a boat riding at anchor on a calm tide. Ilse felt it under her knees, and it didn’t frighten her at all.

On the ridge, the hollowings drifted.

They hung where they were for a while, as though puzzled. Then one by one they slid away over the lip of the ridge and out into the dark. There was nothing for them here any more, nothing fixed and fighting. Only ground going where it went.

The fold was very quiet. Absalom Grice said faintly, “Is it over? Is that it?”

And Cass began to laugh. She sat down in the sand and laughed with her head back and her braids swinging, laughed until she wept, and after a moment Teo was laughing too, and Orla, and even Kasimir, holding his ruined right hand in his left, put his head down on his knees and shook.

* * *

Afterward, while Teo splinted Absalom’s leg with Kasimir’s spare rule and two straps, Ilse sat with the open charter on her board and looked at it by lantern.

She looked at it the way she would have in hedge country: as a forger.

She had ghosted Quince’s mark under her own thirty-odd times, and she knew in her fingers what a ghost needed. Registration. Line for line, a ghost had to lie in perfect register with what it ran beneath, or it slid off and took nothing. That was why her off-register skew had spared Teo every sliver since Frost. And Hallam’s guides had to keep register at both ends: under the drawing on the sheet, and through the Conduit with the Zero Sheets, the most fixed lines in the world, twelve hundred years unmoved. A ghost carried weight from one *here* to another *here*.

She looked at the charter. The north bound. The south bound. The hinge, the curve, the rate.

There was no *here* on it. Every line was a limit or a motion: the bounds were not where the ground lay but where it could not go, and the hold lived in the swing between them. A trace laid beneath could match the ink to a hair and still be in register with nothing, because the record’s truth was in the range, and no Zero line in the world described a range. You couldn’t trace a movement in blind ink.

“A ghost needs the lines to sit still,” she said aloud.

Kasimir was sitting beside her with his hand wrapped in his scarf. He turned his head.

“Say that again.”

She said it again, and watched him take it apart and check every joint of it the way he checked a traverse, and watched it hold.

“Then if the Equinox Charter were drawn open,” he said. “If the Wing were drawn open—”

“Then there’s nothing for him to put under it.”

He looked at her. In the lamplight his face was grey with exhaustion and pain, and there was a look in it she had never seen there before, and it took her a moment to name it. He was amazed.

“We will have to draw the Wing,” he said. “On the way in. The causeway, all of it, open.”

“Yes.”

“Before dawn.”

“Yes.”

Kasimir looked at the east, where there was no sign yet of the sun.

“Then we had better be going,” he said.

* * *

They came under the Lantern Gate at twenty minutes past five on the morning of Thaw 22, seven of them, with Absalom Grice on a litter made from two plane-table boards and a chain.

They had drawn the whole return open: the ridge and the fold and the glasswrack slide, the Margin heath, and last of all the Drift Wing itself, chained in along the causeway in the dark before First Bell, Teo counting, Cass reading the floor, Ilse feeling for the give of every corridor, Kasimir drawing the bounds. The Wren Stair turned, as it always had, and now the sheet said so, and how far, and how fast. Seven marks stood at the foot of the route, Absalom’s shaky and Piety’s small and very neat. The Wing sheet they left unsigned, as every party did: by the articles the Wing was the College’s to attest, under the Gate, at the Equinox. And at the Gate end of the causeway Ilse had asked Kasimir to draw the Wing twice. He had looked at her, and drawn it twice, and not asked why.

Theirs was the only party to finish the course. The Ammonite girls had come in at sunset. Five parties had come back in the night without their routes, and four were still out, with proctors gone after them.

The judges stood under the arch: Calder, Halloway, the Provost in a dressing gown under his gown, and Hallam.

Calder read the charter by the keystone lamp, sheet by sheet, and gave it to Halloway, and Halloway made a small strangled noise.

“Oh,” he said. “Oh, it’s—they’ve—Magistra, it’s the rate annotation. It’s the *whole* thing. The b-bounds and the rate, and it closes, it closes on every leg, I don’t—the error’s *recorded*, it’s all—” He looked up at Ilse with his three watches swinging and his eyes wet. “Marrow, it’s *beautiful*.”

“It is irregular,” said Provost Steyne smoothly. “Seven is not four. And this is not, so far as I understand it, a charter. A charter fixes a place.”

“A charter,” said Calder, “records what is.”

The arch was very quiet. Above them, carved in the stone, the words ran from one side to the other. WE RECORD WHAT IS.

Calder let the pause sit.

“I have examined this record,” she said. “I find it true. I have felt it held, and it holds. I find it holds more lightly and more firmly than any route charter I have seen in eleven years of this course. The fourth article governs parties, Provost. It does not govern truth. By the Law of Fidelity, which is older than any article, this charter stands.” She rolled the sheets. “It will be the basis of the Equinox Charter. Master Hallam will prepare the folio from it. Thank you, Miss Marrow. Mr. Reyne. Have someone set that young man’s leg.”

She held the roll out. Hallam took it.

He unrolled it under the lamp with his gloved hands and looked at it a long time. Ilse watched him read the bounds of the fold, and the hinge, and the rate. She watched him turn to the last sheet, the Drift Wing drawn open from the Gate to the far end of the Third Range, and read the Wren Stair, turning, and how far, and how fast. His spectacles caught the light. His gloved forefinger moved along the line of a bound, the way a man feels along a wall in the dark for a door he knows is there.

There was no door. She saw him find that out.

He looked up, then, over his spectacles, and found her in the lantern light, and for a moment he was not kind or anything else, only an old man who had been the best doubter Brask had ever known, looking at a thing he had not thought of.

Then he smiled, gently, and rolled the charter up again with great care.

“Remarkable,” said Aurek Hallam. “Truly remarkable, my dear. Get some sleep. We have a long morning ahead of us.”
