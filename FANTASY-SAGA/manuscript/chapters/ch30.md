# Chapter 30
## Under the Lantern Gate

The second sheet of the Drift Wing had been inked on the causeway in the dark, and at half past five, in the Hall of Instruments, Kasimir still would not let her roll it until the ink had stopped shining.

“It’s dry,” she said.

“It is dry on the surface. If you roll it now the Second Range will print itself onto the back of the Observatory and we shall have charted a building inside out.” He held the lamp low and sighted along the sheet with his cheek nearly on the table. “There. Now it is dry.”

It was the ugliest thing either of them had ever made. A charter of the old kind was all single lines, ruled and certain. This one had none. Every wall of the Drift Wing was drawn twice, its near limit and its far, with the space between hatched in Kasimir’s fine diagonal, so that the whole Wing looked like a building seen through rain. Every length carried two figures. *Long Gallery, 1 ch. 56 l. to 1 ch. 65 l.* *Wren Stair, turning on its foot through 4 degrees either hand.* *Third Range, swinging on the causeway through 2 ch. 6 l., westward on the ebb, home on the flood.* They had chained the Wing going out and again coming home, and between the two lay the truth: not where the Wing was, but how far it went. The limits were his, written to a hair. The sense of where to set them was hers. The first sheet was in Hallam’s gloved hands. This one was theirs.

She could have signed it then, in the empty Hall, and the Wing would have swung open on its causeway before anyone was out of bed. Kasimir had said so on the walk in, and she had said no, and he had thought about it and agreed. An open Wing at half past five would save nobody. The Morning Survey sheets in the presses would still carry their knots, and the Conduit would still sit in Hallam’s study, and Hen would still lie in the end bed looking at nothing. The Conduit only left that study when its master stood under the arch. And three hundred people who had been told about Drift-fatigue for three years needed to *see*.

“You’ve made the Observatory look drunk,” she said.

“The Observatory has been drunk since 1140. We have simply never been honest about it.” There was a scratch down his jaw from the fold. “I have to go. Coyle will lose his nerve if he waits alone, and Cass will lose her temper if she waits with him.” He pulled his coat on one-handed, the right hand still wrapped in his scarf, the brass buttons dull with Drift-mud. “I will be where we said.”

“Kasimir.”

He stopped with his hand on the door. She had meant to say a word about the lock, or the stairs, or Hallam. What came out was, “Your cuffs are filthy.”

“I am aware. It is the first time in my life.” The corner of his mouth went up. “I find I do not mind as much as I was raised to.”

Then it was just her and Teo, asleep on the bench with his hands folded on his chest like a knight on a tomb. The grey on his fingertips had not gone back a hair. She rolled the charter round its rod, slid it into its case, and woke him.

* * *

The College gathered under the Lantern Gate at a quarter to six, with the fog still coming in off the sea.

There were three hundred of them, and they filled the Old Court from wall to wall, gowns grey over their coats, breath smoking. The brass studs of the meridian ran down the middle, and nobody stood on them. Above the fog the arch went up into a sky the colour of a pigeon’s breast, and on the keystone the lantern burned in its iron cage, small and gold, the one light in the College nobody was ever allowed to put out. Under it, cut so long ago that the letters had gone soft: WE RECORD WHAT IS.

Beyond the arch the Drift Wing was a long darkness with its windows lit, the Observatory dome faint as a reflection in a puddle. It had held all night on yesterday’s Survey and the last of last year’s Equinox Charter. Ilse could feel it at the back of her head, a low note going thin.

A black oak lectern stood under the arch. On it lay the Equinox folio, its corners weighted with brass, the Wing drawn in single, certain, beautiful lines. She had copied for that hand two evenings a week since Gleaning.

“Breathe,” said Teo.

“I am breathing.”

“You’re breathing like a bellows with a hole in it, which I know because I am also breathing like that.” He had insisted on carrying the charter case. “There are forty-four proctors. I don’t know why I needed to know that. It’s helping.”

Sorry stirred against her ribs. It had slept like a dropped stone since the fold, and by every rule Cass knew it should have slept till nightfall. Now it lifted its head. Its ears came up, then flattened, and its whole small body turned under her hand like a compass needle until it pointed past the Masters’ bench toward the low door of the Register.

Cass had been leaning by that door five minutes ago, braids tucked into her collar, looking bored. Now there was only wall. In the clerks’ row on the steps, where Merrin Coyle should have been sitting with his inkhorn, there was a gap.

Hallam came out at ten to six.

He walked slowly, a little stooped, his white beard combed and his spectacles catching the lantern. The grey kid gloves. The plain black gown of the Register, without any of the velvet Steyne wore. People quietened for Aurek Hallam the way they quietened in a chapel.

He took his place at the lectern. Provost Steyne stood at his right hand, silver and splendid. Magistra Calder sat at the end of the Masters’ bench with a face like a shut book.

“My dear friends,” Hallam said. He did not need to be loud. The arch took his voice and laid it gently over the court. “Last night a party of seven came home to us out of the dark. We sent twelve parties to the Tern Stack, and the Drift was crueller this spring than I have known it in forty years, and only one came home with the course run. And it was not one party. It was two, who had been rivals, and who chose to be one.” He found Ilse in the crowd and smiled at her with such plain warmth that her stomach turned over. “The Ninefold wrote, *the Reach is held by many hands or by none*. I have never seen it truer.”

“They have brought us something new. An open charter, which attests not a place but a range. Magistra Calder has ruled, rightly, that it is true, and that by custom it stands as the basis of this morning’s charter. It is remarkable, and that is exactly why I must ask you to be patient with it.” He laid one gloved hand on his folio. “We do not know what an open charter does in a year, or in a storm. We know what *this* does. I propose that we set the open charter aside, with honour, pending study, and sign the folio we know. For safety, my dears. The Wing must hold. There are students who sleep in the Third Range every night of the year, and they will not thank us for an experiment.”

It was the most reasonable thing she had ever heard. She watched the heads begin to nod. Steyne nodded. Of course Steyne nodded.

“I will have my objection recorded, Master Hallam,” said Calder, without rising.

“Recorded, Magistra, with my respect.”

And Ilse, who had spent nine years making sure nobody looked at her for longer than it took to sell a basket of eggs, stepped out of the front row onto the brass of the meridian, where nobody stood, and said, “No.”

* * *

She had decided, coming in over the heath at three in the morning with one pole of Absalom’s litter on her shoulder, that she would not make it pretty. Making things pretty was the one thing she was good at, and look where that had got everyone.

“Under every Morning Survey sheet filed in this College since 1201,” she said, “there’s a second drawing. Blind ink. Gall and alum and quicksilver, so it dries to nothing. Every time you sign a Morning Survey sheet in the Antechamber, you sign over a ghost-line, and a sliver of the burden of something very old runs down it into your hand.”

Her voice was smaller than his, and the arch did not lay it gently over anyone.

“That’s the Quiet. It isn’t Drift-fatigue. It starts in the signing hand because the signing hand is what signed. Hen Solberg signed more than anyone in our party, because she was quickest, and because she was kind.” Her voice caught, and she waited for it to steady. “And the voids. Brine End. Corrow. The Little Wick lane in the Fallowmarch, where I was standing when it came apart. Every one followed an inspection tour from this College.”

“Miss Marrow,” said Steyne. “This is neither the time—”

“There are seven Laws,” Ilse said. “Master Hallam teaches six. The seventh is Transfer. It says you can trace a mark under another mark, line for line, and the burden will run to the one underneath. They don’t teach it here. Every hedge-forger in the Fallowmarch knows it.” She made herself look at the Masters, then at the crowd, then, last, at Hallam. “I know it because I am one. I’ve moved hedges for money since I was fourteen, and for three years under a dead man’s mark, so he’d carry the cost instead of me. That’s how I know what I’m looking at. That’s the only reason anyone in this court can see it at all.”

The silence was not empty. It was full of three hundred people adjusting what they knew of her, and she could feel them doing it, like a field of hedges all straining at once.

Hallam took off his spectacles, polished them on a fold of his gown, and put them back. When he looked at her over them his face held nothing but sorrow. It had always been a kind face. It still was.

“My dear friends,” he said gently, “you have heard our accused forger. She is a gifted young woman, and on the eve of her tribunal she would have you doubt the Register. I do not ask you to think ill of her. I ask only that you think which of us has been honest with you for forty years.”

It went through the crowd like wind through barley, and she watched the barley bend.

Then, from her left, a voice she knew said, “She pulled us out of the fold.”

It was Piety Mercer, who had screamed at them in the fold, with a bandage round her wrist and her pious little mouth set hard. Orla Devane, beside her, folded her arms. “She did. Rather rudely. But she did.”

It was not enough. But Steyne’s hand, which had been lifting toward the proctors, went back to his side.

Hallam saw it. He only sighed, as though the morning had grown more tiring than he had hoped, and dipped his pen. “The Wing will not wait for us, my dears, and nor will the sun.” He signed the Equinox folio, a neat looped sigil like a coiled rope, and the level in Ilse’s ears changed pitch.

She felt the folio take. It was beautiful work. The Wing, thin at the end of its year, slid into those single lines and lay still. And under the lines, where no one could see, the ghost woke: a long cold pull that had nothing to do with the Wing, going away under the Old Court, down through seven levels of stone, toward the oldest thing in the College, which wanted holding.

It was waiting for the next pen.

“Proctor Arnaud,” said Hallam. “If you would lead the proctors.”

Lisbet Arnaud came forward, tall and correct, and took the pen from his gloved hand.

There was no time to think, so Ilse measured. Precedence asked two questions, in order. Which record was truer? And only if they were equal, which was older? She did not need to be first. She needed to be truer, and she needed to be before Lisbet Arnaud’s pen, because she did not know how much a ghost could pour into a hand in the time it took to sign. The Wing was the College’s to attest. She would do it alone.

“Teo.”

He had the case open before she finished the word. His grey fingers dropped the cap and did not stop for it. The charter came out unrolling into the fog, and he caught the far end, and they held it open between them on the steps of the Lantern Gate like a banner nobody had asked for.

She uncorked the common black with rope-burned fingers. She did not lay her palm on the sheet and agree with it, the way Quince had taught her. She did not have to. There was no lie in it to agree with. It said the Long Gallery lay between a chain and fifty-six links and a chain and sixty-five, and so it did. It said only what was so.

At the foot of the sheet, where nobody had yet signed, she set her own mark. Three lines and a spur.

* * *

The Drift Wing breathed.

Nobody, afterward, found a better word. It began at the First Range: the square stone face of it leaned out a hand’s breadth into the dawn and settled back, slow as a sleeper’s chest. Then the Second Range, a long sigh of lit windows sliding a foot along the dark and returning. Out at the end of the causeway the Observatory dome swung like a lamp on a chain, caught the first of the sun on its glass, and threw it back across the heath in a long gold blade.

It did not come apart. It swayed, all of it, within its limits, and the sound that came across the Margin was old timber easing, the creak of a house settling for the night. The note in Ilse’s ears, which had been thin and straining for a year, opened into a sound she had never felt from any building. It rose and fell. It was the level of a thing that was allowed to move.

Someone said, “Oh,” on a high note, and then a great many people said it.

Hallam had put his gloved palm flat on his own folio, the way you feel a charter to see if it holds. She watched his face change. The Wing had slipped out of his lines as easily as a hand out of a glove and gone to the record that told the truth about it. His folio was only a drawing now, and a drawing bound nothing. A ghost rode an attestation; the pull beneath his lines, which had been waiting for three hundred pens, had no hold left to run along, and let go.

The fog had been settling on the folio all morning. Lisbet Arnaud bent to it, pen in hand, and frowned. “Master. There’s something under the lines.”

On the damp linen, faint violet lines had come up through the sheet beneath the drawing, the way the second line had come up under Ilse’s mark when Sorry spilled Coyle’s tea. Pale as a vein under skin. Perfectly registered. The word went back through the crowd faster than anything Hallam had said.

“Sign it, Lisbet,” said Hallam quietly. “It will do you no harm now. It holds nothing any longer. That is rather the trouble.”

Across the court, the Register door banged open.

Kasimir came through it at a dead run, his hair all over his face, a sheet of linen-rag clutched flat to his chest like a page saved from a fire. Cass was a pace behind him with her sling in one fist, and behind her came Merrin Coyle, stumbling, his inkhorn bouncing on its cord, looking as though he had been sick and might be again. At the foot of the steps Kasimir stopped so short that Cass ran into his back. He could not speak. He held the sheet out to Ilse.

Sorry went rigid in her coat and pressed itself as far from the sheet as it could, its fur standing in a ridge.

She took the Conduit.

To the eye it was nearly blank: a double folio of good rag, a few registration crosses, and at the bottom, in black ink, the coiled rope of his mark. But the fog found it at once. The damp came up through the linen and the violet came with it, hundreds of lines fine as hairs, each beginning at a little knot of strokes that was a student’s mark, each running inward, branching and joining and thickening, a river delta drawn backward, all of it flowing to the root of the sheet and into the coiled rope. In that tangle was a wren’s foot. In it, too, was Hen. And it hummed, not like the honest Ammonite charter he had once let her hold, but lower and sicker, a note that went down into the ground under her and kept going.

Hallam came down from the lectern, one step and then the next, a gloved hand on the stone of the arch. He stopped two steps above her, and when he spoke it was not to the crowd.

“You’ll kill her.”

“Master Hallam—” said Steyne.

“You will *kill* her.” His voice cracked straight across, like old ice. “The centre is failing, Ilse. Not the Margin. The Zero Sheets. The First Atlas. I went down to Level Seven in twelve hundred with a lens and a rule, as I had every year since I took the Register, and they were softening. A hair a season. Then two hairs. I measured it for a year, because I could not believe my own hand, and then I laid the figures on the Provost’s desk.” He looked past her, over her head, at Steyne. “And I was told to keep it quiet.”

Steyne looked away. Three hundred people watched him do it. He looked down at the brass studs of the meridian as if he had never noticed them before.

“Do you know what it is,” Hallam said, “to *measure* the end of the world, to the hair, and be told to keep it to yourself?” Behind the lenses his eyes were wet, and tired, and absolutely certain. “I tied my own mark into the Zero Sheets with my own hand, and look.” He held up his gloved hands, and they did not shake, because they could not. “So I asked for help. A sliver from each of you, so small most of you never felt it. And I pruned the edges, Ninefold forgive me, because a tree that will not be pruned will fall. A few hundred of us, my dear, against a million of her children. The children of Hobbet are among them. Your mother is among them.”

She saw it then, and the seeing was worse than anything she had imagined in Brask’s shed with Kasimir. There was no cruelty in him. There never had been. There was only a man who had loved a thing so long that it had turned him to stone from the inside, and stone could not change its mind. *He came back certain*, Brask had said. The grey was not only in his hands.

“You could have told us what we were signing,” she said. “Some of us would have signed it anyway.”

“Some.” He smiled at her, and it was terrible, because it was fond. “Never enough, my dear. That is the whole history of the Reach in two words.”

She knew what tearing it would do. A ghost lived by registration, and a torn sheet lay in register with nothing. A ghost-trace was a channel, and if you broke a channel the water did not vanish. It went back the way it had come, every sliver from every hand, down the delta to the root. The weight had been his before it was theirs. He had tied his own mark into the Zero Sheets, and the Conduit had only lent it out. Quince had carried her weight for three years and never felt it, because Quince was in the Hobbet churchyard. The root of this sheet was standing two steps above her, alive, in grey kid gloves.

Her hands would not move.

Hallam looked at them and understood. Some last stiff thing went out of his face, and what was left looked very old.

“Do it, then,” he said quietly. “Someone has to carry her.”

She tore the Conduit in half.

There was no flash. There was only the sound of good linen parting, small and dry, like a sheet torn for bandages. Then she tore the halves across, because she was thorough, and let the pieces fall on the step.

Everything went still. It was not the stillness of held ground. It was a held breath let go, everywhere at once. The cold pull under the Old Court stopped. She felt a thread in her own right hand go, a thread she had never noticed until it was cut, and her fingers opened on their own. Beside her Teo made a small sound. He was staring at his hand on the edge of the open charter, where the grey was going out of his fingertips like a tide, pink coming back into the skin from the knuckle down.

Up in the infirmary, whose tall windows looked onto the Old Court, somebody laughed.

It was not a polite laugh. It was a big, startled, unlovely bark, the laugh of someone who has just remembered the end of a joke told them a whole winter ago, and it rang across the silent court from an open window. Ilse would have known it at the bottom of the sea. Teo’s face crumpled, and he did not let go of the charter to wipe it.

“That’s Hen,” he said. “That’s Hen.”

On the step above them, Hallam’s gloves split.

They went along the seams first, at the backs of the fingers, the fine kid opening like the skin of a ripe plum, then across the palms. What was underneath was the same colour as the gloves. It had always been. The grey went up past his wrists while she watched, under the black sleeves, and came out again at his collar, a slow slate tide climbing his throat into the white of his beard. He did not cry out. He looked only mildly surprised, like a man handed a bill a little larger than he had expected, who has decided on reflection that it is fair.

He sat down on the steps of the Lantern Gate, carefully, the way an old man sits on a bench in the sun. He turned his face east, toward the sun coming up over the Reach, and Tessary far beyond it.

Then he was still. His eyes stayed open. The light came up into them, and they did not blink.

Nobody moved. Calder was on her feet with a hand pressed flat to her mouth. The fog was lifting off the sea. Behind the arch the Drift Wing went on breathing, in and out, as though it had waited a very long time to be allowed to.

Above them, on the keystone, the lantern burned in its iron cage, small and gold, over words that nobody had ever thought to doubt.

Ilse looked down at her hands. They were shaking so hard she could not have held a pen, and she could not make them stop.
