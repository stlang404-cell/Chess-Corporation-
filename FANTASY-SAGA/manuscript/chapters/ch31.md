# Chapter 31
## The Board of Errata

Hen ate four boiled eggs, a heel of bread, a dish of stewed prunes she had always claimed to hate, and most of Teo’s breakfast, and then she cried for a quarter of an hour without stopping, and then she laughed at herself for crying, and then she asked whether there was any more bread.

“You’ll be sick,” said Dr. Orle, from the next bed.

“I don’t care. I’ve been not caring about bread for four months.” Hen tore the crust off the new slice and looked at it as though it were a letter from home. Her hair was lank and her freckles stood out on her skin like flecks of rust on a white plate, and her right hand, when she reached for the butter, was pink to the fingertips. “Tide’s in,” she said, and laughed again, the big unlovely bark that had come out of the window on the morning of the twenty-second, and two beds down a first-year who had been Quiet since Deepwinter laughed too, without knowing why.

The infirmary had been like this for a day and a half. Nine beds, nine students sitting up, all of them hungry and most of them weeping at odd moments, and Orle going from one to the next with her sleeves rolled and her mouth very straight. Ilse had been sitting on the end of Hen’s bed since First Bell.

“I remember all of it,” Hen said, more quietly, when Orle had moved on. “That’s the strange part. I remember you sitting there in Frost, and Teo bringing me those awful little cakes, and Cass shouting. I remember knowing you were all being kind. I just couldn’t find where the kind was supposed to go.” She turned the crust over. “It was like the tide going out and never turning. You stand on the flats and you know there should be water.”

“I knew about the lines,” Ilse said. “For two weeks before you went Quiet. I knew, and I didn’t tell anyone, because telling would have meant telling what I was.”

Hen looked at her for a while. The look was flat for a moment, from habit, and then it was not flat at all.

“Teo said you’d say that,” she said. “He said you’d been saving it up. He said I was to tell you that you’re an idiot and then give you a prune.” She held one out. “It’s a horrible prune. I’ve picked it specially.”

Ilse ate the prune.

Teo came in at nine with his hands held out in front of him like a man carrying a full tray. He had been doing this to everyone for a day and a half. He held them out to Hen, who kissed the fingertips one at a time in front of the whole ward, which made him go so red that the first-year two beds down laughed again.

At the far end of the ward, by the window, a young man in a grey infirmary shirt was sitting up very straight with his hands on the blanket. Ilse had first seen him in the corridor outside, in Gleaning, on a bench, staring at nothing, with slate-coloured fingers. Wil Ashdown. He had not said a word that anyone on the Wren Stair had ever heard. Now he was watching the orderly set a tray down on the table by the door, and he cleared his throat, and everyone in the ward went still to listen.

“Is that marmalade?” said Wil Ashdown.

The orderly said that it was.

“I used to like marmalade,” said Wil, slowly, as if he were reading it off a sheet. He looked down at his own hands, which were pink, and then out of the window at the Old Court, and then back at the tray. “I think I still do. Could I have some?”

Orle, who had written to Tessary eleven times and been told by the Provost to stop, turned her back to the ward and stood looking at a shelf of brown bottles for a long moment. When she turned round again she was brisk.

* * *

Cass told her about the lock that afternoon, on the roof of the Second Range, with her boots hooked over the gutter and the Wing swaying gently under them like a hammock.

“Annotated. Wouldn’t take a crowbar. Wouldn’t take Coyle crying on it, and he tried.” She spat over the edge, thoughtfully. “Your Reyne goes white and says, the key, man, where does he keep the key, and Coyle says *I don’t know*, and then Coyle says, *oh*. Picks up that great ivory paperweight off the desk. Hollow. Unscrews at the base like a pepper-pot. Key inside, wrapped in a bit of chamois.” She shook her head. “Knew all along, the eel. Just didn’t know he knew.”

“Is he all right?”

“Coyle? Wept on the Antechamber floor for an hour, then burned every unprinted folio in the press-room.” Below them, out on the causeway, the Observatory dome rocked a little westward with the ebb. “They carried the old man down to the Crypt last night. Orle’s orderlies. Eight of them. Said he was heavy as a church door.” She picked at a route-cord. “I didn’t want to be glad, Marrow. I keep thinking of Nan’s camp sliding down the hill in the dark. And I’m not glad.”

“No,” said Ilse.

At noon on the day of the equinox, after the Wing had been breathing for six hours and nobody had died of it, Magistra Calder had laid the open charter on the black oak lectern under the arch and the whole College had signed it, one by one, three hundred marks under the merged party’s seven. Ilse had watched from the steps. Not one of them had felt anything go down into the ground. She had felt the College take its own weight again, every hand light, the way it ought to have been.

On the twenty-fourth, Provost Steyne addressed the Mess.

He stood at the high table in his velvet and spoke for nine minutes, and he was very good. He spoke of a grievous discovery, and of grave misconduct by a single Master, a man who had served the College for forty years and whose mind, the infirmary believed, had been disordered by the burden he had chosen to carry in secret. He spoke of the Quiet, now cured. He thanked the students of the Long Measure, without naming any. He spoke of calm, and of the confidence the Assembly might continue to place in the Register.

He did not say the words *Zero Sheets*. He did not say *Level Seven*, or *softening*, or *the center is failing*. Three hundred people had heard Hallam say those words under the Lantern Gate two days before, and the Provost of the Meridian walked all the way round them like a man walking round a hole in a floor.

Ilse noticed. She saw Kasimir, at the Patent end of the Mess, notice too. He took out his notebook of errors observed and wrote something down.

When Steyne sat, there was a silence. Then, from the Walkers’ table, somebody said, not loudly, “Broth.” And somebody else said it, and then a dozen people, low and flat and steady, like the Sounders’ Choir at the bottom of a hymn. *Broth. Broth. Broth.* Steyne did not look up from his plate. The proctors did not stop it. Lisbet Arnaud, at the head of the proctors’ table, examined her spoon.

* * *

The Board of Errata sat on the twenty-eighth of Thaw in the Hall of Instruments, because no other room in the College had a table long enough.

They had pushed the great theodolites back against the walls under their dust-sheets, so that the hall was lined with shrouded shapes like a congregation of patient ghosts, and down the middle they had set the long table with five chairs behind it. Magistra Calder sat in the center, in Court black. On her right sat Halloway, with his three watches laid out in front of him in a row, and Master Moy, broad and quiet as a quarry wall. On her left, Dr. Orle, and old Damask, who had brought a lens on a brass arm and kept turning it in his fingers.

In a chair by the tall east window, a little apart, sat the Chancellor of the Concordance Court.

Lady Imogen Reyne was smaller than Ilse had expected, and older, and more beautiful. She had her son’s grey-blue eyes and none of his hesitations. She wore a dove-grey travelling coat and gloves of black kid, and she sat with her hands folded on the head of an ebony cane and watched the room the way Ilse watched a sheet she suspected of being forged: without hurry, without warmth, waiting for the place where it would give. When Ilse came in, she looked at her once, from boots to hair, and then looked away as if the inventory were complete.

On the table in front of the Board stood a box. It was a Court evidence box of black-japanned tin, the kind that went by mail coach under seal, and its lid was open.

Ilse knew what was in it before she reached the chair set for her. She would have known it from across a field.

“The charge,” said Calder, and paused, as was her habit, long enough for a person to count three, “is forgery. Contrary charting without licence, upon parish charters of the Held Reach, within the Fallowmarch, over a period of years. The particulars have been laid by the Concordance Court upon the information of Mr. Kasimir Reyne, a Member of this College. The Board has the sheets.” She laid a hand on the lid of the tin box. “Twenty-nine of them. Drawn by Court writ over the winter from the chests of eleven parishes. Each bears, as its visible mark, a wren’s foot. Each bears beneath it, in blind ink, the mark of Abelard Quince, surveyor, of Hobbet, deceased.”

She did not look at Kasimir, who sat at the side of the hall with his hands on his knees. Ilse did. He had done this all winter. While she sat by Hen’s bed, while they stood on the roof of the Old Court under the cold stars, he had been writing to parish clerks across the Fallowmarch and waiting for the coach. He met her eyes and did not look away and did not apologize. She found, to her surprise, that she did not want him to.

“The Board calls Field-Surveyor Brask.”

Brask came in from the Old Court with the Long Lie in her right hand and her grey left hand hanging at her side, and the patch black over her eye. She had been reinstated at eight o’clock that morning, for the purpose of this testimony, by a minute of the Board.

She gave her evidence standing. She told them about the Walk survey, and the stream bank that did not close by fourteen links, and the way Ilse had drawn it closed. She told them about the conversation in her cottage in Fogmonth and the promise she had extracted, and that she had told no one.

“You knew the candidate to be a forger,” said Calder, “and you did not report it to this Board.”

“I knew her to be a surveyor who had forged. There’s a difference. I’ve spent nineteen years in a leaking shed trying to teach it.” Brask leaned on the Long Lie. “She’s the best pair of hands I’ve had through that shed since it was built, and the worst liar, which is something in a forger. She lied to me twice this year and both times I knew before she’d finished her sentence. Put that in the minutes.”

“It is in the minutes, Field-Surveyor.”

“Good.” Brask looked at Lady Reyne, briefly, with her one eye, and then back at the Board. “Is that all?”

It was all. She went to stand at the back of the hall by the shrouded theodolites, and Ilse felt her there the whole rest of the morning, the way you feel a benchmark at your back.

“The Board calls Mr. Reyne.”

Kasimir stood where he was, at the side of the hall, and when he spoke he spoke in complete sentences, without contractions, the way he did when he was on his dignity, and his voice did not shake at all.

“Miss Marrow forged,” he said. “I have no doubt of it, and I have never had any. I saw the root-tear under the Tull hedge in Reaping and I knew what it was. Every sheet in that box is a forgery, and I gathered them, and I would gather them again.” He paused. “I must also tell the Board something it may not wish to hear. The law under which Miss Marrow would be expelled this morning is the same law under which Master Hallam would still be Master of the Register. His crime was invisible to every honest instrument in this College. It was invisible to me. I have the best hand of my year, and I signed over his ghost-lines every morning for six months and saw nothing. It was visible to exactly one person, and she could see it only because she had done, on a small scale and for poor people, the thing he was doing to us all.” He turned his head, then, and looked across the hall at the chair by the east window. “I do not ask the Board to say that forgery is right. I ask the Board to notice what our honesty failed to see, and who saw it for us.”

Lady Reyne did not move. Her gloved hands stayed folded on the head of her cane. But Ilse, who had not stopped watching her, saw the knuckles go white through the black kid, and then go slowly back.

* * *

“Miss Marrow,” said Calder. “The Board will hear you.”

She had written this out four times on the backs of old Mensuration problems and burned all four, because they were too good. She stood up without anything in her hands.

“It’s all true,” she said. “All twenty-nine. There are thirty-one, actually. Two the Court didn’t find. A ditch at Fennock, in the chest at Saint Ivo’s, and a field gate on the Marlow road. I’ll write out where they are.” Halloway, without being asked, made a note. “I started at fourteen with Abelard Quince, and when he died I went on alone. I used his mark under mine so that the burden would go to him, because he was dead and couldn’t feel it, and I thought that was clever. I moved things back where I believed they belonged. Sometimes I was right. Mostly I was right. That isn’t a defence. It’s just what happened.”

She took a breath.

“I’d like to release them,” she said. “All of them. Now. With my own hand, if the Board permits it. They’re my sheets. They should be unsigned by the hand that signed them.”

There was a stir along the table. Damask stopped turning his lens. Calder looked at her for a long moment, the full count of three and then some.

“You understand what release means for the land.”

“Yes, Magistra.”

“The Tull charter is among them.”

“I know.”

Calder looked down the table. Halloway nodded, jerkily. Moy nodded. Orle nodded. Damask said, “Oh — yes, yes, of course,” and then, more quietly, “oh, my dear.” Calder took the first sheet out of the tin box and laid it on the table in front of the empty chair, and turned the chair to face it.

Ilse sat. She took her pen out of her coat, the same pen, and the common black, and drew the first sheet toward her.

It was a ditch at Lower Hendle, from the spring of 1201. She remembered the client, a cowman with a stammer and three daughters, and the ditch, which a neighbour’s new survey had walked eleven yards into the cowman’s grazing. She looked at her wren’s foot in the corner, three lines and a spur, a little stiffer than she made it now. She put the nib to it and drew one straight line through it, from the spur to the topmost toe, and meant it.

The level in her ears gave a small faint drop, like hearing a door close in another house. Five hundred and sixty miles east, a ditch would be sliding eleven yards back into a neighbour’s field, and a cowman with three daughters would wake to find it there. Under her line, in blind ink no one in the room could see, Quince’s mark let go of its thread at the same moment. She felt that too. It was very slight. It was like setting something down that you have carried so long your arms have forgotten it is there.

*Sorry, old man*, she thought. *You can put it down now.*

She drew the second sheet toward her.

It took most of an hour. Nobody spoke. The Board watched, and Brask watched from the back of the hall, and Kasimir watched from the side, and Lady Reyne watched from the window with her face like a winter field. A hedge at Marlow, twenty-two yards. A gatepost at Bell Cross. A well-head at Ashby that Ilse had moved four feet one wet night in 1202 so that a widower could reach it without crossing his brother-in-law’s yard. Some of the drops were so faint she barely felt them. Some went through her like a missed stair. One, a strip of water-meadow at Coldharbour, she had been especially proud of; she had walked it eleven times. She drew the line through the wren’s foot and felt the meadow go, and her hand did not shake, and she was glad of that, and she did not know why.

The Tull charter was last. She had known it would be, because Calder had laid them out by date.

It was the best work she had ever done. Forty-one stations, two hundred and twelve measured lengths, every angle checked twice by sightvane and once more by spite. She knew the hedge on it the way she knew the lines of her own palm. She thought of the slow green slide of it through the dark, the sparrow’s nest riding along on its fork. She thought of Agnes Tull coming down the path in her nightcap with the candle held up, saying *there’s my old Pearmain back*.

She would write to Agnes tonight. She did not yet know what she would say. *I’m sorry* was true but not enough, and Agnes deserved better from her than a thing that was true but not enough.

She drew the line.

Far away in the Fallowmarch, forty yards of hawthorn would be walking back east tonight, two rods, thirty-three feet, taking four apple trees with it onto Lord Saffery’s land. It would not want to. But Fask’s charter was still there, and nothing now said otherwise. The drop in her ears was the deepest of the morning. She sat with her pen in her hand and let it go through her.

Then she capped the ink and put the pen back in her coat.

* * *

The Board withdrew for twenty minutes. When it came back, Calder did not sit.

“The Board finds,” she said, “that Ilse Marrow is guilty of forgery.” The pause. “The offences were committed before her membership of this College, and upon charters of the Held Reach. They are, properly, matters for the Concordance Court. Except that she is now a Member, and the privilege of the College is not the Board’s to waive, nor the Court’s to take.” She did not look at the east window. She did not need to. “Under the precedent of *Errata v. Pell*, 1061, a Member found guilty of an offence of charting is to be sentenced to restitution and to the use that the College most needs. The candidate has made restitution this morning, in the Board’s sight, and beyond what this Board would have dared ask of her.” Another pause, longer. “As to use.”

“This College has not admitted a student to the discipline of Contrary Charting since 1097. The discipline remains in our statutes. It is restricted. It is dangerous. It is the only discipline we possess whose purpose is to understand, from the inside, how a record may be made to lie. The Board is of the view, after the events of the twenty-second, that the College has been without it for a hundred years longer than was wise.” Calder laid both hands flat on the table. “Ilse Marrow will remain a Member of this College, on probation, as a student of Contrary Charting. Her instruction will be in the charge of Field-Surveyor Odile Brask, whose suspension is lifted from this hour. Any further contrary record made by the candidate without the knowledge of her Master will end her membership and deliver her to the Court. The Board is risen.”

There was a sound from the back of the hall that might have been Brask clearing her throat.

Lady Imogen Reyne rose from her chair by the window. She took up her ebony cane and settled her coat, and walked the length of the Hall of Instruments between the shrouded theodolites, unhurried. She passed within an arm’s length of her son. He stood very straight. She did not turn her head, and she did not slow, and she did not speak, and then she was out of the door into the Old Court and the sound of her cane on the flags went away across it, tap, tap, and was gone.

Kasimir stood where she had left him for a while. Then he sat down, and took out his notebook, and did not write anything in it.

Ilse went out by the other door.

The Old Court was empty at that hour. The sun had come out, thin and white, a spring sun with no heat in it yet, and it lay along the honey stone and caught the brass studs of the meridian one after another down the length of the court, so that they made a line of small fires. Beyond the Lantern Gate the Drift Wing swayed in the light. The lantern on the keystone was almost invisible in the sun, but it was burning. It was always burning.

She stood on the step where Hallam had sat down. She was a Member of the College of the Fixed Meridian, legitimately, on honest terms, with a discipline and a Master and her name in the minutes of the Board, and she had given up everything she had ever made to get there. She had nothing to be proud of. She had looked for pride, standing there, out of habit, the way you feel in a pocket for a coin that you know isn’t there.

It wasn’t there. Something else was. It was lighter than pride, and it did not need anybody to see it, and she found that it would hold her weight.
