# Chapter 32
## Until the Term Is Spent

The Crypt of Benchmarks was colder than the sea.

Ilse went down on the third of Seedtime, in the hour after supper when the chapel was empty, with a lamp and one silver mark in her pocket. The stair under the chapel turned twice and opened into a long low vault of the same honey stone as the Old Court, gone the colour of weak tea down here where no sun had touched it in a thousand years. Lamps burned in niches at intervals along the walls, tended by Orle’s orderlies. Between the lamps, in two long rows facing each other across the flagged aisle, stood the benchmarks.

There were forty-one of them. She had counted once, before the Ladder, when the party had come down with pennies for luck. A woman in a surveyor’s coat of a cut two hundred years out of date, one hand raised a little as if to stop someone at a door. A boy, barely older than a link, who had stilled in 1088 holding a sea-wall against a spring tide, his grey face turned up and his mouth a little open. They were alive, in a sense nobody fully understood. They did not breathe that she could see. They did not blink. Orle’s people dusted them once a week with soft brushes, and spoke to them while they worked, because it seemed rude not to.

In every grey hand there were coins. Centuries of them. Pennies and half-marks and foreign silver and buttons from students who had nothing else, stacked in the curled palms and wedged between the fingers, so that when the lamplight moved the whole Crypt glittered faintly from waist height, like a field of small moons.

Hallam was at the far end, by himself.

They had not been able to stand him up. He had stilled sitting, and seated he remained, on a plain oak bench that the orderlies had carried down and set at the end of the eastern row. Someone had straightened his gown and combed his beard. The half-moon spectacles were still on his nose. His hands rested on his knees, palms half-turned upward, and they were bare, and they were grey: not the soft grey of kid, but slate, the cool dense grey of a roof after rain, from the fingertips up into the sleeves. Orle’s people had set the bench so that he faced east. He was looking at the Crypt wall. Beyond it, three hundred miles off through the rock, was the Zero Stone.

There were already two pennies in his left hand. She had not expected that. She stood looking at them for a while.

“Well,” she said at last. Her voice went a short way into the vault and stopped. “I came to tell you some things. You can’t stop me, so.”

She sat down on the end of the bench beside him.

“Hen’s back in the party. Orle let her out on the first. She laughed so hard at Teo falling over the chain in the Long Gallery that she had to sit on the stair. Wil Ashdown asked for marmalade. He’s eaten nothing else for a week. Orle says she’ll let him, because she wants to see what happens.” She looked at her hands. “The Wing’s holding. Under the open charter. It sways a hand’s breadth at the First Range and two chains at the far end, and it goes out on the ebb and comes home on the flood, and nobody has been fined for a lost stair in eleven days. Halloway has written a paper. It has forty-one footnotes.”

“You let me hold the Ammonite charter,” she said. “In Gleaning. In the Antechamber. You put it in my hands and said four hundred years of stone, my dear, feel it, and I felt it, and you were watching my face while I did. You said I had a draftswoman’s hand.” She stopped. “I’ve been trying to work out whether you meant it. Whether all of that was just how you picked which of us would sign the most sheets. And I think you meant it. I think that’s the worst part. I think you were kind to me, and you’d have let me go Quiet, and both were true, and you didn’t see any difference.”

He looked at the wall.

“You were right about the centre,” she said. “Brask thinks so. Halloway went down to Level Seven with a lens. You were right, and you told the wrong man, and then you did the wrong thing about it, and I tore it up in front of everyone, and I’d do it again.” Her throat had closed a little. She waited. “But I’m sorry it was you at the bottom. I am sorry about that.”

She took the silver mark out of her pocket. It was one of the six left over from the Long Field money, after the coach to the Cape. It was the last thing she owned that had been meant for the field. She held it a moment, and then she leaned over and put it into his grey right hand, which was empty, and closed his fingers over it as far as they would go, which was not far. They were as cold as the sea.

Then she took her lamp and went back up into the chapel, where someone had left a window open, and the evening air was full of the smell of gorse.

* * *

Brask walked her out into the Margin at dawn on the fifth, as she had on the third of Gleaning, half a year ago, before any of it.

The gorse was in full flower now, from the College wall to the edge of the heath, a yellow so loud it seemed to make a noise, and the whole Margin smelled of it, sweet and nutty, like warm biscuits left on a sill. Larks went up out of it and hung singing. The ground breathed under their boots, and Ilse found she no longer braced against it. Brask walked ahead with the Long Lie, setting it down at every pace, reading the grain through the brass shoe. She had been Field-Surveyor again for a week, and in charge of Contrary Charting for the same week, a charge the College had not given anyone since 1097 and which, as far as Ilse could tell, came with no salary, no room and no instructions.

“First lesson,” Brask had said on the first morning, in the leaking shed. “Everything you know is a lie. Your job, for the next four years, is to work out which bits, and how they were made, and who made them. Then you write it down. Then you sign it.” She had sniffed. “I’ll be checking your spelling.”

Now she stopped at the top of a low rise where a cairn-crow had built a cairn of seven white pebbles on a flat stone. The College lay behind them on its headland, the Held Side honey-gold in the early sun and the Drift Wing trailing out over the heath, moving slightly on its tide.

“Halloway went down to Seven again last night,” Brask said. “Calder went with him. They took the lens and those three watches of his.” She knocked the Long Lie on the stone. “It’s faster. Two hairs a season when Hallam measured it. Now it’s nearer two a month.”

Ilse had known it would be. She had known it since the twenty-second, in the place behind her ears where she knew things before she would admit them. The old man had been holding the Zero Sheets up with three hundred borrowed hands and his own, and she had taken the hands away.

“Because of me.”

“Because of him, Walker. Because of whatever’s doing it in the first place. You took a crutch off a man with a broken leg. Doesn’t mean you broke the leg.” Brask squinted east into the sun, toward the Reach. “Steyne’s written to the Assembly. I’ve seen the letter; Calder made him show it. Two pages on grave misconduct and the Quiet cured, and the gratitude of the College, and not one word about Level Seven. He thinks if nobody says it, it’ll stop.”

“And nobody knows why.”

“Nobody knows why the oldest bindings in the world are coming loose after twelve hundred years of holding like iron. No.” She said it quite calmly. “Not Halloway. Not Calder. Not Damask. Not me.” She was quiet a moment. Out on the heath a wayhorn lifted its head from the gorse, its antlers branching like the veins of a leaf, every tine pointing toward the Cape. “Next year,” Brask said, “we find out.”

She reached into her coat and took out a book.

It was small and thick and swollen with old damp, bound in oilcloth that had gone brown and brittle at the corners, and tied round with a bootlace. On the cover, in faded ink, someone had written *O. Brask — Field-Book — Sallow 1183*. Ilse knew the hand. It was Brask’s, from before the Sallow, when it had been quick and slanting and young.

“The Board had a copy,” Brask said. “This isn’t the copy.” She held it out. “There are things in there I didn’t give them, because I knew what they’d call them. Drawings. Soundings. Some of what I heard. The last pages I wrote in the dark, after the eye, so forgive the penmanship.”

Ilse took it. It was heavier than it looked.

“Read it,” said Brask. “All of it. Then come and tell me how I might be wrong.”

* * *

On the evening of the eighth, Archivist Damask was waiting for her in the Second Range corridor, beside a door that had not been there that morning.

It was oak, black with age, its brass plate worn almost smooth: FAR RANGE — CLOSED BY ORDER 1105. Damask stood beside it, hopping very slightly from foot to foot, his lenses pushed up on his forehead on their brass arms, smelling powerfully of vinegar.

“It stays, now,” he said. “Oh, my dear, it *stays*. Well, not stays. It goes about between the third window and the fifth, a little forward on the flood. But it goes about where it is *told* to.” He clasped his hands. “Ninety-nine years, and then two children with an open charter and two days’ chaining.”

She and Kasimir had done it on the first and second of the month, chaining the corridor of the Second Range forty times in two days while the door came and went, until they had its range: here at its nearest, there at its farthest, moving thus. Kasimir had drawn it. She had felt for the limits. When they had both signed, at midnight, he had stood looking at the door and said, *I believe that is the first thing I have ever charted that I could not have charted alone*, and then looked appalled at having said it aloud.

Damask opened the door.

The Unheld Stacks went down and away into the dusk like the inside of a shell. The shelves had drifted. Nobody had walked the way in for ninety-nine years, and in that time the library had gone where low-hold ground goes, slowly, with no one to tell it otherwise. The great oak presses had wandered off their lines and stood about at angles like cattle in a field. One whole bay of shelving had turned on itself into a loose spiral, so that its books lay along a curve as gentle as a staircase. The floor rose and fell. Dust lay on everything, thick and soft and grey, and the last of the evening came in through a high window and made a long slanting bar of gold across the room, and the bar moved as the Stacks swung on the tide.

Sorry put its head out of her coat and looked at it all with its amber eyes.

“Oh,” said Damask, “let it down, let it down. Nothing in here will mind.”

She set it on the floor. She did not describe it. She had been doing that more lately, setting it down and saying nothing, and watching it go soft at the edges into the shape it had worn before she trod on it at the Ossel Fair, a small grey indeterminate thing that was mostly ears and eyes. It sat in the dust and blinked at her, and seemed content.

Damask had been in twice already that week, alone, and he knew what he wanted. He led her through the leaning presses to a reading-desk by the high window, where a folio lay open under a dust-sheet, and lifted the sheet away as gently as if a sleeper were under it.

“Near enough Ninefold,” he whispered. “A working copy of a sheet of the First Atlas. The Ammonite margin. A fair copy, no hold in it, from perhaps the second century, by a clerk who copied the founders’ note stroke for stroke and could not read a word of it either. And look. *Look.*”

In the margin beside the chained line of a hill, in faded brown ink, there was a note. It was not in any script she knew. It was not, she thought, quite writing at all. It was a run of small linked strokes, looping and doubling back, some heavy and some so faint they barely marked the page, and the longer she looked the less certain she was where one stroke ended and the next began.

“The founders’ hand,” said Damask. “Nobody has read a word of it in a thousand years. I have spent fifty of them trying.” He took a small notebook out of his coat, very worn. “I have eleven signs. Eleven! Out of who knows how many. Guessed from where they fall. There is one that sits always beside a traverse where it closes, where the chain comes back to its start. I read it *spent*, or *paid out*, as you would say of a chain run to its end. There is one that sits beside a span of years. *A term.* There is one that sits where a boundary runs to a stream and stops. *As far as.* *Until.*” He tapped the note in the margin. “And I think they are all here. But I cannot tell which strokes are which. The hand runs them together. It is like trying to read a knot.”

Behind them something rattled. Ilse turned.

Sorry had climbed the leg of the desk. In climbing it had knocked over Damask’s sand-pot, the little perforated pot of fine pounce he used for drying ink, and a pale fan of sand had spilled across the desk beside the folio. Sorry was sitting in the middle of it, looking at the margin note.

And then it was not a small grey indeterminate thing any longer.

Nobody had spoken. Nobody had described it. It changed anyway, the way it had changed once in the Mess kitchen at the clatter of a dropped pan, into a shape no one had named: it drew itself together and down and long, and it became something like a hand. Not a human hand. Smaller, and grey, with too many joints in its fingers and no wrist to speak of, and two pale amber eyes set where a knuckle might be. It laid one long finger in the spilled sand.

It traced the first stroke of the note.

It did not copy it. Ilse, who had copied ten thousand lines in her life, could see that it did not copy it. It *drew* it, the way the hand that made it must once have drawn it: the heavy stroke first, pressing deep; then lifting; then the faint stroke after, drawn lightly and quickly, in a different direction. And then it stopped, and lifted its finger out of the sand, and left a gap. And began again.

Stroke by stroke, in order, with its gaps, the note came apart in the sand. What had been one tangled run was now a row of separate small figures, each one standing clear of its neighbour, like words with spaces between them.

Damask made a sound like a man stepping on a stair that isn’t there.

His hands were shaking so badly that Ilse took his notebook and held it flat on the desk where he could see it. He went along the row of figures with one finger, not touching, his lips moving. He stopped at one, and looked at his notebook, and went on. Stopped again.

“There,” he whispered. “There is *as far as*. *Until.* Do you see? The hook, and the drop. And there.” His finger moved two figures along. “The sign by the span of years. *The term.* And there, there at the end, that is the traverse closing. The chain paid out to its end. *Spent.*” He was breathing very fast. “The rest I cannot read. I cannot read *any* of the rest. But those three are in that order. *Until the term is spent.*” He looked up at her with his old wet eyes enormous behind the lenses. “What term? What term, my dear? Spent on what?”

Ilse did not know. She looked down at the small grey hand in the sand, which had gone quite still and was looking back up at her with its amber eyes, as though it were waiting to see whether she had understood. Then it sighed, and went soft at its edges, and was a small grey nothing again, and curled up in the sand and went to sleep.

* * *

She told Cass that night, on the roof, with the stars out.

Cass listened all the way through without interrupting, which was unlike her. When Ilse had finished she sat with her arms round her knees for a while and looked west, out over the Drift, where the horizon would not sit still.

“Nan says it at weddings,” she said at last. “When the bride’s mother is crying. And at funerals. And when the stew’s burnt.” She pulled one of her braids round and turned its route-cord over in her fingers. “*Rent’s coming due.* I’ve heard it ten thousand times, Marrow. Never once thought it meant anything.”

She did not say anything else. After a while she went down. Ilse stayed. She had Brask’s field-book in her coat, and she read it by the lamp at the stair-head, the Sallow in Brask’s young slanting hand, until the stars went pale, and she did not want to stop.

* * *

The letter came on the ninth, in her mother’s hand, though half of it was not her mother’s words.

*The hedge went back the night of the 28th,* Hesta wrote. *Agnes sat up in her nightcap with a candle to watch it go, the daft old thing, and came round in the morning to tell me. She says to tell you she had her Pearmain one autumn more than she ought, and she would have given you the eggs for that alone, and you are not to fret. I am to say that word for word. I don’t know what you have done, Ilse, but the clerk says the Big College has written to him very civil and that you are not to be hanged, so I suppose it is something. Write, you wretch.*

Ilse read it twice, and then folded it into Brask’s field-book, inside the cover, where it would be safe.

* * *

The First Bell rang on the tenth of Seedtime an hour before dawn, and the four of them went out onto the Wren Stair with the chain.

It was cold and clear. Hen held the zero end and counted, out loud, the way she always had, and somewhere in the twenties she got the giggles over nothing at all and had to start again, and nobody minded. Teo paid the chain out down the stair with the careful hands of a man laying out pastry. Cass went ahead with her palm flat against the wall, reading it. “Leaning west,” she said. “Little bit. Tide’s on the turn.” She was right. She was always right.

They did not fight the stair any more. They found it: where it stood this morning, within the limits drawn on the open charter, and which way it was going, and how fast. Hen did the arithmetic in her head and Teo checked it in his and they agreed to the link. The Long Gallery was three chains and forty-two links, and creeping. Ilse wrote it down.

Kasimir’s party came through the far end of the Gallery as they were finishing, on their way back from the Third Range. Piety Mercer, astonishingly, nodded. Absalom swung along on crutches with his splinted leg out in front of him like a bowsprit. Kasimir came last, coiling his own chain, and as he passed Ilse she fumbled the end of theirs, and he caught it before it hit the boards and put it back into her hand.

His fingers stayed over hers a little longer than a chain needs. Not much longer. Long enough to be measured.

“Miss Marrow,” he said.

“Mr. Reyne.”

He went on down the Gallery after his party, and Cass, behind her, made a small noise of enormous satisfaction, and Ilse did not turn round.

In the Register Antechamber Merrin Coyle sat at his desk with ink on his cuffs, looking thinner and less frightened than she had ever seen him. The stack of folios at his elbow was new. When she took one and held it to the lamp she could see the watermark, a wheat-sheaf, the old mill.

Out of habit, before she signed, she bent and breathed on the sheet. The damp bloomed on the linen and faded. There was nothing under it. There was only the page.

She signed her mark. Three lines and a spur. Her own, and nobody else’s, and nobody under it. It was very light.

When she came out of the Antechamber the sun was coming up. She went and stood under the Lantern Gate with her hands in the pockets of her father’s coat, where Sorry was asleep against one palm and Brask’s field-book lay against the other, and looked out at the Drift Wing in the first of the light.

It was breathing. The First Range leaned a hand’s breadth into the morning and came back. The windows of the Second Range slid and returned. Far out on the causeway the Observatory rocked on the tide like a boat that knew its mooring and was in no hurry to leave it. It was held, and it was moving, and it was true, and for the first time in her life she did not think those were three different things.

East of her, beyond the honey stone, beyond five hundred and sixty miles of hedges held by other people’s ink, the oldest maps in the world were coming loose a hair at a time, and nobody knew why, and next year they would go and find out. The Long Field was still Saffery’s. Agnes Tull’s Pearmain was on the wrong side of a hedge. Hen was laughing in the Old Court behind her at a joke of Teo’s, and it was the best sound she knew, and none of it was finished, the world or her.

The Wing moved at a quarter past six in the morning, and it wanted to, and so did she.
