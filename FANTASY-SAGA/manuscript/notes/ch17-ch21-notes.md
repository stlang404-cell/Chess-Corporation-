# Notes: Chapters 17–21 (Part Three opening; Frost 3–14, 1203 H.)

## Chapter 17 — “Confession in the Mess Kitchen” (Frost 3) — 3,882 words

**Summary.** In the small hours after Ch 16, Ilse takes Teo and Cass down to the Mess kitchen, because the Wren Stair's walls carry voices and Hen's stripped bed is there. She unpicks her father's coat lining and lays out her forger's kit, including Quince's mark on oiled silk, and tells them everything: Quince, blind ink, the Law of Transfer, thirty-four charters in nine years, the Tull hedge, Kasimir, the Walk as a refuge. She mentions the Long Field only in one line and defers the full story. Teo is hurt ("Seventy-one days late"), then steadfast: "You lied about yourself. You're not lying about this." Cass laughs: "Paper-wall with a hedge-thief's hands." Ilse explains the ghost-lines and takes the blame for her two weeks of silence about Hen. Cass suspects Kasimir. They can't go to Steyne without exposing Ilse, and they can't stop signing without drawing notice, so Ilse teaches them to sign off the rule (a sixteenth of an inch high, turned a little sunwise). Teo can't skew his mark until Ilse tells him to start it from its end. Ilse knows she is breaking her promise to Brask after twenty-two days. Sorry eats common ink, recoils from Ilse's blind ink and turns its back on a blank Morning Survey sheet. A dropped copper pan startles it into an undescribed heron-like bird for three breaths. Cass: "Ossels don't do that." At First Bell they survey and sign crooked. Ilse notices that the faint draw through the nib is gone. That evening, under the damp lens, she sees the ghost-line running past their marks. The stair holds, shakily. Teo: "we just lied to the building."

**End state.** All three sign off-register daily from Frost 3. Hen is in the infirmary. Ilse knows the trick works. Teo and Cass know everything except the full Long Field story. Brask doesn't know about the ghost-lines or the broken promise. Ilse has a spoiled blank Morning Survey form from the Antechamber waste basket, and her kit is back in the coat lining.

**Invented details.**
- Quince's mark: a looped, old-fashioned mark "like a hook caught through a ring." Quince died in **Seedtime** 1200. He took Ilse on after she pointed out that his privy stood four links over his neighbour's line.
- Ilse's tally: **thirty-four charters in nine years** (hedges, ditches, a mill-race at Fennock). This matches Ch 26's "hedges, ditches, a mill-race."
- The Errata Society meets in the Mess kitchen on Thursdays. Teo's spirit-stove takes eleven pumps.
- Quince's term for the trick: "signing off the rule." His image: a ghost-line is "a lodger who had copied your key."
- Mechanism (matches Ch 26): the blind guide-line runs in from the left edge of the sheet under each signing place and off toward the binding. A true mark sits on it stroke for stroke. A skewed mark crosses it and touches only at a point or two.
- Hen's survey count-call: "tally one, and tally two, and that's the tide in."
- The Long Gallery has eleven windows.

**Deviations.** None of substance. The outline's Scene 4 ("next morning") is set the same morning, with the check that evening; the effect is the same.

## Chapter 18 — “Drift-Fatigue” (Frost 5) — 3,865 words

**Summary.** Ilse and Teo visit Hen in the infirmary's long, draughty north ward. Five of nine beds are occupied, and Wil Ashdown is in his chair by the window. Hen's hair has been plaited by someone else. She speaks in flat declaratives ("It's cake." "You should let me sign. I'm quickest.") and ignores Teo's seed-cake. The grey is past the first joint of every right-hand finger. Dr. Orle takes Ilse into the dispensary. Every case's grey begins on the signing hand, including one left-handed student, so "fatigue doesn't choose a hand." Orle says "Drift-fatigue" appears in no text before the Provost named it. Steyne visited on Frost 3 and ordered her to stop writing to the Surgeons' Hall in Tessary; she has stopped sending the letters but not writing them. Ilse tells her only to "watch who signs the most," and Orle becomes an ally: Ilse may visit at any hour and should bring Orle the answer "before anyone in a good coat." At four, Lisbet Arnaud fetches Cass from the Fieldwork shed. Steyne questions Cass about Brine End, because she visited Corrow that week. Ilse waits in the Provost's passage. Hallam passes, kind, and says he has told Steyne the Tenting have "no more to do with these voids than the gulls do." Cass comes out white: "They think we're unmaking the ground. *We.*" Steyne asked her to inform on her kin. On the Second Range roof that night, Ilse tells Cass that Brask sang to the hollowing. Cass identifies it as a Tenting "turning-song," sings it three ways, and explains that songs change because the ground does. The chapter ends on her line: "You paper-walls think if it isn't written, it isn't there."

**End state.** Orle is an ally (no ledger dates are revealed; those stay for Ch 23). Cass is under official suspicion. Ilse has told Cass about Brask's song. Ilse is due in the Register on Thursday to copy Salt Steps tithe maps.

**Invented details.**
- **Benet Lowe**, a left-handed Third Range student whose grey began on the left hand.
- The Surgeons' Hall in Tessary, where Orle wrote twice.
- The Provost's lodging: west end of the Old Court, a meridian-arrow knocker, thick carpet, a long-case clock and a portrait of a former Provost holding dividers.
- A skylight and hatch above the Wren Stair top landing gives onto the Second Range roof. It came into reach when the landing drifted in Gleaning.
- The "turning-song" for hollowings. Cass guesses that "somebody Tenting" taught it to Brask.
- Nan's remark, via Cass's letters: "six was an odd number for laws." This is a light nod to Transfer, kept as folk scepticism.
- The Fieldwork-shed drip rings every eleven seconds (Teo timed it).

**Deviations.** I added the brief Hallam encounter in the Provost's passage. It keeps him warm and adds dramatic irony; no clue is planted beyond the ledger's.

## Chapter 19 — “Corrow” (Frost 7–8) — 3,898 words

**Summary.** On Frost 7 a heath crow brings Cass a knotted route-cord from Aunt Bet: "Corrow. Ground going. Wagons on chocks. Two days. Come." Brask authorizes a field exercise for Ilse, Cass and Teo, with a report due by noon on Frost 9 and a crow from Mother Nettle. She orders Ilse to sign nothing and asks to be remembered to "Ottery Tenter." At Corrow eleven painted wagons sit on chocks, each trailing a dotted line of abandoned chocks uphill. The bowl's floor slides toward the stream at about the pace of a minute hand. Fires wander and a dog sleeps while being carried along. Nan Ottery Tenter feeds them stew and black bread and argues with Teo about Lisk bread, then sings the Corrow road-song. Ilse realizes the song is a survey, with distances, bearings and conditional clauses: a map that moves. Nan: "Paper says where a thing is. Song says where a thing *goes*." Nan tells them that a gentle old College man with a white beard came two nights earlier, asked the way to the Corrow stone, took off his gloves to touch it, showed grey hands and paid Tobin a silver mark. The next night the ground let go. Ilse goes cold and refuses the thought ("She knew how not to take a measurement"). Nan's tin holds the College grant of 1190, which is in order. Ilse sends a crow to Brask, not to the Register, and the reply reads: "Not in the Register. Not misfiled. Missing. Keep them moving. B." Nan says "Rent's coming due," and when Cass asks what it means, answers "What it says." That night the camp moves to the low ford by singing the route. Ilse walks a road that moves, unafraid, "listening."

**End state.** It is dawn on Frost 8 at the low ford, and the camp is on firmer ground. Ilse knows that Corrow's master charter is missing and that an old man with grey hands visited the stone. She has consciously refused to connect this to Hallam. Her field-book traverse of the night route is nonsense. No one attested anything.

**Invented details.**
- **Aunt Bet** (Bettony Tenter) and **Tobin**, a 10-year-old cousin.
- Eleven wagons. The Corrow stone is a leaning standing stone on the rise, the "high stone" of the Ch 4 song.
- The **Heath Treaty**: the College's 1190 grant confirms the winter ground to the Tenter wagon ("the bowl, the stream and the rise unto the standing stone"). Nan's thumbprint is on it, and she keeps it in a Lisk tea tin, an echo of Ilse's savings tin.
- Brask knows Nan by name ("Everyone on the heath knows your nan"). This is left ambiguous.
- Further lines of the Corrow song: forty paces from the leaning gorse; the sheepfold wall after three days' rain; the stream on the left hand; "When the stream's quiet, it has moved; go up and find it."
- Heath-kale. The Tenting send messages as knotted route-cords tied to a crow.

**Deviations.** The outline has Nan say the old man came "three nights back." I changed this to "two nights back" so that, with "the next night the ground let go," the void falls on Frost 6–7. That matches the void date "Corrow, Frost 7" in the already-drafted Ch 26. The missing charter is confirmed through Brask by crow, following the Ch 13 pattern.

## Chapter 20 — “The Moot” (Frost 10) — 3,894 words

**Summary.** The Moot-stone is a three-hundredweight basalt block in a one-rod sand bed of Brine End sand, with four brass corner studs as the only legal stations. Calder states four rules: survey from the studs only; one glass (a quarter hour) to measure and one to draft; chart the stone anywhere within its latitude (about a yard, on its face) and declare the south-west-corner position with a pin; attest together, and "the stone will go where the better map says." Before each bout the previous winner releases their charter. She cites *Saffery v. the Parish of Wick*, 1193, Ilse's father's case, as the model of fidelity over age. Ilse and Kasimir are drawn as the last of twenty-nine pairs. All day, Ilse watches the stone rather than the duelists. Most winners use "the standing claim" (charting the stone where it lies). After each release, the stone settles a fingernail's width south-west. Teo beats Absalom with a standing claim. Cass claims the ground's lean against Piety; the stone strains toward her pin, but her sloppy charter loses. Ilse concludes that a standing claim is a perfect record of where paper put the stone, not of the ground. In their bout, Kasimir makes a flawless standing claim. Ilse reads the sand with her levelglass bead, her knuckles, ripple lines and tell-tale pins. She finds the rest point three inches south-west, turned two degrees sunwise, and drafts the stone there, adding the creep rate and bearing. On simultaneous attestation the stone grinds three inches to her red pin, and Kasimir's charter goes dead. Calder: "Fidelity to the ground, Mr. Reyne. Not to the page." Hallam applauds softly and watches her too long. Brask, under the arch, says only "Hm." Afterward, Kasimir admits he has checked her lines and can't find one she moved: "That was not a survey. That was a forgery that happened to be true."

**End state.** Ilse's charter now holds the Moot-stone for a year, a small ongoing burden. She signed the Moot charter true, on the rule, not crooked. Kasimir is shaken and afraid. The seed of the open map is planted: the creep rate is written on the charter (alongside the Ladder's rate annotation and Corrow), but Ilse draws no general conclusion yet.

**Invented details.**
- The Moot-stone has been in the Old Court 311 years. The bed is sunk in the south-east corner of the Old Court, and its sand is re-carted each autumn from the strand below Brine End.
- Pins carry coloured ribbons (Kasimir blue, Ilse red). The Moot urn and the chalk board of pairings.
- "The standing claim" is the common safe tactic. Twenty-nine pairs fight (58 first-years; Hen is absent). The bout order includes Grice v. Anselm (Teo wins), Mercer v. Tenter (Piety wins), and Reyne v. Marrow last at dusk.
- Kasimir's instruments: a black boxwood folding rule, brass beam-compasses, and a sightvane with hair-wires.
- Moy is on the dais.

**Deviations.** None. The outline's "her mark" is rendered as Ilse's declared pin position.

## Chapter 21 — “The Accusation” (Frost 12–14) — 3,896 words

**Summary.** On Frost 12 Lisbet Arnaud hands Ilse a Board of Errata summons after Morning Survey. In the Chapter Room above the Library of Record, before Calder, Steyne, Halloway and a clerk, Calder reads Kasimir's complaint. The Tull charter (Reaping 14, 1203) was obtained via the Concordance Court and examined by a Court assessor. Its visible wren's foot matches her Old Wall Benchmark, and under a damp lens Quince's mark (dead since Seedtime 1200) shows in blind ink. Ilse reserves her answer: "Silence is recorded too." The tribunal is set for the Board's next quarterly sitting, in Thaw after the equinox. She stays a Member, confined to the grounds, and keeps her Register copying at Hallam's request. Kasimir says Brask declined to answer him in Fogmonth. Halloway stammers comfort ("the *unrecorded* error"), and a note from Hallam reads: "Courage, my dear. The Board is fairer than it looks, and so, I think, are you. — A. H." On Frost 13 Brask tells the Board she knew from the day of the Walk (the stream bank) and kept the secret by her bargain. Steyne suspends her from teaching and bars her from the grounds from sundown. Packing the Fieldwork shed, Brask refuses to let Ilse say her "heavy" thing: "I'm not carrying anything out of that gate I can't put down again." She approves the creep rate on the Moot charter ("Did it hold? Then don't ask me stupid questions") and says, "You'll do the Long Measure without me. Good. You'd have leaned." On Frost 14, in the snowing Old Court, Ilse confronts Kasimir and tells him about the Long Field: Fask, sixty yards, the well, her father at the window, the appeal (*Saffery v. the Parish of Wick*), his death in 1195, and the 44 marks. "You think I don't know what a forged boundary costs? Fask's was legal." Kasimir is shaken and apologizes, but holds his ground: a forger with a better pen is no answer, and you don't get to choose who carries. "Then the law must be fixed. Not ignored." She answers: "Most people with a grievance don't have a pen, Mr. Reyne." He replies "The complaint stands" and walks away, and his footprints fill with snow.

**End state.** Ilse is formally accused, with her tribunal in Thaw (Ch 31). Brask is suspended and barred from the grounds, at home in Verity Cross. She still knows nothing of the ghost-lines, consistent with Ch 26. Kasimir now knows the Long Field story and the Wick Fallow case. Ilse still holds the Register copyist post. Teo and Cass appear only offstage.

**Invented details.**
- The Board meets in the **Chapter Room** above the Library of Record, up a stair too narrow for two people to pass. The summons carries a black wafer seal. Three of the five Board chairs were filled (Calder, Steyne, Halloway).
- The Tull charter's apple trees: Pearmain, Russet, Codlin, Old Tom. The sheet is foxed by vestry damp.
- The charter reached the College on Frost 11.
- Brask's line: "This College took the Mastership off me nineteen years ago." Her oldest chain has a brass fifty-link tally worn smooth.
- Brask's Fieldwork classes pass to a fourth-year proctor.

**Deviations.** None. Ilse quotes Calder's citation of the case from Ch 20, which ties the Moot to the Long Field reveal.

## Continuity flags for other writers and the line edit
- **Saffery v. the Parish of Wick, 1193** is used as the Long Field appeal (from the VOICE-GUIDE example). If an earlier chapter has Calder cite it in class, Ilse "knew the case" still works.
- Off-register signing runs from Frost 3 onward, and Ch 26 already assumes it. Ilse signed the Moot charter true.
- Sorry's undescribed form in Ch 17 is a heron-like bird, slate-grey with two head-plumes and amber eyes.
- Hallam appears warm in Ch 18 (passage), Ch 20 (applause) and Ch 21 (note, copying request). The only planted clue is Nan's "grey hands" in Ch 19, and Ilse refuses it.
