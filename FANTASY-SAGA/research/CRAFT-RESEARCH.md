# Craft Research Notes — Project Wonderforge

**Method and honesty note.** These notes come from craft literature and publishing references whose ideas are widely documented and that I can cite by author, title and year. I did not fetch these texts during this session; the summaries are paraphrased from general knowledge of the works. No quotations are given unless they are very widely quoted. Where a point is my own artistic judgment rather than a researched principle, it is marked **[Judgment]**.

---

## 1. Epic fantasy architecture

| Principle | Source | Application to this saga |
|---|---|---|
| *Secondary belief*: the reader must believe in the invented world from the inside, as opposed to "suspending disbelief." Consistency is what earns it. | J. R. R. Tolkien, "On Fairy-Stories" (lecture 1939; essay published 1947) | The rules of Hold are set down in a formal spec (`world-bible/MAGIC-SYSTEM.md`) and checked against every chapter. |
| *Eucatastrophe*: the sudden joyous turn that doesn't deny sorrow. | Tolkien, same essay | Book One's climax turns on a lie that saves people. The cost stays real (Ilse is exposed). The joy is earned rather than handed over. |
| A long series needs one *spine* question that stays unanswered until the end, plus questions that are answered book by book. | General series-craft consensus; cf. John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007), on the "story world" and nested desire lines | Series question: *What did the Ninefold promise, and what is owed?* Book One question: *Who is making the land come apart, and why are students falling ill?* |
| Fantasy clichés grow from unexamined defaults: dark lords, prophecy, orphan farmboys, interchangeable inns. | Diana Wynne Jones, *The Tough Guide to Fantasyland* (1996) | No prophecy, no secret bloodline. The protagonist's distinctive skill is a *crime* she taught herself. |

## 2. Magic-system engineering

| Principle | Source | Application |
|---|---|---|
| An author's ability to solve conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic. | Brandon Sanderson, "Sanderson's First Law" (essay, 2007, brandonsanderson.com) | Book One's climax uses only rules shown earlier: Witness, Latitude, Precedence and Transfer. Each is dramatized at least twice before the climax. |
| Limitations are more interesting than powers. | Sanderson, "Sanderson's Second Law" (essay, 2012) | Burden and Stilling make the cost of every attestation physical and moral. |
| Expand what you already have before you add something new. | Sanderson, "Sanderson's Third Law" (essay, 2013) | Later books *deepen* Hold (Wending, the Lease) rather than adding unrelated powers. |
| Hard and soft magic can coexist. A rule-bound human craft can sit inside a mysterious, unknowable source. | Same essays; and the contrast in Ursula K. Le Guin's Earthsea essays | Human cartology is "hard." The Drift and the Vey are "soft," known only partially, and that is where the series mystery lives. |

## 3. Magical-academy and mentorship narratives

| Principle | Source | Application |
|---|---|---|
| The mentor's function is to give the hero a gift and then be removed, so that the hero has to act alone. | Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's Journey* (1992; 3rd ed. 2007), after Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949) | Brask is deliberately *absent* (suspended) during the Book One climax, so Ilse has to apply the lessons herself. |
| An institution is a story engine when it has calendared, recurring rituals the reader can anticipate. | **[Judgment]**, informed by how school stories are structured across the genre | Morning Survey, Benchmarking, Errata Night, the Lantern Count and the Long Measure. |
| Competence porn: readers enjoy watching skilled people work, provided the process is shown. | Common editorial term; widely discussed in genre criticism | Every survey scene shows instruments, numbers, method and error. |

## 4. Long-form mystery and revelation

| Principle | Source | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Fair play: the reader should have access to the clues the detective uses. | Ronald Knox, "Ten Commandments of Detective Fiction" (1929); S. S. Van Dine, "Twenty Rules" (1928) | The clue map (`planning/MYSTERY-CLUE-MAP.md`) lists each clue's chapter of introduction before its payoff. |
| Hide clues in plain sight by placing them in emotionally loaded scenes, so attention is elsewhere. | Widely taught misdirection practice; cf. Agatha Christie's technique as analysed in John Curran, *Agatha Christie's Secret Notebooks* (2009) | The double signature trace is first seen during a comic Morning Survey scene. |
| MICE quotient (Milieu, Idea, Character, Event): threads close in the reverse order they opened. | Orson Scott Card, *How to Write Science Fiction & Fantasy* (1990) | Book One opens on Event (the unravelling road) and Character (Ilse's fraud), and closes them in reverse: Character resolution (the tribunal), then the Event explanation (the Lease hook). |

## 5. Character psychology and reader attachment

| Principle | Source | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Readers attach to characters through *desire* plus *vulnerability*, and through competence in action. | Lisa Cron, *Wired for Story* (2012); Truby (2007) on "need vs. desire" | Ilse *wants* legitimacy and land. She *needs* to stop believing that anything flexible must be false. |
| A *ghost* (past wound) drives the misbelief. | Truby (2007) | Her family's field was legally redrawn into a lord's estate. She concluded that maps are weapons and that truth belongs to whoever signs first. |
| Distinct voices: each speaker should be identifiable without tags. | Common editorial practice; cf. Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (1998) | Voice sheets live in `characters/VOICE-GUIDE.md`. |

## 6. Prose and the "fictional dream"

| Principle | Source | Application |
|---|---|---|
| The *vivid and continuous fictional dream*. Avoid needless distraction such as clumsy diction or mannered prose. | John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction* (1983) | Close third person on Ilse, past tense. No head-hopping. |
| Sentence rhythm and sound; concrete detail over abstraction. | Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (1998) | The style guide sets a varied cadence and bans a list of tic phrases. |
| Scene and sequel: goal, conflict and disaster, followed by reaction, dilemma and decision. | Dwight V. Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965) | The chapter plan alternates action scenes with breathing scenes. |

## 7. Folklore and mythology inputs (broad, non-derivative)

- **Mutable landscapes** appear in many folk traditions: fairy hills that move, islands that vanish, roads that lead elsewhere at night. The saga turns this into a *legal and scientific* system rather than whimsy.
- **Boundary rituals**: "beating the bounds," the English parish custom of walking the parish boundary each year and striking boundary markers, is a documented historical practice. It inspired the Morning Survey and the Lantern Count. Those are transformed rather than borrowed: in the saga, walking a boundary *literally* holds it in place.
- **Survey history**: real triangulation projects such as the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (1802–1871) and the Ordnance Survey of Great Britain (founded 1791) show surveying as both science and instrument of power. The theme that maps can dispossess is grounded in historical land-enclosure disputes. The *Inclosure Acts* in England are a documented example of legal maps redrawing who owns land.

## 9. Distinguishing research from judgment

The following are **artistic judgments**, not researched facts:
- That a forger-protagonist suits a cartographic magic system.
- That a self-mapping school ("the Drift Wing") is the strongest recurring wonder image.
- The exact target of roughly 32 chapters of 3,300–3,800 words.
- All taste calls on tone, humor density and romance pacing.
