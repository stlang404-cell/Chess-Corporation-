# Chapter 1: The Cold House

> The living keep clear.
> Visiting hours, second bell to sixth. Do not touch the casings.
>
> — *Board above the door of the Ambry Charitable Brinery, Coldwater Street*

Hester Vail's brother had been fourteen for eleven years. On the first morning of autumn she went, as she went on the first of every month, to pay for the privilege.

The Sere had come in overnight. It always did in Russet: the dry east wind off the White, carrying the plain's salt in it as a dog carries a smell. By the second bell it had got into everything. It gritted in the hinges of Lowside's shutters and furred the slate roofs. It turned the puddles in Tanner's Row into small white crusts that cracked under her boots like sugar. There was no use complaining. People did anyway. It was a thing to say to one another on the first of Russet, the way they said *mind the step* or *tide willing*, and Hester said it twice before she reached the bottom of the lane, to the baker's boy and to the old man who sold matches from a tray. "Sere's in." "Sere's in." It cost nothing, and it was nearly always true.

She went down Coldwater Street with her collar turned up and her purse inside her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel its weight with her elbow. Six sols in silver and four flecks in copper. She had weighed it on the kitchen scales the night before, which was a habit and not an anxiety. She knew the weight of a sol the way other people knew the weight of their own hand. A sol was a fifth of an ounce, less the wear. These were old sols and they had been worn by a great many hands. She knew to the grain what it took to keep her brother for a month.

The Ambry Charitable Brinery was the last building on Coldwater Street before the street gave up and became the old sea-wall. It had been a customs warehouse once, when there were ships, and it still had the look of a building meant to hold things for other people. It had a wide front, few windows and a deep door. Someone had painted the board above the door in white letters on blue three generations ago, and someone else had repainted it, carefully, around the original words, so that it read now in a slightly heavier hand:

THE LIVING KEEP CLEAR.

The city called it the Cold House, which was accurate.

* * *

Inside the door there was a desk, and at the desk there was a clerk, and in front of the clerk there was a ledger the size of a paving slab. Hester waited behind a man in a carter's smock who was paying for his wife in flecks, counting them out in stacks of five. It took some time. The clerk did not hurry him. That was a thing Hester liked about the Cold House: nobody hurried anybody at the desk. Everyone in that queue would be standing in it again next month, and the month after.

When it was her turn she put the six sols and four flecks on the felt.

"Vail," she said. "Two hundred and eleven."

"Morning, Miss Vail." The clerk was a young man called Ennis with a moustache he was not yet good at. He found the line without looking, which meant he was getting good at the ledger if not the moustache. "Six and four. That's you square to the first of Fallow."

"Ta."

"Sere's in," Ennis said.

"So it is."

She wrote her name in the column, where it had been written a hundred and thirty-two times before in her hand and four times, at the beginning, in someone else's. She had counted. She did not look at the first four lines any more.

The charity ward was through the double doors and down six steps. The steps were salt-glazed and worn into dishes in the middle, and the air changed on them. It went cold and still and faintly sweet, the way the air in a cellar of apples goes sweet, except that nothing in the Cold House was rotting. That was the point of it. Nothing in the Cold House was doing anything at all.

There were three hundred and four casings in the charity ward, in eight long rows, on stone plinths about the height of a bed. The windows were high and small. In autumn the light came through them at a long angle and lay across the rows in bars, and in each bar the casings caught it and held it. Casings were made of salt, the same salt that was getting into the shutters outside, but they did not look like it. They looked like glass, or like ice that had been taught some manners. They were clear right through, faintly rounded at every edge, and they fitted their sleepers the way water fits whatever you drop into it: close at every point and nowhere tight.

Inside them, the sleepers lay as they had been laid, on their backs mostly, some on their sides. One or two were sitting up, kept in a hurry. There was an old man with his mouth open on a word. There was a girl of nine or ten with her hair spread out and a ribbon in it, red, which had stayed red for sixteen years. There was a woman with a baby. Hester had stopped looking at the woman with the baby some years ago. She did not look now.

People said of keepings that they were like flies in amber. People who said that had not stood in the Cold House. A fly in amber is dead. The sleepers in the Cold House were alive, in law and in fact, and they looked it. That was what nobody warned you about. The colour was in their faces. Their skin had the faint wet sheen of skin, and their eyes, where they had been kept open, had the wet of eyes. If you stood close enough you could see the hairs on a forearm standing up in a gooseflesh that had been raised by some draught a decade ago and never been allowed to settle. They were not dead and they were not sleeping. They were between one moment and the next, and they had been there a long time, and the Cold House charged six sols a month to keep them there.

Two hundred and eleven was in the fourth row, nearly at the end, under the third window.

"Morning, Tib," she said.

Her brother lay on his back with his head turned a little toward the window, as though someone had said his name from that direction. His eyes were half open. His mouth was closed. He was fourteen and he looked it: long in the shin, short in the patience, with hands too big for his wrists and wrists too big for his sleeves. His hair needed cutting and always would. There was colour high in his cheeks that was not health but fever, the hectic pink of the bloom, which was the last thing his body had been doing when they kept it. His right hand lay on his chest with the fingers curled, the thumb pressed against the first finger, as though holding a pencil.

He had always been holding a pencil. He had drawn landships on everything: on the backs of their father's tally-sheets, on the plaster by his bed, once on the inside of Hester's best shoe, so that she found out about it at a funeral. Landships with three masts and with five, landships that did not exist and could not, with sails on top of their sails and runners as long as streets. He had wanted to be a pilot. Half the boys in Lowside wanted to be pilots, and the other half wanted to be delvers until they were old enough to see what happened to delvers. Tib had wanted it as some people want air. He knew the rig of every freighter in the dry harbour and he could tell you the wind on the White from the sound of the Lantern's mirrors turning, which nobody could actually do, but he said it with such certainty that you let him.

She stood at the side of his plinth with her hands in her coat pockets.

"Sere's in," she told him. "I know. You'd like it. You'd be down the harbour with your nose in the wind telling everyone it'll blow nine knots by noon. Daisy Fenn's had another one, did I tell you? That's five. Girl. She's called it Marguerite after herself, so there's two of them now and the whole office is going to have to start using surnames. Comptroller Mallory has a new wig. It is the colour of a wet fox." She considered. "That's all the news. The *Constant Wren* is due in today, from the east. Three masts. Ferrers and Lisk. You'd know her rig."

The casing gave back a little of the window. Her own face was in it, faintly, over his: a long, plain, rawboned face with a crooked front tooth and grey coming in at the temples where she had cut her hair short enough that she did not have to think about it. Thirty-three. She had been twenty-two when they kept him. She had been older than him then by eight years, and now she was older by nineteen, and every year the sum got a little more ridiculous.

She looked at his hand. Then she looked at the casing over his hand, because something was wrong with it.

* * *

It was wet.

Not much. A dozen beads of moisture, no larger than the head of a pin, standing on the curve of the casing above his knuckles, the way sweat stands on a cold jug. She leaned close. There were more lower down. They were on the side of the casing facing the far wall of the ward. She walked round the plinth to look, and on that side there were dozens, on the shoulder and the flank and the long slope down to his heel. They were beading in lines. That was what made her stop. Not a scatter of damp, the way frost sits on a window, but lines, short ones, a thumb's length or two, all of them running the same way. The lines ran down and toward the eastern end of the plinth, toward his feet. A few had joined into a single runnel that had crept to the plinth's edge and was hanging there, a drop as fat as a lentil, not falling.

Casings did not get wet. That was rather the point of them.

Hester stood very still and looked at the lines and did not touch anything. Do not touch the casings. It was written on the board over the door and on another board just inside the ward and on a third, in red, beside the plinths of children. She had never touched Tib's casing. Not in eleven years. She was not a sentimental woman and she did not touch things in other people's buildings that she had not paid for.

She had paid for this.

She took off her right glove and put the tip of her smallest finger to the fat drop at the plinth's edge, and lifted it, and put it on her tongue.

What she had, and what she had always had, and what had got her off the floor of the Customs House at twelve years old and onto the stool at the assay bench at nineteen, was a tongue. Not a palate, which was a thing rich men had for wine. A tongue, and behind it, somewhere at the back of her skull, a sense of weight that was not weight. Some people with the sense heard grain. They said stillsalt rang, that a pinch of good Middling hummed like a wet finger round a glass, and that the deeper the grain the lower the note. Some people felt it as cold. Hester tasted it. Grain tasted sweet to her, at the very back of the tongue, under the brine. The sweetness pressed downward behind her eyes, as if somebody had laid a coin on each lid. The deeper and stiller the grain, the heavier the coins. She had spent twenty-one years putting things on her tongue that other people would only touch with tongs, and she could tell you, from the weight behind her eyes, which fathom of the Hollow Sea a pinch of salt had come up from and how long it had been out of the ground. She was nearly always right.

A casing that had been kept eleven years should have tasted like nothing she owned a word for. She knew roughly what it would be. It would be deep Halcyon grain, the expensive kind, the kind that kept people. It would have been lying in this cold room soaking in the stillness of the life inside it for a decade, and it would be like putting a mountain behind her eyes. Old keepings were stiller than new ones. Everybody knew that. They settled.

This drop was not still.

It was sweet, yes. Grain, yes, and deep grain, the coins on her eyelids landed hard. But under the sweetness was something else, a taste she had met before only rarely and never in a keeping. It was metallic and bright and wet. It was like the taste of your own mouth when you have bitten your tongue: blood and salt and a sort of startle. It was the taste of a thing in motion. Assayers, the few who could taste at all, had a word for it. They said a sample was *quick*.

The drop on her tongue tasted quick. It tasted, she thought, of something going somewhere.

She stood with her finger in her mouth like a child, and the light from the window lay across her brother, and the beads on his casing stood in their little lines, all pointing the same way.

* * *

"You touched it," said Mother Agathe.

"I did."

"Well." The old woman looked at Hester's bare finger and at the casing and at Hester's face, in that order, without hurry. "Then we shall neither of us mention it. Was it bad?"

Agathe Linden had been senior keeper at the Cold House since before the Great Bloom. She was seventy, very small, very upright, and she wore the grey shawl of a lay sister of the Returning Tide pinned with a cheap brass wave at the throat. The Tiders did not hold with keeping. They believed the kept could not meet the sea when it came home. The Order had argued about Agathe for years, as far as Hester could tell, and in the end let her alone. The Cold House was the people's choice, Agathe said, and the people had to be cared for in whatever they chose. She walked the eight rows twice a day with a soft brush and a can of oil for the plinth-lamps. She knew every sleeper's name and the names of most of their families. She talked to them. Hester had heard her, more than once, telling the old man with his mouth open that he was quite right and she'd see to it.

"It's wrong," Hester said.

"Yes."

"You've seen it."

"On twelve of them, my dear." Agathe did not lower her voice. She had a theory, she had told Hester once, that the sleepers liked being talked over, the way babies do. "Fourteen, now, with your lad and the Corrie twins. Started midsummer. Mr. Pell says it's the damp season."

"There's no damp season. It's been dry since Seedmonth."

"So I said to Mr. Pell."

"What did Mr. Pell say?"

"He said it was the damp season." Agathe's mouth moved in a way that would have been a smile on someone less tired. "He's had the brinewright in to recast two of them. Mrs. Ommaney in the sixth row and the Hallam boy. Forty sols each, out of the charity fund. They wept again inside the month."

Hester looked down the long rows. Now that she knew what to look for she saw it: the faint misting on a casing here, three plinths along; on another at the end of the second row, where a big man lay kept with his boots on. The light found the wet and made it shine. It was not much. If you did not know the place, you would not have seen it at all.

"Show me," she said.

They walked the rows. Agathe pointed and Hester looked. Fourteen casings wept. Every one of them wept in lines. Every line ran the same way: down, and toward the eastern end of the plinth. On the plinths that were turned crosswise, at the ends of the rows, the lines ran toward the side of the casing that faced east, which was not toward the feet at all but toward the wall. Hester stood at one of those with her hands in her pockets and looked at the wall. It was plain whitewashed brick. Beyond it, she knew, there was nothing but the last few yards of Coldwater Street and then the sea-wall and then the White, the whole flat glittering width of it, out to where the Halcyon sat on its dome two miles east with its Lantern.

"What is it?" she said. "What does it mean, if they weep?"

"It means they're losing their keep," Agathe said. "Casings weep when they're tired. It's natural in the old ones, a little. Sixty, seventy years, you get a sweat on some of them in summer, and the brinewright comes and gives them a fresh coat. But it is slow, my dear. It is slow and it is all over, like a cold glass. This—" She lifted her chin at the lines. "I've not seen this."

"What happens if it goes on?"

Agathe looked at her for a moment, the look of a woman deciding how much to give a customer who has paid.

"They thin," she said. "Casing gets thin. Then it gets a crack, and the crack goes, and they come back."

Come back. That was how the Cold House said it. Hester had heard it said for eleven years and had heard, under it, every time, the other thing it meant. A sleeper who came back came back to the moment they had been kept in. A man kept with a knife in his belly came back with a knife in his belly. A girl kept in the last hour of the bloom came back in the last hour of the bloom. Keeping did not mend. Everybody knew that, and the Cold House knew it best, because the Cold House was where the people went who could not afford to be kept anywhere that expected a cure.

"How long?" she said.

"I don't know. I don't. Tide willing, they'll find what it is."

"Who's they?"

"The Halcyon, I suppose." Agathe's mouth did the thing again. "Mr. Pell's written to them."

"When?"

"Midsummer."

Hester nodded. She was conscious of her purse, emptier now, against her ribs, and of the taste still at the back of her tongue, faint, like a coin she had sucked.

They had come round again to the fourth row. Tib lay as he had lain. The beads stood on his casing. Agathe stopped beside him and looked down at him the way she looked at all of them, the way a gardener looks along a bed.

"He's clear," she said. "Yours is clear, my dear. You look." She put out a finger, not to touch, and drew it in the air along the casing's flank. "See it? Clean through. That's what you watch for. The living keep clear and the dead keep grey. If one goes grey you'll know it's done and nothing to be had by keeping it. Yours is as clear as the day they did it. Hold on to that."

Hester looked. She had looked every month for eleven years and it was true, the casing was perfectly clear, so clear that you forgot it, so clear that only the beads standing on it told you where its surface was. On the plinth across the aisle a woman lay in a casing that had a faint pearly bloom in it, like breath on a mirror. Hester had never asked about that one. She did not ask now.

"Mr. Pell," she said. "Is he in?"

* * *

Mr. Pell was not in, which surprised no one. Mr. Pell was at a meeting of the Charitable Board, said Ennis at the desk, or at the Exchequer, or possibly at his sister's. Mr. Pell was a great deal at his sister's. Hester left a note. She wrote it standing at the desk with Ennis's pen. She knew, as she wrote it, that it was the kind of note that is written so that the writer can say afterward that she wrote it.

*Mr. Pell. Casing 211 is weeping, as are thirteen others. The weeping is directional (east) and the brine tastes quick. This is not damp. I would be grateful to know what the Halcyon has said and when a Reader is to be sent. H. Vail (sister). Customs House, Dry Harbour.*

She read it over and added *Assay Bench* after *Customs House*, because the Exchequer seal on her stool was the only thing about her the Charitable Board was likely to respect. Then she read it again and thought that it sounded like a woman who was frightened and had decided to sound like a customs schedule instead.

"I'll see he gets it," Ennis said.

"Ta."

She stood a moment longer. Through the double doors she could see the end of the fourth row. From here the casings were just light, a long low glitter under the windows, like the White itself on a winter morning. You could not see the beads from here. You could not see who was in which.

She had not meant to think about the night they kept him. She did not, as a rule. She had a way of not thinking about it that was like a way of walking round a soft place in a floor: practised, and so quick she hardly knew she did it. But the Sere was in, and it had been Russet then too, the end of Russet, the night before Lantern Night. She had been standing at this desk, in a coat that had been her mother's. Their father had been outside on the step, drunk, which was not his fault, because he had been sitting up with Tib for four nights. The bloom had been in Tib's lungs for three weeks by then, and the physician from the Exchequer dispensary had said, kindly, *days*. The Cold House had a bed. The keeping fee was six sols a month, though it had been five then. Hester had been earning nine. The clerk, who was not Ennis, had turned the form round on the felt for her to sign. She had taken the pen. And before that, upstairs in the room in Tanner's Row, an hour before, with the fever heat coming off him like a stove—

He had said something to her. He had opened his eyes and found her and said something, very clearly, the way the very sick sometimes do. And she had come down Coldwater Street with it in her ears and stood at this desk. She had signed her name, Hester Vail, sister, on a form that did not ask what the sleeper himself might have said, because the sleeper himself was not considered competent.

She stepped round it. Quick and practised. She looked at the board over the inner door — DO NOT TOUCH THE CASINGS — and buttoned her right glove over the finger that had.

"Morning, Ennis."

"Morning, Miss Vail. Mind the step."

* * *

Outside, the Sere had got stronger. It came up Coldwater Street in long dry shoves and the salt in it stung her face. Hester turned her collar higher and walked west, away from the sea-wall, toward the harbour and her work.

At the corner of Coldwater and Chandler's Lane the street rose, as all Lowside's streets rose eventually, because Lowside was built on the old seabed and the rest of Ambry was built on the old shore. From the top of the rise you could see over the roofs. She did not usually stop. She stopped today, the way you stop to count a change you were sure of.

Lowside lay below her, a slope of grey slate and salt-glazed chimney pots, smoking. Beyond the roofs was the old sea-wall, tall and black and stupid. It had once kept water out of a town that now had no water to keep out. Beyond the wall, the White.

People who had not grown up on the Rim never believed how white it was. They thought it would be grey, or yellow, like sand. It was not. It was the white of new paper and new teeth and bone in the sun. It ran flat to the edge of the world and past it, so flat that the eye kept looking for a slope to make sense of it and failed. In the morning, in Russet, it was faintly rose with the low sun, and the long shallow pans along the margin, where the brine stood, were coloured in bands: pink, and copper-green, and pink again, where the shrimp and the weed were. Out on it, small, the sails of two landships were standing in toward the harbour, white on white. One had three masts. It was the *Constant Wren*, a day early. Hester knew her by the set of her topsails, which was not a thing she had ever wanted to know; twenty-one years in the Customs House teach you a few things whether you want them or not.

And two miles out, east, on the only rise in all that flatness, the Halcyon.

You could not see much of it from here. A dome of salt, white on the white, that might have been a cloud's shadow except that shadows are darker. On top of it the Lantern stood up thin and bright and tall: a tower of salt-glass and iron. At its head the great mirrors turned slowly all day to take the sun and send it down into the dome. They had caught the sun now. As she watched, the Lantern's head flashed once, white and hard, as the mirrors came round. The flash came again. It would go on like that till dusk, the long slow blink that the whole city lived under and no longer saw.

Somewhere under that dome, Mr. Pell's letter of midsummer was presumably sitting on somebody's desk.

Hester had never been to the Halcyon. She had never had cause to go. The Halcyon came to the Customs House when it wanted something, in the persons of its Readers, men and women in grey wool gowns who smelled faintly of brine and were always cold. They tasted her grain and countersigned her schedules and occasionally, when they were young, tried to explain to her what she had just told them. She did not think about the Halcyon much. It was where the grain went, and where the light came from, and it was where you sent the clever children of the Upper Ward, and Lowside's children if Lowside's children were very clever and very lucky. Then you got them back thirty years later, Settled, with white hair and salt in their tears. It was a fact of the landscape, like the sea-wall.

The taste of the drop was still faintly on her tongue. Quick.

She thought: something in that room is leaving.

She thought: it is leaving east.

The Lantern flashed. Down in the harbour, a bell began to ring for the *Constant Wren*, which meant that the excisemen were going down to the quay and that in about half an hour Comptroller Mallory would want to know where the assayer was. Hester put her bare hand in her pocket, found the edge of her purse and closed her fingers on it. She told herself she was counting. Then she went down Chandler's Lane toward the harbour, fast, with the wind behind her, and did not look east again.


# Chapter 2: The Dry Harbour

> Grain shall be declared by grade, as Shelf, Middling, or Deep. False declaration is fraud. No declaration of Hush grain shall be received, there being none lawfully for sale.
>
> — *Customs Schedule of the Free City of Ambry, Article IX*

The harbour of Ambry had been dry for nine hundred years, and nobody had told the harbour.

It still had the long curve of the quay in black Marrane stone, with iron bollards worn bright on one side by the ropes of ships that had been firewood for centuries. It had a harbour-master's tower, and taverns called the Anchor and the Mermaid and the Drowned Man after things no living person in Ambry had ever seen. It had steps going down from the quay to where the water had been. At the bottom of the steps, where the sea should have slapped at green weed, the steps simply stopped. There was the White, level and blank, and a fringe of grey crust where the salt had piled against the stone like snow against a door. On the wall above the steps you could still see the tide-marks. There were three of them, faint and brown, nine centuries old, one above another. Lowside children were told they were the marks of the sea's fingers, where it had held on before it went.

The landships moored at the quay where the ships had. They came in off the White under bare poles, the wind spilled from their sails, the big iron runners grinding and then hissing as the pilots dropped the brakes. They rode up the long ramps of packed salt that the harbour-men raked smooth every morning. At the top they came to rest with their bowsprits over the quay and their sterns out on the plain. In a good season there were forty of them at once, three-masters and two-masters and the little single-sail skiffs, their hulls painted blue and red and ochre, their sails furled grey. From the end of the quay it looked very much like a picture of a harbour. You could almost hear water.

Hester could not hear water. She could hear Comptroller Mallory.

"Vail. *Vail.* The *Wren*'s been on the ramp twenty minutes and I've excisemen standing about on the quay like hens. Where have you been?"

"The first of the month, sir."

"The first of the—" Mallory remembered, visibly, and visibly decided not to have remembered. He was a heavy, soft, careful man with a reddish wig that sat on him like a cat on a fence. Daisy had been right about the wet fox. "Well, you're here now. Forty crates. Ferrers and Lisk. Shelf grain for curing, so the manifest says, Exchequer-certified at the warehouse in Quell. Sample it and sign it, and let's have the excise off and the *Wren* unloaded before the Sere takes the roof off."

"Exchequer-certified in Quell," Hester said.

"So the manifest says."

"Since when does the Exchequer have a certifying warehouse in Quell?"

Mallory blinked. "Since it put one there, I suppose. Sample it, Vail. Sign it."

* * *

The assay room of the Ambry Customs House was on the second floor at the back, above the bond-shed. It had north light, which was the only good thing about it. It had a long bench of slate scarred by two hundred years of grain, a rack of brass scales, and a rack of tasting spoons in horn and silver, horn for Shelf and silver for anything better. It had two basins of brine and a cupboard of reference grains in sealed jars, labelled by assayers long dead. It had a stove that did not work. It had Daisy Fenn.

"She lives," said Daisy, as Hester came in. "Mallory's been hopping. He's been hopping like a man with a wasp in his breeches. I told him you were at your devotions."

"I was at the Cold House."

"I know where you were, duck. I didn't tell *him*." Daisy was forty-one, round, quick and loud. She had five children and had been back on her stool nine days after the fifth. She had an assayer's eye, though not an assayer's tongue: she could grade Shelf and Middling by look and crumble and weight, and she kept the most accurate ledger in the Customs House. "How's your lad?"

Hester hung her coat on its nail. She thought about the beads.

"Same," she said. Then, because it was Daisy: "Weeping."

Daisy's face changed. "Oh, Hes."

"Fourteen of them. The charity ward. Pell says it's damp."

"*Damp.*" Daisy put an enormous amount of opinion into one syllable. Then she looked at Hester's face again and put her pen down. "What d'you want me to do?"

"Nothing. Sample the *Wren*." Hester took down the horn spoons and a small brass scale and the tray of numbered dishes. "Did you see the manifest? Certified in Quell."

"The Exchequer hasn't a warehouse in Quell."

"No."

"Mallory says it has."

"Mallory," said Hester, "would say the Exchequer has a warehouse on the moon, if Ferrers and Lisk put it on a manifest in a good hand."

Daisy snorted and went back to her ledger.

The excisemen brought up five crates, chosen at random. That was the law: one crate in eight, opened on the quay before witnesses, carried up to the bench under seal. Hester signed for the seals and broke them. The crates were good Hask pine, iron-banded, with the Ferrers and Lisk mark burned into the lids, an F and an L leaning on each other like drunk men. Inside, under sacking, the grain was packed in waxed paper bundles of five pounds each. She chose a bundle from the top of the first crate, one from the middle of the third and one from the bottom of the fifth, which meant unpacking it, which made the excisemen sigh. She ignored them. She laid the three bundles on the slate and opened them.

Shelf grain was grey-white and coarse, a little damp, with a smell of the surface crust: cold, faintly sulphurous, like a cellar step. It came up from the first few fathoms under the White, where the delvers worked open pits. It was cheap, and it kept a herring for a season. A Rim housewife would buy it by the bucket.

This grain was white. Not grey-white: white. It was white the way the White was white in high summer, so white it had a little blue in it. The crystals were large and clean-edged, sharp-cornered cubes, some of them as big as the nail of her thumb. It was perfectly dry. And it was warm.

Hester had put her hand flat on the open bundle to feel its weight, as she always did. She took her hand away and looked at it. Then she put it back.

Warm. Not hot. The warmth of a stone wall in the evening after a sunny day. Grain was cold, always. The deeper it came up from, the colder. Old delvers said that deep grain kept the cold of the bottom of the world. It was not true, not exactly; it was only that stillsalt did not readily take up heat, the way it did not readily take up anything. But it was always cold to the hand, and Shelf grain was cold, and Middling was colder, and Deep was so cold that the Readers who handled it wore gloves.

This was warm.

"Daisy."

"Mm."

"Come and put your hand on this."

Daisy came. She put her hand on it, flat, and her eyebrows went up into her cap.

"It's been in the sun," she said. "On deck."

"It's been in a crate, in a hold, under sacking, for three days on the White. And it's the second bell of the morning in Russet." Hester bent close to the bundle and looked into it. She took up a pair of fine brass tweezers and lifted out one of the large crystals and held it to the north light from the window.

It was clear. Clean, sharp, a cube with very slightly bevelled edges, the kind of crystal you got only from deep grain, where the salt had lain undisturbed under the weight of everything above it. And inside it, near one corner, there was a bead.

At first she thought it was a bubble. Inclusions were common; brine got trapped in grain as it formed. The old delvers called them *eyes*. But eyes in grain were always still, sealed in the crystal long ago, fixed as a fly in amber, as people said who had not stood in the Cold House. This bead was not still. As she turned the crystal in the tweezers it moved. It slid along the inner face toward the lowest point, slow as honey. When she turned the crystal back it slid back, and settled, and trembled.

Liquid. Liquid water, in grain, moving.

Hester set the crystal down very gently in a dish and stood for a moment with her hands flat on the bench.

"Hes?" said Daisy.

"Get me the reference jars," Hester said. "The Deep. All of them. And the—" She thought. "The old cupboard. The bottom shelf. The ones Mr. Thorne put up."

"Those are Halcyon samples. We're not supposed to open those without a Reader present."

"Then don't watch me open them."

* * *

It took her an hour. The excisemen, bored, went down to the quay to smoke. Mallory came up once, saw her face, and went down again. Daisy did not go anywhere.

Hester did it properly, because if she did not do it properly no one would believe her, and if she was right about this she was going to need to be believed. She weighed. She crumbled. She dissolved a measured pinch of the warm grain in fresh brine and watched how it went down, and it went down slowly, sullenly, as the deepest grains did, as though reluctant to let go of being salt. She did the same with the reference grains from the cupboard. There were jars of Deep at forty fathoms and eighty and a hundred and forty, sealed in wax by the Halcyon in 861 and kept for comparison in every customs house on the Rim. The Ferrers grain dissolved slower than any of them.

Then she tasted.

She tasted the Shelf first, from the jar, to clear her mouth: grey, sulphurous, the faint sweetness hardly there, a feather on each eyelid. Then the Middling. Then the Deep at forty, the Deep at eighty, the Deep at a hundred and forty. Each was sweeter and each was heavier, and at a hundred and forty the coins on her eyelids were gold sovereigns, and she had to sit down on her stool for a moment and wait for her eyes to stop watering. That was the deepest grain she had ever had in her mouth, and she had had it in her mouth perhaps a dozen times in her life, because there was not much of it, and it went to the Brineries, and the Brineries used it to keep people.

Then she rinsed her mouth three times with fresh water and spat into the basin. She took a single grain of the warm white salt from the *Constant Wren*, no larger than a sesame seed, on the tip of a silver spoon, and put it on her tongue.

The weight came down behind her eyes like a door closing.

It was nothing like the Deep at a hundred and forty. It was heavier than that, so heavy that for an instant she could not see the window, could not see the bench, could see only white, and in the white the sense of something enormous pressing, pressing, on the far side of her face. The sweetness was so deep it was nearly bitter. She knew, as surely as she had ever known anything on that stool, that this salt had not come from a hundred and forty fathoms, or from three hundred. It had come up from somewhere much farther down than any delver's pit in the open White. It came from a depth that only one institution on the Rim was licensed to dig to. The Charter said, in so many words, that grain from that depth could not be sold.

And under the weight, under the sweetness, the other thing. Bright and metallic and wet. A bitten tongue. A startle. The taste of the bead in Tib's casing, the taste of something going somewhere.

Quick.

She sat with her eyes shut and the salt dissolving on her tongue and her hands gripping the edge of the slate. Then, because she had come this far and because she did not know when she would have the chance again, she did the thing that she was not, strictly, licensed to do.

She went into the hold.

It was not a thing she had been taught. Nobody had taught her. It was a thing she had found out, at fourteen, sitting on this same stool with a pinch of Middling on her tongue: that if she went still enough, still the way a cat goes still before it jumps, the taste opened. Breath slow. Heart slow. Hands slow, shoulders slow, the muscles of her face. The whole of her settling down and down around the grain on her tongue, like silt settling in a glass, until she was as still as it was, nearly. Then the salt, which had been only a taste, became a place.

Readers called it *reading*. In the Customs House it was called nothing, because the Customs House did not officially know that its assayer could do it.

She went still. The office went away.

For a moment there was only the white, and the weight. Then there was a sound.

It was a roar. That was what everyone said, the few people who could read deep grain and the many who said they could: that old grain from the White held a roar in it, like the sound in a shell held to the ear. The Readers called it residue. They said it was the noise of the Ebb, of the great crust forming, caught in the salt at the moment it set. Hester had heard it in the reference Deep, faintly, like wind under a door. She heard it now, and it was not faint. It filled her. It was a vast low continuous rushing, with a shape to it she could not grasp, a rise and a fall, a rise and a fall, and the rise coming again before the fall had finished.

And over it, inside it, something else.

A cry. High and falling. Two notes and then a third that cracked. A sound like a gate hinge, or a child calling a long way off, or a cat, though it was not a cat. It came once and then again, closer, and then a third time, and something in the back of her neck went cold. She did not know it. She had grown up on the Rim and lived thirty-three years in Ambry and she did not know what it was. It was a living sound. She was sure of that. Something in that salt had been alive, and crying out, at the moment the salt had set.

She came out of the hold the way you come up from deep water, too fast, with a sound in her own throat. She was on the stool. Daisy had both hands on her shoulders.

"Hes. *Hester.*"

"I'm all right."

"You went—you went all still. Like a—like one of *them*." Daisy's round face was blotched. "Your lips went blue."

"I'm all right." Her hands were very cold. Her mouth was very dry. She found the water jug without looking and drank from it, the whole thing, and her throat was still dry. "Daisy, write this down. Write it in the ledger. In ink."

"Write what?"

"That the *Constant Wren* cargo, Ferrers and Lisk, manifested as Shelf grain for curing, is not Shelf. It is not Middling. It is not Deep." Her voice came out steady. That was good. She would need it steady. "It's Hush grain."

Daisy stared at her.

"And it's quickened."

* * *

There was a scene, of course. Hester had known there would be a scene.

Comptroller Mallory came up the stairs at a speed he would not have attempted for his own house on fire. He stood in the door of the assay room, with his wig askew, and told her in a hoarse whisper what she could not do. She could not seize an Exchequer contractor's cargo on the basis of a *taste*. She could not write *Hush grain* in the Customs House ledger, because there was no such category, the schedule did not permit its declaration, it did not legally exist in trade. She could not have Ferrers and Lisk's crates nailed shut and carried into the bond-shed and sealed with the blue seal of the Free City of Ambry, which was what she had already had the excisemen do while he was coming up the stairs.

"It's the Exchequer's own contractor," he said. "Ferrers and Lisk. *Ferrers.* Do you know whose name that is, Vail? Do you know whose cousin—"

"I know whose name it is, sir."

"Then you'll release it."

"I'll not."

The room went very quiet. Down on the quay someone was shouting at a winch.

"I'll not release it," Hester said. She was standing now. She had not decided to stand, but she was standing, and she was taller than Mallory, as she was taller than most men, and she let him see it. "Article Nine. No declaration of Hush grain shall be received, there being none lawfully for sale. If there's Hush grain on the *Wren*, then there's grain on the *Wren* that's been unlawfully sold. The Customs House is required to seize it and to notify the Halcyon under the Charter. And there is. And I have. Daisy has it in the ledger."

"In ink," said Daisy, who had gone pink and stubborn.

"And under Article Twelve," Hester went on, "the Halcyon is required to send its Inspector of Trade Grain to examine and confirm or overturn the seizure within the day. If he overturns it, sir, I'll release it myself and carry the crates down to the quay on my own back and apologise to Mr. Ferrers in writing. If he doesn't—"

She stopped. She had been going to say, *if he doesn't, then you will want to have been on the right side of the ledger*. She did not say it. Mallory had a wife and a daughter at the Glassmarket school, and he was not a wicked man. He was a soft man in a hard place, and he had looked, for a moment, frightened, and she did not think it was of her.

"Send for the Inspector," Mallory said at last. His voice was thick. "Send for him, then. On your head, Vail."

"Yes, sir."

He went down the stairs. Daisy let out a long breath and put her face in her hands.

"*On your head,*" she said through her fingers. "Oh, Hes. Oh, you great stubborn *mule*."

"It's Hush grain," Hester said.

"I know. I wrote it down." Daisy took her hands away. "What was it? When you went still. What did you—what's it like?"

Hester thought about the cry. Two notes, and a third that cracked.

"Loud," she said.

* * *

The Halcyon's Inspector of Trade Grain arrived at a quarter past the ninth bell, when the light was gone from the north window and Daisy had gone home to her five children and the assay room was lit by a single oil lamp that smoked. Hester had sent the runner at noon. She had begun to think that the Halcyon, like Mr. Pell, was going to turn out to be at its sister's.

She heard him before she saw him. Somebody was coming up the stairs two at a time and humming. It was not a tune; it was the kind of hum a man makes when he is thinking and has forgotten that other people have ears. The door banged open and a man stood in it, out of breath, in a grey wool gown with a white cord at the shoulder and the hem of the gown spattered to the knee with salt-mud.

"Inspector," he said, "of Trade Grain. Aske. Corvin Aske. I'm sorry, I'm late, I was below when your runner came and then the lift stuck, and the Causeway's solid with ox-drays, so I sailed. Where is it?"

He was about forty-five, thin, with a long clever face and spectacles so thick that his eyes swam behind them like fish in a bowl. His hair stood up. His fingers were stained to the second knuckle with ink, black on the right hand and blue on the left, as if he wrote with both. He was cold; she could see it from the bench. His lips had the faint bluish line that Readers' lips had, and he held his hands tucked under his arms as he stood there, the way all grain-workers did, to warm them.

"In the bond-shed," Hester said. "Under seal. I kept back samples." She pushed the tray of numbered dishes across the slate toward him.

"You did? Good. Good." He came to the bench and bent over the dishes, bringing his face very close to them, eighteen inches, twelve, six, peering. "Whose bench is this?"

"Mine."

"Yours." He looked up. Behind the spectacles his eyes went over her, her customs blue, the darned elbow, the stool. "You're the assayer."

"I am."

"You're the one who wrote *Hush*." He said it the way a man might say *you're the one who saw the comet*. "On the seizure. Your runner had a copy. *Hush grain, quickened.* In ink. I've never seen a customs house write that. I've never seen anyone write that outside the Halcyon. Nobody's supposed to know what it *means* outside the Halcyon. What made you—no. Wait. Don't tell me." He took a silver spoon from the rack, looked at it, put it back, and took a small horn case from inside his gown. From it he took a spoon of some white metal she did not know, finer than any on her rack. "May I?"

"It's your Charter."

"Ha. So it is." He took a grain from the third dish on the tip of the white spoon and put it on his tongue.

She watched him. She had watched a good many Readers taste grain, and they mostly did it with a sort of absent-minded grandeur, as if they were tasting wine at a dinner they were bored by. Aske did not. He shut his eyes. He went still, not deeply, not the way she had, but enough. She saw his breathing slow. She saw the colour go out of his lips, a little more. For about the time it would take to count to twenty he stood like that in the lamplight, bent over the bench, his ink-stained hands flat on the slate.

Then he opened his eyes and said, very softly, "Oh, that's *wrong*."

His hands were shaking.

"That's beautifully wrong," he said. "That's the most wrong thing I've had in my mouth in twenty years. Where did it—the *Wren*. Ferrers and Lisk. From Quell, the manifest says? From *Quell*." He gave a short laugh that had nothing funny in it. "That didn't come from Quell. That came from under my feet. That came from—" He stopped. He looked at Hester as if he had just remembered that she was a person and not a piece of furniture. "You tasted it."

"I tasted it."

"And you knew it was Hush grain. By taste."

"By weight," Hester said. "Taste gives me the grade. Weight gives me the depth. I've never had Hush grain in my mouth before today, so I couldn't swear to it by comparison. But I've had the Deep at a hundred and forty from the Halcyon reference cupboard, and this is twice the weight at least. There's nothing I know of on the open White that goes deeper than that. So it's from under your feet. And it's quick."

"*Quick.*" He was staring at her. "You said quick on the seizure, too. Where did you learn that word?"

"An assayer called Thorne. He had this stool before me." She paused. "He's dead."

"I knew Thorne. He couldn't taste quick. Nobody can taste quick, it's not a—" Aske stopped again. He put his hand in his gown and brought out a flat leather case, the size of a prayer book, and laid it on the slate and opened it. Inside, in twelve small wells lined with green baize, were twelve pinches of grain. Each well had a number stamped beside it in brass, and nothing else. "Would you do something for me, Miss—?"

"Vail."

"Miss Vail. Would you tell me what these are?"

It was a test. She knew it was a test, and he knew she knew. She looked at him for a moment over the lamp. She thought about Mallory on the stairs, and about the blue seal on the bond-shed door, and about what it would cost her in the morning if this thin, cold, humming man went back across the White and wrote that the Ambry assayer had seized a Ferrers cargo on the strength of a taste and been wrong.

She rinsed her mouth. She took up a horn spoon.

She did all twelve. It took her the better part of the tenth bell. The Customs House around her was dark and creaking and cold, and somewhere below her the night watchman was singing to himself in the bond-shed. Aske did not speak once. He stood by the bench with his arms folded and his hands in his armpits, and watched her, and hummed, very quietly, under his breath. She did not think he knew he was doing it.

"One," she said. "Shelf, under two fathoms, Southern Rim margin, from the taste of the sulphur. Two. Middling, about twenty. Three. Middling, thirty-five, thereabouts, and it's been dried in an oven, badly, because it's lost its sweet at the edges. Four. Deep, sixty. Five. Dead salt. Varrow rock-salt. You put that in to see if I'd guess. Six. Deep. Ninety, a hundred. Old. It's been out of the ground—" She paused, tasting. "Forty years? Fifty. Seven. Shelf. Eight. Middling. Twenty-five. Nine. Deep." That one was strange. She held it on her tongue for a long time. "This one's been kept. It's casing grain. Somebody kept something in it, and then returned it, and then used the same grain again to keep something else. It's—tired. Twice-used. I didn't know you could do that."

"You can't," said Aske, "lawfully. Go on."

"Ten. Middling, Western Rim, about fifteen. Eleven. Deep, two hundred, from the Halcyon's own pits, I'd say. It's got that sort of cleanness the Halcyon reference jars have. And twelve." She tasted twelve. She sat back. "Twelve's the same as the *Wren*."

The watchman was still singing in the bond-shed. Aske was not humming any more.

"Twelve's from the Fifth Fathom stays," he said. "Hush-grade. Pushed up from below. Quickened. I took it with my own hands two days ago, and it is not supposed to exist anywhere on the Rim except in a locked cabinet in my workroom. And you've just graded the other eleven better than half the Readers on my own Bench could." He took his spectacles off again and did not wipe them. Without them his face was naked and myopic and younger. "Who taught you?"

"Nobody taught me. I watched Thorne."

"You read, too. You went into the hold to read the *Wren* grain. Didn't you?" He saw her face. "It's all right. I won't put it in my report. I'm not a Proctor. What did you hear?"

She hesitated. She had not told Daisy. She did not know why she wanted to tell this man, except that he had asked the question the way a man asks who actually wants the answer.

"The roar," she said. "Like everyone says. But louder. And—something in it."

"What?"

"A cry. An animal. Something alive." She heard how it sounded and set her jaw. "High, and falling. Like a hinge. I don't know what it was. I've never heard it."

Aske was quiet a moment.

"No," he said. "No, I don't suppose you have."

He put his spectacles back on. He closed the leather case. He looked round the assay room: at the cracked slate, at the dead stove, at the reference cupboard with Mr. Thorne's labels.

"How long have you been on this stool, Miss Vail?"

"Fourteen years on the stool. Twenty-one in the House."

"What do they pay you?"

She told him. He winced.

"I'm going to confirm your seizure," he said. "Tonight. In writing, in ink, under the Charter, with my seal on it, and send a copy to the Proctory and another to the Exchequer, and I'm going to enjoy that last one very much. And then I'm going to make you an offer. And you're going to say no, I expect. People like you generally do. But I'm going to make it anyway."

"What offer?"

"I'm a Reader of the Halcyon," Aske said. "Assay Bench. Once in ten years a Reader of the Halcyon may admit a candidate to the Collegium without fees. It's called the Reader's Privilege. I've never used mine. I've never met anyone I wanted to use it on." He said it quite simply, without any flourish, as if he were reading out a grade. "The Turning Intake matriculates on the sixth. That's five days. You'd have to pass the Entry Assay, but you've just passed it, more or less, on a customs bench in the dark. I'd sponsor you on my Bench. You'd learn to do properly what you've been doing by instinct, and a great deal you've never dreamed of. You'd be good. I think you'd be very good. I think you might be the best tongue I've ever met."

Hester looked at him.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"I've a brother," she said. "In the Cold House. I pay for him. Six sols a month. The Halcyon doesn't pay its aspirants, I know that much. Fees or no fees, I'd have no wage."

"There are stipends—"

"Thank you, Reader." She stood up. She was conscious of her darned elbow and the smoking lamp, and of her whole life arranged on and around this stool like the tools in their racks. "It's kind. I'm not a child. I've a wage and a debt and a brother. I'll not throw them over for the chance of a gown."

Aske did not argue. That surprised her. He nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed a grade he had expected. He took a card from his gown and wrote something on the back of it in blue ink with his left hand and put it on the slate beside the dishes.

"My lodging in Ambry," he said. "Above Harker's the bookbinder, in Quill Street. I'm there until the sixth." He turned to go. At the door he stopped, with his back to her, and stood a moment, and when he turned round his face had changed.

"Your brother," he said. "In the Cold House. Has his casing been weeping?"

The watchman had stopped singing. Hester stood very still.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've seen it," said Aske. "Not at the Cold House; I've never been inside the Cold House. Elsewhere. Things that are kept, weeping. And grain coming up from the Fifth that tastes the way that cargo tastes." He looked at the dishes on the slate, at the warm white salt in its waxed paper. "Something is wrong in the deep, Miss Vail. I've thought so for a year. I don't know what it is. I think—" He stopped himself, visibly. "I think it would be useful to have someone with your tongue, finding out."

He went out. She heard him going down the stairs two at a time, humming.

Hester stood in the assay room for a long time. Then she picked up the card. On the front, engraved, it said *Corvin Aske, Reader, the Halcyon*. On the back, in blue ink, in a small fast hand that sloped hard to the right, it said: *Quill St, above Harker's. Come and find out why.*

She put it in her purse, with the four flecks that were all that was left in it, and blew out the lamp.

The bond-shed was empty.

Hester stood in its door at the first bell of the next morning, with the key in her hand and the blue seal of the Free City of Ambry hanging from the hasp by one string. The other string had been cut very neatly, with a knife, close to the wax, so that whoever looked at it first might have taken it for whole. She did not take it for whole. She stood and looked at the floor.

The floor was Marrane flagstone, swept. Yesterday afternoon forty iron-banded crates of Hask pine had stood on it in five stacks of eight, sealed, under the yellow chalk numbers she had written on them herself. They were gone. The floor where they had stood was cleaner than the floor around it. Someone had swept up after them.

"The watchman," she said aloud, to nobody. Then she went to find the watchman, who was asleep in the porter's cubby by the quay door with an empty bottle of Quell clear in his lap. She looked at the bottle a long moment. It was not the kind of bottle a night watchman bought on a night watchman's wage.

She went upstairs to the assay room, and opened the ledger at yesterday's date, and found that the page was not there.

It had not been torn. It had been cut, the same way as the seal string, close to the spine with a good knife. Unless you knew the ledger you would not have seen it. Hester knew the ledger. She ran her thumb along the stub. Then she turned to the back, where Daisy kept her own fair copy of each day's entries in a smaller, neater hand for the end-of-month returns. That page had been cut too.

She sat down on her stool.

The north window was grey. On the slate in front of her stood the tray of numbered dishes from yesterday, with yesterday's samples still in them. Whoever had cleared the bond-shed had not thought to clear the assay bench, because whoever had cleared the bond-shed did not know very much about assay benches. Dish three held a little pyramid of white grain, sharp-cornered, clean. She put her hand flat over it, not touching. Warm.

She took a twist of waxed paper from the drawer and tipped the dish into it and folded the paper small and put it inside her shirt. Then she sat and waited for Comptroller Mallory.

* * *

He came at the third bell, which was early for him. He did not take his coat off. He stood in the doorway with his wig on straight for once and his face very pale and told her, in the voice of a man reading something he had learned by heart, that she was suspended from her post without pay pending a hearing of the Customs Board. The charge was insubordination to a superior officer and the improper seizure of a lawfully manifested cargo. The cargo had been released overnight on the authority of the Exchequer, and the Customs House would be making a written apology to Messrs. Ferrers and Lisk.

"On whose authority?" Hester said.

"The Exchequer's. I said."

"The Exchequer doesn't have authority over a Charter seizure. Only the Halcyon does. Reader Aske confirmed it. He wrote it in ink. Under seal."

"I've seen no confirmation."

"He sent a copy to you, sir. And one to the Proctory, and one to—"

"I have seen," said Mallory, more loudly, "no confirmation." He looked at the cut stub of the ledger, which she had left open on the slate where he could see it, and looked away. "The hearing will be in Fallow. Or Cinder. You'll be written to. You'll hand in your key."

She held it out to him. He took it without touching her fingers. He was sweating, though the room was cold.

"Sir," she said. "Who came for it?"

Mallory looked at her. For a moment she thought he was going to tell her. His mouth worked.

"Go home, Vail," he said. "For God's sake. Go home."

* * *

Daisy caught her on the stairs. She had her bonnet on still, and her basket, and she pressed Hester against the wall of the stairwell with the whole of her round, furious person and said in a whisper like a kettle, "I heard. I heard it in the street. Hes. They came at the eighth bell with a dray and six men and a paper with the Exchequer's seal on it, and old Dobbs let them in and they gave him a bottle. Ruth Penny saw it from her window. Six men, Hes, and a seventh who didn't lift a thing. He stood by the dray with a lantern and a paper, and Ruth says when he held the lantern up his coat came open and he'd grey wool under it. *Grey wool.* Like a Reader."

"I know."

"And they cut the ledger. Both ledgers. *My* ledger."

"I know."

"Well, they didn't cut this." Daisy dug in her basket and pushed a folded paper into Hester's hand. "Bill of lading. The *Wren*'s. The excise copy. It was in my basket all night, because I took it home to write the duty out fair and then I fell asleep over it, because Marguerite was up at the third bell and the fourth bell and the fifth, the little madam. So it's the only one. Take it. Take it, before I lose my nerve and go and give it to Mallory."

Hester unfolded it. It was the standard form, printed in Ambry black, filled out in a clerk's fast copperplate. *Vessel: Constant Wren. Master: Jas. Orrick. Consignee: Messrs. Ferrers & Lisk, Exchequer Yard. Cargo: 40 cases Shelf grain (curing), Exch. cert. Quell.* And at the bottom, in the box for the shipper's mark, in a different hand, small and upright and very plain:

*W.L.F. per E.D.*

"Who's W.L.F.?" said Daisy.

"I don't know."

"It's not a firm. I know all the firms."

"I know." Hester folded the paper and put it inside her shirt with the twist of grain. "Ta, Daisy."

"Don't *ta* me. What are you going to do?"

Hester did not know. She stood on the stairs in her customs blue that she would have to take off, with no key, and no wage. She had four flecks in her purse, and a card from a Reader of the Halcyon, and a brother who was weeping in lines toward the east.

"Go home," she said. "Like he says."

* * *

She did not go home. She went to see her father, which was worse.

The Lowside Pans lay on the north edge of the district, where a flume on black trestles brought marsh-brine in from the Weeps. Under a long roof with thirty chimneys, thirty iron pans the size of carts stood over thirty fires, boiling the brine down to a white sludge that was raked, dried, and sold at the Corn Exchange as table salt. Dead salt. It kept nothing, it was worth almost nothing, and three hundred Lowside families had lived on it for two hundred years.

Aldous Vail had pan nineteen. He had had pan nineteen for forty years.

She found him in the steam, raking. He was a short wide man with a short wide face and forearms like a shipwright's. Like hers, they were crossed and recrossed with the white seams of old burns. He did not stop raking when he saw her. He looked up once, through the steam, and down again.

"Hes."

"Da."

"No work?"

She had not told him. She had not told anyone but Daisy. It did not surprise her that he knew. The Pans knew everything that happened in Lowside about an hour after it happened, because the Pans were where Lowside's men went when they had nowhere else to go.

"Suspended," she said.

"Why?"

"I seized a cargo."

"Whose?"

"Ferrers."

He grunted. It might have been a laugh. He raked, and the rake went *hush, hush* in the sludge. The heat came off the pan in a solid wall. Her face was already wet with it.

"Well," he said. "That's a daft thing to have done."

"It was the right thing."

"Aye. Usually is, the daft thing." He knocked the rake on the pan-edge and the white sludge fell off it in lumps. "You want money?"

"No."

"I haven't got any."

"I know, Da."

They stood. Pan eighteen and pan twenty were going on either side, the boilers stripped to the waist, shining, raking. Nobody looked at them. In the Pans you did not look at a man talking to his daughter, the same as you did not look at a man being sick.

"It's Tib," she said.

He did not stop raking. But the rhythm changed, very slightly. A half-beat.

"His casing's weeping," she said. "Fourteen of them are, in the charity ward. Agathe says they'll thin, and crack, and come back. Pell says it's damp."

"Pell's an arse."

"Yes."

"Is it the damp?"

"No."

Aldous raked. *Hush, hush.* He was sixty-one. His hair, what was left of it, was white, and his face in the steam was red and glazed like a pot. He had not been inside the Cold House in nine years. He had gone twice a month for the first two years. Then once, with drink on him, he had stood at the end of the fourth row and said to Hester, loudly, *What are we doing to him? What are we doing?* Then he had walked out. He had not been back. He paid nothing toward the fee. It was one of the many things they did not discuss.

"Let him go, Hes," he said.

"No."

"Eleven years." He did not look up. "Eleven years and nothing. No cure. No nothing. The bloom's the bloom. They've been keeping bloomers since your grandmother's time and none of them's come back cured, not one, not in the Cold House and not in the fancy houses up the hill neither. They come back and they die. That's all they do. Let the lad go."

"He's not dying. He's kept."

"He's dying slow, is what he is. Slowest a lad ever died."

"He's alive, Da. He's alive in law. Agathe says his casing's clear, he's as clear as the day—"

"I know what Agathe says." He set the rake down against the pan's rim, hard, so that it rang. He turned to look at her for the first time. His eyes were small and pale and bloodshot from the steam, and very steady. "I know what it says on that board over the door. And I know what he said. I was on the stair, Hes. *Let me sleep*, he said. *Just let me sleep.* You think I didn't hear him?"

"He asked to sleep," Hester said. Her voice did not come out as she meant it to. "That's what keeping is. It's sleep. He asked for it."

Her father only looked at her. She had not known that he heard.

It went through her like the cold goes through you on the White at night, when the sun drops and the tiles begin to tick. For a moment the heat of the Pans was nowhere. She could not have said anything if she had tried.

Aldous looked at her. Then he looked away, down at the pan, and picked the rake up again. He seemed smaller.

"Well," he said. "You've your reasons. You always have."

Neither of them spoke for a long while. Hester stood in the heat with the twist of warm grain against her ribs and the bill of lading crackling when she breathed. She thought that she had perhaps never in her life been so near to walking away from him for good. She did not walk away. She did not know why. Possibly it was only that she had nowhere else to walk to.

Then pan nineteen spat.

It was a small sound, at first. A tick. A pop, like a knot in a fire. A fleck of white sludge leapt from the surface of the brine and landed on the brick floor at her feet. Then another. Then the whole skin of the pan began to shudder and crack. Little craters opened in it and snapped shut, and white gouts the size of a thumb leapt up and fell back. The tick became a crackle. On pan eighteen the boiler looked round sharply and said, "Nineteen's going, Aldo."

"I've got her."

Hester stepped back. She knew what a spitting pan could do. She had the scars on her arms from it, from the summer she was ten and their mother was dying and she had been sent to help on the pans because there was no one to mind her. A pan that spat in earnest would throw boiling brine across a room. Men had lost eyes to it. The trick, the trick that every child on the Pans was taught, was to drop the lid on. Each pan had an iron lid on a chain and pulley overhead. You let it down fast, smothered the surface, and waited.

Aldous did not touch the lid chain.

He went to the fire-door instead, under the pan, and opened it with his rake-handle. With the iron hook he began to draw the fire out. Not all at once. A shovelful of coals onto the brick, then another, then a long pause while he watched the surface. The pan crackled and jumped. He drew another shovelful. The crackle slowed. He drew a fourth, and stood back, and the pan went *tick* — *tick* — and a long pause, and *tick*, and then a soft, continuous, grumbling bubble, low, like a cat purring in another room.

"There," he said to the pan. "There, now. Talk it out."

He stood and watched it, rake in hand, for some time. Then he raked the drawn coals together on the floor and shovelled half of them back, gently, and shut the fire-door.

"You didn't lid it," Hester said.

"No." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "You lid a spitting pan, she'll blow the lid through the roof. Or she'll go quiet and you think you've done it. Then you lift the lid, and she goes up in your face all at once. All the steam she's been saving." He knocked the rake on the rim. "Pan spits because there's something in her wants out. Air. Steam. Muck in the bottom. You can't stop it wanting. You take her off the heat slow and let her talk, and it comes out a bit at a time, and nobody gets hurt."

"Who taught you that?"

"Nobody taught me. I lost two fingers' worth of skin finding out." He held up his left hand. She had known since she was a child that the last two fingers were shiny and stiff. She had never asked. "Lid's for when you're in a hurry. Most of what goes wrong in this world, Hes, is folk in a hurry with a lid."

He went back to raking.

She stayed a while. She did not know why she stayed. She stood in the heat and watched him work, and he let her, and neither of them said anything else. When she left she put her hand on his shoulder for a moment, on the wet hot skin. He did not stop raking. But he said, without looking round, "Mind the step."

* * *

She sat that night at the table in the room in Tanner's Row and counted. It was the room she had lived in since she was eight. Tib's landships were still on the walls in pencil, at the height of a boy's arm; the paper had been changed twice, and both times she had cut it round them.

Four flecks in her purse. Two sols and nine in the jar. Eleven sols in the Lowside Savings Society. Rent paid to the end of Fallow. She did the sum twice, and both times it came out at two months of Tib's fee, three if she sold her mother's coat, which she would not. No grain-house on the Exchequer Yard would take a suspended assayer; they were owned, all of them, by men who dined with Aurel Ferrers. The Pans would take her at three sols a week, and the arithmetic would come out the same, only slower.

The Halcyon's Stay Choir paid three sols a week too, Lowside said. It paid that to the men and women who went down into the deep Fathoms and came home on their days off with blue lips and a way of sitting very still in taverns, and who came home Settled in their fifties, if they came home. Three sols a week was twelve a month. Tib's fee was six.

She thought about Mr. Thorne, who had given her the stool and died on it at sixty-eight, Settled, in the north light, sure to the end that the Halcyon Readers were a pack of jumped-up guessers. *They've got the theory, Hessie. We've got the tongue.* She thought about the bill of lading inside her shirt, and the warm grain, and the beads running east. *Pan spits because there's something in her wants out.*

She thought that she had never in her life done anything because she wanted to, only because it wanted doing.

She put the coins back in the jar. She put the card back in her purse. She went to bed, and did not sleep.

* * *

Harker the bookbinder's was in Quill Street, in the Middle Ward, where the streets were cobbled and the houses had window boxes and nobody's shutters were full of salt because the Sere did not come so far uphill. It was a narrow shop smelling of glue and leather. Above it was a narrow stair, and at the top of the stair was a narrow door. Behind it, when she knocked at the second bell on the fourth of Russet, was Corvin Aske in his shirtsleeves with a pen in each hand and ink on his nose.

"Miss Vail," he said. He looked, absurdly, as if he might burst into tears. "Oh, *good.*"

The room behind him was a rented room the size of a ship's cabin, and he had filled it with paper. Paper in drifts on the bed and the chair and the windowsill. Paper pinned to the walls, covered on both sides in the small fast sloping hand, black and blue. There were jars of grain, a brine basin on the washstand where the jug should have been, and a tray of tasting spoons. On top of the wardrobe there was a stuffed salt-lark, which looked at her with one glass eye as if it, too, wanted to know what she was doing there.

"I've been suspended," she said, in the doorway. "Without pay. The cargo's gone. Somebody cut the ledger and released it on an Exchequer paper in the middle of the night. My Comptroller says he never saw your confirmation."

"Ah," said Aske. "Yes. I wondered if that would happen." He did not look surprised. He looked, for a moment, very tired. "I'm sorry."

"Is the offer still open?"

"Yes. Oh, yes." He began clearing the chair for her, transferring an armful of paper to the bed, where it slid onto the floor. "Sit, please. Sit. I'll make tea. I've no milk. I've some Hask honey. Do you take salt in it? Halcyon people do. It's a disgusting habit. I can't stop."

She sat. He made tea on a spirit stove by the window, talking all the time. He told her the Entry Assay was a formality for anyone who could do what she'd done on the customs bench, though Reader Crisp would be on the panel and Reader Crisp disliked formalities. He told her to bring wool, and more wool. The Choir stipends were real, he said; he could put her name forward, though the Warden of the Lower Fathoms ran the Choir like a ship.

"I've thought," Hester said.

"Have you? Already?"

"Three sols a week. I can do sums." She took the tea he gave her. It was very strong, with salt in it. She drank it anyway. "Reader. What you said in the Customs House. About something wrong in the deep. And the weeping."

He had been about to sit on the bed. He stopped, half-bent, and then sat, slowly, among the paper. The tea in his hand steamed.

"Yes," he said.

"I want to know what it is."

"So do I."

"No. I mean—" She set the cup down on a pile of paper, carefully, and folded her hands. "My brother's casing is weeping. It's weeping in lines, east, and the brine on it tastes quick. Like that cargo. Like your sample twelve. There are fourteen in the charity ward doing it, and the director says it's damp. And the cargo came off the *Wren* marked Shelf, and somebody with six men and an Exchequer paper took it away in the night. And the bill of lading's got a mark on it I don't know." She took the paper from inside her shirt and unfolded it and held it out to him. "*W.L.F. per E.D.* Do you know what that is?"

Aske took the bill. He looked at it six inches from his spectacles. Then he looked at her over the top of it. His face had gone careful, as if he had stepped from a lit room into a dark one and was waiting for his eyes.

"I don't know whose mark that is," he said. "Not yet. I've a guess I don't like, and I'll not say it in a rented room with a bookbinder's boy under the floor who sells gossip to the *Courant*." He gave her back the bill. "But I'll tell you one thing, since you've earned it. And then I'll tell you why I want you, and it isn't kindness."

"Go on."

"Sample twelve. The grain that tastes like your cargo. We call grain like that quickened. It comes from places under the Halcyon where the salt goes warm and wet and the brine starts to move. Pockets. The Fathomers hold them down; it's their whole job." He turned his cup in his hands. "Five years ago there were four such pockets in the Fifth Fathom. This summer there were fourteen. It's in the Warden's returns to the Conclave, if anyone reads them, which nobody does. I've raised it twice. The second time the Deputy Chancellor thanked me for my diligence and moved to the next business, which was the price of lamp oil."

Hester said nothing. Fourteen. The same number as the casings. It meant nothing; numbers came round. She noted it anyway, as she would have noted a short weight.

"So that's why," Aske said. "I'm a Reader with a hobby and a reputation for tasting my own experiments, and nobody listens to me. You're a sworn assayer of the Free City with no stake in the Halcyon at all. If this comes to the Assize, and I'm beginning to think it will, the Assize will believe you before it believes me." He smiled, crookedly. "I did say I'd be honest. I want your tongue, Miss Vail. I want your testimony more."

She looked at him. He looked back, thin and cold and ink-nosed among his drifts of paper, with the salt-lark staring down from the wardrobe.

"All right," she said.

"All right?"

"I'll come. On the sixth."

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"You won't regret it," he said. Then: "Well. You might. But not for the reasons you think."

* * *

She went to the Cold House on the way home, though it was not the first of the month.

Ennis looked surprised. She said she would not be long. She went down the six worn steps into the cold sweet air and along the fourth row to the third window. The beads were still standing on Tib's casing in their lines. There seemed to her to be more of them. She did not know if that was true or if it was only that she was looking.

"I'm going to the Halcyon," she told him. "Don't laugh."

He did not laugh. His hand lay curled on his chest, holding its pencil.

"I'm going to find out what this is," she said. "That's all. I'm going to find out what it is, and then I'm going to stop it. It's got nothing to do with gowns." She thought. "They have a Lantern. You'd like that. I'll tell you about the Lantern."

Agathe was at the end of the row with her brush and her oil can. She came up slowly when Hester turned to go. She did not ask anything. She looked at Hester's face and seemed to find what she wanted in it.

"I shall write, my dear," she said. "If anything changes. The Halcyon takes letters. I'll write."

"Ta, Mother."

"Tide willing," Agathe said, "you'll find it."

At the top of the six steps Hester stopped and looked back. From the top step the beads were invisible again; the ward was only light lying in rows, and any of it might have been anyone. She stood a moment, and then she went up into the street, where the Sere was coming down Coldwater Street off the White, as it would all through Russet, out of the east.
