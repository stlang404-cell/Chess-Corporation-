# Chapter 2

## The Dry Harbour

> Grain shall be declared by grade, as Shelf, Middling, or Deep. False declaration is fraud. No declaration of Hush grain shall be received, there being none lawfully for sale.
>
> — *Customs Schedule of the Free City of Ambry, Article IX*

The harbour of Ambry had been dry for nine hundred years, and nobody had told the harbour.

It still had the long curve of the quay in black Marrane stone, with iron bollards worn bright by the ropes of ships that had been firewood for centuries. It had taverns called the Anchor and the Mermaid and the Drowned Man, after things no living person in Ambry had ever seen. It had steps going down from the quay to where the water had been, and at the bottom the steps simply stopped, at the White, level and blank, with a fringe of grey crust piled against the stone like snow against a door. On the wall above them you could still see three tide-marks, faint and brown, one above another. Lowside children were told they were the marks of the sea's fingers, where it had held on before it went.

The landships moored where the ships had. They came in off the White under bare poles, iron runners grinding and then hissing as the pilots dropped the brakes, and rode up ramps of raked salt to rest with their bowsprits over the quay and their sterns out on the plain. In a good season there were forty of them at once, hulls painted blue and red and ochre, sails furled grey. From the end of the quay it looked like a picture of a harbour. You could almost hear water.

Hester could not hear water. She could hear Comptroller Mallory.

"Vail. *Vail.* The *Wren*'s been on the ramp twenty minutes and I've excisemen standing about on the quay like hens. Where have you been?"

"The first of the month, sir."

"The first of the—" Mallory remembered, visibly, and visibly decided not to have remembered. He was a heavy, soft, careful man with a reddish wig that sat on him like a cat on a fence. Daisy had been right about the wet fox. "Well, you're here now. Forty crates. Ferrers and Lisk. Shelf grain for curing, so the manifest says, Exchequer-certified at the warehouse in Quell. Sample it and sign it, and let's have the excise off and the *Wren* unloaded before the Sere takes the roof off."

"Exchequer-certified in Quell," Hester said.

"So the manifest says. Sample it, Vail. Sign it."

* * *

The assay room of the Ambry Customs House was on the second floor at the back, above the bond-shed. It had north light, which was the only good thing about it. It had a long bench of slate scarred by two hundred years of grain, a rack of brass scales, and a rack of tasting spoons in horn and silver, horn for Shelf and silver for anything better. It had a cupboard of reference grains in sealed jars, labelled by assayers long dead. It had a stove that did not work. It had Daisy Fenn.

"She lives," said Daisy, as Hester came in. "Mallory's been hopping like a man with a wasp in his breeches. I told him you were at your devotions."

"I was at the Cold House."

"I know where you were, duck. I didn't tell *him*." Daisy was forty-one, round, quick and loud. She had five children and had been back on her stool nine days after the fifth. She had an assayer's eye, though not the tongue, and she kept the most accurate ledger in the Customs House. "How's your lad?"

Hester hung her coat on its nail. She thought about the beads.

"Same," she said. Then, because it was Daisy: "Weeping."

Daisy's face changed. "Oh, Hes."

"Fourteen of them. The charity ward. Pell says it's damp."

"*Damp.*" Daisy put an enormous amount of opinion into one syllable. Then she looked at Hester's face again and put her pen down. "What d'you want me to do?"

"Nothing. Sample the *Wren*." Hester took down the horn spoons and a small brass scale and the tray of numbered dishes. "Did you see the manifest? Certified in Quell."

"The Exchequer hasn't a warehouse in Quell."

"Mallory," said Hester, "would say the Exchequer has a warehouse on the moon, if Ferrers and Lisk put it on a manifest in a good hand."

Daisy snorted and went back to her ledger.

The excisemen brought up five crates under seal, one in eight, as the law required. They were good Hask pine, iron-banded, with the Ferrers and Lisk mark burned into the lids, an F and an L leaning on each other like drunk men. Inside, under sacking, the grain was packed in waxed paper bundles of five pounds each. She chose one from the top of the first crate, one from the middle of the third and one from the bottom of the fifth, which meant unpacking it, which made the excisemen sigh. She laid the three bundles on the slate and opened them.

Shelf grain was grey-white and coarse, a little damp, with a cold sulphurous smell like a cellar step. It came out of open pits in the first few fathoms, and it kept a herring for a season.

This grain was white the way the White was white in high summer, so white it had a little blue in it: large clean-edged cubes, some as big as her thumbnail, perfectly dry. And it was warm.

Hester had put her hand flat on the open bundle to feel its weight, as she always did. She took her hand away and looked at it. Then she put it back.

Warm. Not hot: the warmth of a stone wall in the evening after a sunny day. Grain was cold, always, and the deeper it came up from, the colder. Shelf was cold, Middling colder, and Deep so cold that the Readers who handled it wore gloves.

This was warm.

"Daisy."

"Mm."

"Come and put your hand on this."

Daisy came. She put her hand on it, flat, and her eyebrows went up into her cap.

"It's been in the sun," she said. "On deck."

"It's been in a crate, in a hold, under sacking, for three days on the White. And it's the third bell of a Russet morning." She took up a pair of fine brass tweezers, lifted out one of the large crystals and held it to the north light.

It was clear, a cube with slightly bevelled edges, the kind you got only from deep grain. And inside it, near one corner, there was a bead.

At first she thought it was a bubble. Inclusions were common; the old delvers called them *eyes*. But eyes in grain were always still, sealed in the crystal long ago. This bead moved. As she turned the crystal in the tweezers it slid along the inner face toward the lowest point, slow as honey. When she turned it back it slid back, and settled, and trembled.

Liquid. Liquid water, in grain, moving.

Hester set the crystal down gently in a dish and stood with her hands flat on the bench.

"Hes?" said Daisy.

"Get me the reference jars," Hester said. "The Deep. All of them. And the—" She thought. "The old cupboard. The bottom shelf. The ones Mr. Thorne put up."

"Those are Halcyon samples. We're not supposed to open those without a Reader present."

"Then don't watch me open them."

* * *

It took her an hour. The excisemen, bored, went down to the quay to smoke. Mallory came up once, saw her face, and went down again. Daisy did not go anywhere.

Hester did it properly, because if she was right she was going to need to be believed. She weighed. She crumbled. She dissolved a measured pinch of the warm grain in fresh brine, and it went down slowly, sullenly, as though reluctant to let go of being salt. She did the same with the reference jars of Deep at forty fathoms and eighty and a hundred and forty, sealed in wax by the Halcyon in 861. The Ferrers grain dissolved slower than any of them.

Then she tasted.

She tasted the Shelf first, to clear her mouth: a feather on each eyelid. Then the Middling. Then the Deep at forty, at eighty, at a hundred and forty, each sweeter and heavier, until the coins on her eyelids were gold sovereigns and she had to sit down and wait for her eyes to stop watering. It was the deepest grain she had ever had in her mouth, and only a dozen times in her life; the Brineries took nearly all of it, to keep people.

She rinsed her mouth three times. She took a single grain of the warm white salt from the *Constant Wren*, no larger than a sesame seed, on the tip of a silver spoon, and put it on her tongue.

The weight came down behind her eyes like a door closing.

It was nothing like the Deep at a hundred and forty. It was heavier, so heavy that for an instant she could see only white, and in the white something enormous pressing, pressing, on the far side of her face. The sweetness was so deep it was nearly bitter. She knew, as surely as she had known anything on that stool, that this salt had not come from a hundred and forty fathoms, or from three hundred. It came from a depth only one institution on the Rim was licensed to dig to, and the Charter said, in so many words, that grain from that depth could not be sold.

And under the weight, under the sweetness, the other thing. Bright and metallic and wet. A bitten tongue. A startle. The taste of the bead in Tib's casing, the taste of something going somewhere.

Quick.

She sat with her eyes shut and the salt dissolving on her tongue and her hands gripping the edge of the slate. Then, because she did not know when she would have the chance again, she did the thing she was not, strictly, licensed to do.

She went into the hold.

Nobody had taught her. She had found it out at fourteen, on this same stool, with a pinch of Middling on her tongue: that if she went still enough, still as a cat before it jumps, the taste opened. Breath slow. Heart slow. Hands, shoulders, the muscles of her face. The whole of her settling round the grain like silt in a glass, until she was nearly as still as it was. Then the salt, which had been only a taste, became a place.

Readers called it *reading*. In the Customs House it was called nothing, because the Customs House did not officially know that its assayer could do it.

She went still. The office went away.

For a moment there was only the white, and the weight. Then there was a sound.

A roar. Everyone said that, the few who could read deep grain and the many who said they could: old grain from the White held a roar, like a shell held to the ear. The Readers said it was the noise of the Ebb, the great crust forming, caught in the salt as it set. Hester had heard it in the reference Deep, faintly, like wind under a door. Now it filled her: a vast low rushing with a shape to it she could not grasp, a rise and a fall, a rise and a fall, and the rise coming again before the fall had finished.

And over it, inside it, something else.

A cry. High and falling. Two notes and then a third that cracked. A sound like a gate hinge, or a child calling a long way off, or a cat, though it was not a cat. It came once and then again, closer, and then a third time, and something in the back of her neck went cold. She did not know it. She had grown up on the Rim and lived thirty-three years in Ambry and she did not know what it was. It was a living sound. She was sure of that. Something in that salt had been alive, and crying out, at the moment the salt had set.

She came out of the hold the way you come up from deep water, too fast, with a sound in her own throat. She was on the stool. Daisy had both hands on her shoulders.

"Hes. *Hester.*"

"I'm all right."

"You went—you went all still. Like a—like one of *them*." Daisy's round face was blotched. "Your lips went blue."

"I'm all right." Her hands were cold. Her mouth was dry. She found the water jug without looking and drank from it, the whole thing, and her throat was still dry. "Daisy, write this down. Write it in the ledger. In ink."

"Write what?"

"That the *Constant Wren* cargo, Ferrers and Lisk, manifested as Shelf grain for curing, is not Shelf. It is not Middling. It is not Deep." Her voice came out steady. That was good. She would need it steady. "It's Hush grain."

Daisy stared at her.

"And it's quickened."

* * *

There was a scene, of course. Hester had known there would be a scene.

Comptroller Mallory came up the stairs at a speed he would not have attempted for his own house on fire. He stood in the door with his wig askew and told her in a hoarse whisper what she could not do. She could not seize an Exchequer contractor's cargo on the basis of a *taste*. She could not write *Hush grain* in the Customs House ledger, because no such category legally existed in trade. She could not have Ferrers and Lisk's crates nailed shut and carried into the bond-shed and sealed with the blue seal of the Free City of Ambry, which was what she had already had the excisemen do while he was coming up the stairs.

"It's the Exchequer's own contractor," he said. "Ferrers and Lisk. *Ferrers.* Do you know whose name that is, Vail? Do you know whose cousin—"

"I know whose name it is, sir."

"Then you'll release it."

"I'll not."

The room went quiet. Down on the quay someone was shouting at a winch.

"I'll not release it," Hester said. She had not decided to stand, but she was standing, and she was taller than Mallory, as she was taller than most men, and she let him see it. "Article Nine. No declaration of Hush grain shall be received, there being none lawfully for sale. If there's Hush grain on the *Wren*, then there's grain on the *Wren* that's been unlawfully sold. The Customs House is required to seize it and to notify the Halcyon under the Charter. And there is. And I have. Daisy has it in the ledger."

"In ink," said Daisy, who had gone pink and stubborn.

"And under Article Twelve," Hester went on, "the Halcyon is required to send its Inspector of Trade Grain to confirm or overturn the seizure within the day. If he overturns it, sir, I'll carry the crates down to the quay on my own back and apologise to Mr. Ferrers in writing. If he doesn't—"

She stopped short of *then you'll want to have been on the right side of the ledger*. Mallory had a daughter at the Glassmarket school, and he was not a wicked man. He was a soft man in a hard place, and he had looked, for a moment, frightened, and she did not think it was of her.

"Send for the Inspector," Mallory said at last. His voice was thick. "Send for him, then. On your head, Vail."

"Yes, sir."

He went down the stairs. Daisy let out a long breath and put her face in her hands.

"*On your head,*" she said through her fingers. "Oh, Hes. Oh, you great stubborn *mule*."

"It's Hush grain," Hester said.

"I know. I wrote it down." Daisy took her hands away. "What was it? When you went still. What did you—what's it like?"

Hester thought about the cry. Two notes, and a third that cracked.

"Loud," she said.

* * *

The Halcyon's Inspector of Trade Grain arrived at a quarter past the ninth bell, when the light was gone from the north window and Daisy had gone home to her five children and the assay room was lit by a single oil lamp that smoked. Hester had sent the runner at noon. She had begun to think that the Halcyon, like Mr. Pell, was going to turn out to be at its sister's.

She heard him before she saw him. Somebody was coming up the stairs two at a time and humming. Not a tune: the hum of a man thinking, who has forgotten that other people have ears. The door banged open and a man stood in it, out of breath, in a grey wool gown with a white cord at the shoulder and the hem of the gown spattered to the knee with salt-mud.

"Inspector," he said, "of Trade Grain. Aske. Corvin Aske. I'm sorry, I'm late, I was below when your runner came and then the lift stuck, and the Causeway's solid with ox-drays, so I sailed. Where is it?"

He was about forty-five, thin, with a long clever face and spectacles so thick that his eyes swam behind them like fish in a bowl. His hair stood up. His fingers were stained to the second knuckle with ink, black on the right hand and blue on the left, as if he wrote with both. He was cold; she could see it from the bench in the blue line of his lips and the hands tucked under his arms, as all grain-workers tucked them.

"In the bond-shed," Hester said. "Under seal. I kept back samples." She pushed the tray of numbered dishes across the slate toward him.

"You did? Good. Good." He came to the bench and bent over the dishes, bringing his face close to them, eighteen inches, twelve, six, peering. "Whose bench is this?"

"Mine."

"Yours." He looked up. Behind the spectacles his eyes went over her, her customs blue, the darned elbow, the stool. "You're the assayer."

"I am."

"You're the one who wrote *Hush*." He said it the way a man might say *you're the one who saw the comet*. "On the seizure. Your runner had a copy. *Hush grain, quickened.* In ink. I've never seen a customs house write that. Nobody's supposed to know what it *means* outside the Halcyon. What made you—no. Wait. Don't tell me." He took a small horn case from inside his gown, and from it a spoon of some white metal she did not know, finer than any on her rack. "May I?"

"It's your Charter."

"Ha. So it is." He took a grain from the third dish on the tip of the white spoon and put it on his tongue.

She had watched a good many Readers taste grain, mostly with an absent-minded grandeur, like men tasting wine at a dinner they were bored by. Aske shut his eyes. He went still, not deeply, but enough; she saw his breathing slow and the colour go out of his lips. For a count of twenty he stood like that in the lamplight, his ink-stained hands flat on the slate.

Then he opened his eyes and said, very softly, "Oh, that's *wrong*."

His hands were shaking.

"That's beautifully wrong," he said. "That's the most wrong thing I've had in my mouth in twenty years. From Quell, the manifest says? From *Quell*." He gave a short laugh that had nothing funny in it. "That didn't come from Quell. That came from under my feet. That came from—" He stopped. He looked at Hester as if he had just remembered that she was a person and not a piece of furniture. "You tasted it."

"I tasted it."

"And you knew it was Hush grain. By taste."

"By weight," Hester said. "Taste gives me the grade. Weight gives me the depth. I've never had Hush grain in my mouth before today, so I couldn't swear to it by comparison. But I've had the Deep at a hundred and forty from the Halcyon reference cupboard, and this is near twice the weight. There's nothing I know of on the open White that goes deeper than that. So it's from under your feet. And it's quick."

"*Quick.*" He was staring at her. "You said quick on the seizure, too. Where did you learn that word?"

"An assayer called Thorne. He had this stool before me." She paused. "He's dead."

"I knew Thorne. He couldn't taste quick. Nobody can taste quick, it's not a—" Aske stopped again. He brought out of his gown a flat leather case, the size of a prayer book, and laid it on the slate and opened it. Inside, in twelve wells lined with green baize, were twelve pinches of grain, each numbered in brass and nothing else. "Would you do something for me, Miss—?"

"Vail."

"Miss Vail. Would you tell me what these are?"

It was a test. She knew it was a test, and he knew she knew. She thought about the blue seal on the bond-shed door, and what it would cost her in the morning if this thin, cold, humming man went back across the White and wrote that the Ambry assayer had seized a Ferrers cargo on the strength of a taste and been wrong.

She rinsed her mouth. She took up a horn spoon.

She did all twelve. It took her the better part of the tenth bell. The Customs House around her was dark and creaking and cold, and somewhere below her the night watchman was singing to himself in the bond-shed. Aske did not speak once. He stood by the bench with his arms folded and his hands in his armpits, and watched her, and hummed under his breath.

"One," she said. "Shelf, under two fathoms, Southern Rim margin, from the taste of the sulphur. Two. Middling, about twenty. Three. Middling, thirty-five, thereabouts, and it's been dried in an oven, badly, because it's lost its sweet at the edges. Four. Deep, sixty. Five. Dead salt. Varrow rock-salt. You put that in to see if I'd guess. Six. Deep. Ninety, a hundred. Old. It's been out of the ground—" She paused, tasting. "Forty years? Fifty. Seven. Shelf. Eight. Middling. Twenty-five. Nine. Deep." That one was strange. She held it on her tongue for a long time. "This one's been kept. It's casing grain. Somebody kept something in it, and then returned it, and then used the same grain again to keep something else. It's—tired. Twice-used. I didn't know you could do that."

"You can't," said Aske, "lawfully. Go on."

"Ten. Middling, Western Rim, about fifteen. Eleven. Deep, two hundred, from the Halcyon's own pits, I'd say. It's got that sort of cleanness the Halcyon reference jars have. And twelve." She tasted twelve. She sat back. "Twelve's the same as the *Wren*."

The watchman was still singing in the bond-shed. Aske was not humming any more.

"Twelve's from the Fifth Fathom stays," he said. "Hush-grade. Pushed up from below. Quickened. I took it with my own hands two days ago, and it is not supposed to exist anywhere on the Rim except in a locked cabinet in my workroom. And you've just graded the other eleven better than half the Readers on my own Bench could." He took his spectacles off and did not wipe them. Without them his face was naked and myopic and younger. "Who taught you?"

"Nobody taught me. I watched Thorne."

"You read, too. You went into the hold to read the *Wren* grain. Didn't you?" He saw her face. "It's all right. I won't put it in my report. I'm not a Proctor. What did you hear?"

She hesitated. She had not told Daisy. She did not know why she wanted to tell this man, except that he had asked the question like a man who actually wanted the answer.

"The roar," she said. "Like everyone says. But louder. And—something in it."

"What?"

"A cry. An animal. Something alive." She heard how it sounded and set her jaw. "High, and falling. Like a hinge. I don't know what it was. I've never heard it."

Aske was quiet a moment.

"No," he said. "No, I don't suppose you have."

He put his spectacles back on. He closed the leather case. He looked round the assay room: at the cracked slate, at the dead stove, at the reference cupboard with Mr. Thorne's labels.

"How long have you been on this stool, Miss Vail?"

"Fourteen years on the stool. Twenty-one in the House."

"What do they pay you?"

She told him. He winced.

"I'm going to confirm your seizure," he said. "Tonight. In writing, in ink, under the Charter, with my seal on it, and send a copy to the Proctory and another to the Exchequer, and I'm going to enjoy that last one very much. And then I'm going to make you an offer. You'll say no, I expect. People like you generally do. I'm going to make it anyway."

"What offer?"

"I'm a Reader of the Halcyon," Aske said. "Assay Bench. Once in ten years a Reader may admit a candidate to the Collegium without fees. It's called the Reader's Privilege. I've never used mine. I've never met anyone I wanted to use it on. The Turning Intake matriculates on the sixth. That's five days. You'd have to pass the Entry Assay, but you've just passed it, more or less, on a customs bench in the dark. I'd sponsor you on my Bench. You'd learn to do properly what you've been doing by instinct, and a great deal you've never dreamed of. I think you might be the best tongue I've ever met."

Hester looked at him.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"I've a brother," she said. "In the Cold House. I pay for him. Six sols a month. The Halcyon doesn't pay its aspirants, I know that much. Fees or no fees, I'd have no wage."

"There are stipends—"

"Thank you, Reader." She stood up. She was conscious of her darned elbow and the smoking lamp, and of her whole life arranged on and around this stool like the tools in their racks. "It's kind. I'm not a child. I've a wage and a debt and a brother. I'll not throw them over for the chance of a gown."

Aske did not argue. He nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed a grade he had expected. He took a card from his gown and wrote something on the back of it in blue ink with his left hand and put it on the slate beside the dishes.

"My lodging," he said. "Harker's the bookbinder. I'm there until the sixth." He turned to go. At the door he stopped, with his back to her, and stood a moment, and when he turned round his face had changed.

"Your brother," he said. "In the Cold House. Has his casing been weeping?"

The watchman had stopped singing. Hester stood very still.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've seen it," said Aske. "Not at the Cold House; I've never been inside the Cold House. Elsewhere. Things that are kept, weeping. And grain coming up from the Fifth that tastes like that cargo." He looked at the dishes on the slate, at the warm white salt in its waxed paper. "Something is wrong in the deep, Miss Vail. I've thought so for a year. I don't know what it is. I think—" He stopped himself, visibly. "I think it would be useful to have someone with your tongue, finding out."

He went out. She heard him going down the stairs two at a time, humming.

Hester stood in the assay room for a long time. Then she picked up the card. On the front, engraved, it said *Corvin Aske, Reader, the Halcyon*. On the back, in blue ink, in a small fast hand that sloped hard to the right, it said: *Quill St, above Harker's. Come and find out why.*

She put it in her purse, with the four flecks that were all that was left in it, and blew out the lamp.
