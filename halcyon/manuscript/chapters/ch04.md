# Chapter 4

## Below the Line

> Step over, and be below the sea.
>
> — *Formula of Matriculation, the Halcyon*

The Causeway began at Eastgate, where Ambry stopped pretending to be a city on a shore. It ran two miles straight out across the White on a bank of fitted stone and packed salt, wide enough for two ox-drays to pass if both drivers were polite. At this hour there were no drays. There was Hester, with a carpetbag in one hand and her mother's coat over her arm, and the White on either side of her, and nothing else.

The kept-light posts along the Causeway were still lit. They stood every forty yards, and in each glass bulb a little of last summer's daylight was spending itself, slow and gold. When she passed close under one she felt it on her cheek, faint, like the side of a face turned toward a window. Ambry could not afford kept light in its own streets. The Halcyon lit two miles of empty road with it. Lowside called this an outrage, and so it was, and Hester walked under the posts with the summer on her face and was not as outraged as she had meant to be.

Then the light changed.

It came up ahead of her, so that her shadow ran back along the Causeway toward the city, long and thin, and then the whole White went pink. Not the pans along the margin, which were coloured with weed and shrimp and had been pink all her life. All of it. From the Causeway's edge to the edge of the world the plain took the low sun and turned the colour of the inside of a shell. The cracks between the salt tiles, invisible at noon, ran out in every direction, a net of fine dark lines thrown over the whole plain.

And the tiles were singing. She had heard it from the sea-wall as a child, on cold nights, a faint ticking out on the White like a great many clocks in another room. Out here it was not faint. The night's cold was going out of the salt and the plates were easing against one another, a thin glassy chatter that came from everywhere at once and stopped wherever she looked. As the sun climbed it thinned and slowed, the way rain stops: here, and here, and then a long pause, and here.

At the far end of the Causeway the Halcyon took the light on its dome.

From here it was a hill, low and broad, and it had been shaped. Its western flank stood dark against the sunrise, terraced and cut with deep-set windows, and while she looked, lamps came on behind the salt-glass one after another, so that the whole face of the dome lit up row by row like a ledger being filled in. On the crown, the Lantern. As she watched, its mirrors turned, with a sound that came to her across a mile of salt as a low iron grinding, and found the sun, and flashed it down.

Hester stood in the middle of the Causeway with her carpetbag, and for a while she did not do anything at all.

* * *

There was one other person on the Causeway. Hester did not see her until she was close, because she came up from behind, from the city, walking faster, and she was in grey.

She was near sixty, short and broad, with her hands pushed deep in the pockets of a grey wool greatcoat buttoned to the chin. Her hair under a knitted cap was white, not grey, the white of a much older woman, though the face under it was not old: square, weathered, sensible, high-coloured, with lines at the eyes from squinting at things. Her boots were caked to the ankle with salt-mud. Not the dry white dust of the Sere, which was on everybody, but wet grey mud dried in crusts, the kind you got only down by the old sea-wall where the brine seeped.

She slowed alongside and looked Hester up and down: the carpetbag, the coat, the customs blue.

"You're early," she said. "Gate's not open till first bell."

"I know."

"Then you're keen, or you couldn't sleep." The voice was Lowside. Not put on, not left over: the flat Lowside of the streets behind the Pans, the voice Hester heard every day in her own mouth. "I'd guess couldn't sleep. Tall, Lowside, customs blue. You'll be Corvin Aske's privilege."

"Hester Vail."

"Orsolya Thane. Fathoming." She took one hand out of her pocket and held it out. It was cold: not the cold of a morning walk but bone-cold, like taking hold of a railing in winter. The woman saw her notice and smiled. "Occupational. You'll have it yourself soon enough. Corvin's been crowing about you all week. Says you graded his tray blind in the dark. Says you tasted quick." The smile widened. "Well done, chick. That's the stuff."

Hester had not been called *chick* since her mother died.

"You've come from the city," she said, for something to say.

"Early business. The deep doesn't sleep, so Fathomers don't." Thane glanced back over her shoulder at Ambry, at the black line of the sea-wall and the smoke of Lowside going gold. Something crossed her face too fast to read. Then she tucked her cold hand away. "Go on in. Tell the porter at the Gate I said you could wait in the warm. He'll grumble. Let him." She started past, and stopped. "Your brother's kept, Corvin says. I'm sorry for it. I'm Lowside myself. My da was a delver. I know what a fee is."

She nodded once, briskly, and went on toward the Halcyon. Hester followed at a distance, and did not catch up.

* * *

The Gatehouse was a squat drum of grey stone at the Causeway's end, with a porter who did grumble, and let her sit by his stove. At first bell the doors opened. By the third there were a hundred and more people in the round hall with their bags and boxes and families, and the hall smelt of wet wool and anxiety and the oranges somebody's mother had brought.

They were not the children she had expected. There were a few sons of the Upper Ward, nineteen and pink, in coats that had cost a year of her wages. But most were older: a woman of forty with a baby on her hip; a bald man in a Delvers' Guild jacket with blue lips; two Quellish sisters arguing in Quellish; a Haskish giant with a red beard who had to duck under the lintel. Everyone had given something up to be here, and they stood as if the floor might be taken away.

The floor was what they were all looking at.

Set into the grey flagstones, running the full width of the hall, was a strip of brass as wide as her hand, polished so bright it hurt. At its centre was engraved the halcyon of the Halcyon's arms: a bird on a straight line. Beyond it the floor went on exactly as before. Nobody had stepped over. People walked up to it, and looked, and walked away again.

"The old waterline," said a voice at her elbow. "Before the Ebb. That's where the sea came up to, they say. Everything past it is below the sea."

The speaker was a man of about twenty-five, round-faced, with a gap between his front teeth and a slab of bread and cheese from which he had been eating steadily since before he spoke. A long grey shawl was wrapped round his shoulders, the coarse undyed kind Tider novices wore, pinned with a little brass wave like Agathe's.

"You're Tider," Hester said.

"I was. I am. It depends who's asking and how drunk I am." He swallowed. "Teodor Anselm. Teo. Nine years at Tidewater Abbey, which is to say I can sing the Vigil Office in four modes and have never once been allowed second helpings. I'm making up for it." He held out the bread. "You look as if you walked here."

"From Lowside."

"Ah. Then you'll be the customs woman. I heard about you at the porter's stove. Graded twelve blind for a Reader in the middle of the night. They're laying odds on how fast Crisp fails you." He beamed. "Two to one against inside the hour. I put a fleck on you. Don't let me down. It's my last fleck."

* * *

The Entry Assay was held in a room off the hall, one candidate at a time.

Three Readers sat behind a table. One was a sleepy old man who never opened his eyes. One was a florid gentleman with a gold cord and rings on every finger, who smiled at her as if she were a cake. The third was a woman of about sixty with grey hair drawn back tight enough to hurt and a blue cord at her shoulder. Her mouth looked as though it had been ruled with a straight-edge. The card in front of her said *Reader M. Crisp. Keeping.*

On the table were twelve covered dishes and a basin of brine.

"Grade the grains," said Reader Crisp. "Then tell us which of the objects in the room is kept. Then enter the hold and keep a drop of that brine. Begin."

The grains were easier than Aske's tray. Hester did them fast and aloud, and after the fourth the florid gentleman was making little murmuring noises of pleasure. Crisp wrote on a card and made no noise at all.

"The objects."

There were a dozen: a sprig of lavender under a glass bell, a pewter inkstand, a candle, a pear on a plate, a book, a clock, the Readers' own water-jug and glasses. Hester went and stood beside each in turn and breathed in. At the pear she stopped.

"Not that," said the florid gentleman, delighted. "Everyone picks the pear."

Hester did not pick the pear. It smelt of ripeness and nothing else. She moved on to the jug, and to the third glass beside it, the one nobody had drunk from, with a little water in the bottom. When she leaned close there was sweetness at the back of her tongue and a feather's weight on her eyelids. Shelf grain. Then she saw it: the water stood a hair higher on one side of the glass than the other. It had been kept in the act of being poured.

"The water in that glass," she said.

The florid gentleman began to laugh, helplessly, into his ringed hand. The sleeping man opened one eye. Crisp wrote.

"The hold," she said.

Hester had gone into the hold perhaps a thousand times, but only to read. She had never put her stillness into anything. She took a pipette of brine and let one drop fall, and as it fell she went down, all the way, as she had on the customs stool with the *Wren* grain on her tongue. She did not know what else to do, so she pushed: pushed the stillness out of herself and at the drop, as you put your shoulder to a door.

The drop stopped a quarter-inch below the pipette's mouth, a clear bead the morning light came through. It did not fall.

Her hands went cold to the wrist. There was a grey edge to the room and a roaring in her ears, and when it cleared the clock on the wall had moved a full minute, and the florid gentleman was on his feet.

"Sit down, Bassano," said Crisp, without looking at him. She touched the drop with a glass rod. It came away hard: a bead of salt-glass, round as a pearl, with water locked in it. She held it to the light.

"Crude," she said. "Deep. Square. You lagged a minute on a drop of brine. That's absurd." She laid the bead on her card. "Pass. Next."

* * *

Teo won his fleck, and was still telling her how pleased he was when the Proctor at the inner doors called the intake to order. The hundred and ten who had passed shuffled into a line along the brass, and the porter swung the far doors open.

There was no speech. There was only the Proctor, a heavy grey man with a list, calling names, and at each name a person stepping over the Line onto the stones beyond and being handed a folded grey gown. Some stepped over solemnly. Some hopped. The Haskish giant took it in one stride and looked back at it, puzzled, as if he had expected it to feel like something. The woman with the baby passed the baby to her husband, stepped over, turned, and took it back across, so that the baby was below the sea too.

"Anselm, Teodor."

Teo stepped over as he might have stepped into a church. On the far side he touched the brass wave at his throat and laughed at himself.

"Ferrers, Lucan."

One of the young men from the far wall went over. He was tall and fair and very straight, with a long elegant face and the expression of a man asked to attend something slightly beneath him. He took his gown as if it might be catching. When he turned, his eye passed along the line of waiting candidates and stopped on Hester's customs blue, and went faintly amused.

"I see Customs sends its sweepers now," he remarked, to nobody, not quite loud enough to be rude and not quite quiet enough not to be heard.

"Sweepers and assayers," said Hester. "Somebody has to check the weights. Your father's especially."

Somebody along the Line choked. Lucan Ferrers looked at her for a long, level moment with no expression at all, and turned away.

"Vail, Hester."

She stepped over. It was a strip of brass. It felt like nothing. She took her gown.

* * *

"Well, that was a good start," said a dry voice, as the intake was herded through the far doors. "Below the sea for a count of twenty and you've already insulted the Lord Exchequer's only son."

The woman who had fallen into step beside her was about thirty, small and quick, with a pointed face and dark hair cut square at the jaw. She wore the grey gown already, with a gold cord, and wore it well; she had altered the shoulders. She had a flat, fast Quellish accent and the air of someone pricing everything she passed, Hester included.

"Saskia Orme. Lumen. Bassano's Bench. Thaw Intake, so I've been here half a year longer than you and know where the privies are. Bassano likes to inflict me on people." She glanced at the carpetbag, the darned elbow. "Aske's charity case."

"Aske's privilege."

"Same thing, said nicely." Saskia did not smile. "Keep up. It's nine hundred steps to the Fifth and I'm not carrying anyone."

The tour went down.

That was the whole of it: down. The far doors gave onto a gallery cut in the salt, and the gallery gave onto the Stair, and Hester stood at its rail and looked.

The Stair was a spiral wide enough for four abreast, winding round a central well so deep that its bottom was lost in a soft brown dusk. The walls were salt, polished by nine hundred years of hands to the look of old ivory, and where lamps hung the light went into them a little way, so that they glowed pink near the brackets and paled to cream between. Down the centre of the well, from far above to far below, fell the Lantern's light, sent down from the mirrors through a shaft in the dome, straight and gold and solid as a rope. Salt motes turned in it.

On the walls, all the way down as far as she could see, there were names. Thousands of them, close-written in chalk in a hundred hands, the newest white and sharp, the oldest faded almost to the colour of the salt.

"The Settled," said Saskia. "Everyone who's gone down and not come back up quite right. They rechalk them every year at the Silent Supper. Don't read them. People stop and read them and then they cry and then they're late for Lumen."

The First Fathom: the Benches, tiers of salt seats worn into dishes by nine centuries of scholarly behinds; the Long Table, carved from the living floor. The Second: the Brinestacks, glimpsed through a door, shelves of books going back into the dark and shelves of white tablets that caught the light. At a desk by the door an old man sat so motionless that Hester took him for a carving until she saw that he was holding a pen.

"Callow," said Saskia. "Under-Archivist. He's past eighty and mostly Settled and nobody's seen him move since the Thaw. We call him the furniture. Don't knock him over."

The Third: a long gallery of numbered doors with brass plates. "Readers' workrooms. They call them Cells, which tells you what they think of us." Kilns breathing heat through a grating; a smell of hot glass. A door marked *3-17* with no name on it, only a tongue drawn in chalk that somebody had tried and failed to rub off.

"Aske's," said Saskia. "His aspirants did that. He thinks it's funny."

* * *

The Fourth Fathom was colder, and still. The Stair here was lined not with doors but with alcoves, and in the alcoves, on plinths, behind brass rails, were keepings.

"The Quiet Rooms," said Saskia, and for the first time her voice went down a little, as voices do in churches whether their owners believe or not.

There was a single red rose, open, in a casing no bigger than a jar: *Kept 304 Y.E.* There was dew on it. Hester looked at the dew, which had been on that rose for six hundred years.

And in the largest alcove there was a thing that stopped the whole group. It was a casing the height of a tall man, rising from a plinth of black stone, and inside it, top to bottom, ran a crooked fork of light. It was white and blinding, so bright it threw shadows on the alcove walls, and it did not flicker. It was a lightning bolt. Somebody, two hundred years ago, had caught one out on the White as it struck, and held it.

*Kept 712 Y.E. Reader Abelard Fenn and seven of the Choir. Of whom four Settled in the keeping.*

Teo had his hand on the brass wave at his throat. The Haskish giant took off his cap.

"Down," said Saskia, after a while, in her ordinary voice.

They passed the Choir, a vast oval chamber with a domed roof, empty, its floor worn in rings, where the sound of their feet came back off the roof a long time after. Then a pair of tall iron-grey doors with a brass plate: *FATHOMERS ONLY. BY ORDER OF THE WARDEN OF THE LOWER FATHOMS.*

The Stair went on past them a little way and ended in a landing, and on the landing was another door, smaller, of a grey stuff that was neither wood nor stone. It had been grown, Hester thought: salt, latticed in a close diamond pattern like a pantry screen, with a brass halcyon in the middle of it. It had no handle. There was a keyhole.

"And that," said Saskia, "is the Hush. Founders' workings. Nobody goes in, nobody comes out, don't ask."

Hester was not listening. Something was wrong with her teeth.

Not pain: a pressure so low it was not a sound, coming up through the soles of her feet and her shinbones and settling in her jaw, like standing on a bridge while a loaded dray goes over. Her back teeth ached with it. When she put her tongue to them she could almost taste it: a sweetness, an enormous weight. Something under the floor was holding itself still with all its might.

"You feel it?" said Teo. He had gone a little pale. "I hear it. Like a man humming in another room. A very big man. In a very big room."

"The Hush hums," said Saskia. "It always has. You get used to it." She looked at the two of them, and something that was not sardonic crossed her pointed face. "I didn't, much. Come on. Up. You'll want the Lantern before the Hour."

* * *

The Lantern gallery ran round the head of the tower, two hundred feet above the dome and open to the Sere, which came howling across the White from the east with salt in its teeth. Hester held the iron rail with both hands. Around her the mirrors, each the size of a door, turned on their iron arms and threw the sun off in sheets.

To the west lay Ambry. She found Coldwater Street without trying, and the long low roof of the Cold House at the end of it, against the wall.

To the east there was nothing. The plain went out flat and blazing to a place where the light was too much to look at, and landships moved on it, far off, their sails like flakes.

"What's that?" said Teo.

He was pointing down and east. A quarter of a mile out from the foot of the dome there was something dark on the White: a circle the size of a cart-wheel, crusted round the edge with salt, with a dull iron gleam at its centre and something that might have been chain.

"The Well-Cap," said the Proctor with the list, who had brought the intake up and was leaning on the rail beside them as if he owned it. "Top of the Founders' Shaft. Runs all the way down to the Hush, so they say. Capped and chained before anyone's grandmother was born, and never opened. We walk out once a year and check the chain."

"Lowside children dare each other to go and knock on it," said Saskia. "Your lot, Vail."

Hester looked at the dark circle on the white. It was a lonely thing to put in the middle of a plain.

* * *

By dusk she had a key and a cell. R-41, in the Rookery on the dome's western flank: a room cut in the salt, two cots, two chests, a washstand, a window of salt-glass the size of a tablecloth looking out toward Ambry, and cold. Teo had asked at the Bursar's table whether he might share with her, since he knew no one and she knew no one and they had both walked here. The clerk wrote them in without looking up.

"I should tell you I snore," Hester said.

"I'll hum," said Teo. "Hymns, in my sleep, in the third mode. We'll drown each other out."

Now they sat at the Long Table among a hundred and ten new grey gowns and five hundred old ones. By custom no lamp was lit, so that the only light came from candles: a long double row of small flames down three hundred feet of salt, and the dark going up overhead into the shaft.

Somebody high above rang a bell, once.

And the light came down.

It came from the head of the shaft, where the Glaziers stood on the Lantern gallery with their jars, and it came not as a beam but as a flood, so that for a moment it seemed to fall like water, splashing gold off the edges of the shaft and spilling into the hall. Then it was simply there. Noon. Full summer noon, at dusk, in a hall cut under the ground. Hester felt it on the top of her head and the backs of her hands. All down the table faces came up into it with their eyes shut. The candles went pale and useless. Somebody laughed out loud. There was a smell in it, faint, of cut hay and hot dust, and under the gold she heard, for a breath, a lark singing high up and a long way off, on some summer noon on the Shelf when the jars were filled.

"Oh," said Teo. Just that. He had not opened his eyes.

The light lasted perhaps a quarter of the bell. Then it went amber, and faded, and the candles came back, and the hall was a hall again, with six hundred people blinking in it and somebody starting to clap.

* * *

The First Taste came after the soup.

Second-years set five bowls of white salt-glass along the table, each with a little heap of grain and a placard: *First Fathom. Second. Third. Fourth. Fifth.* Then they stood back along the walls, grinning like people who know what is about to happen to someone else. The new intake rose a bench at a time and filed past, each taking a pinch from each dish on a horn spoon.

It started at the third dish: a gasp, then a woman laughing in a high startled voice with her hand to her face. At the fourth, people stopped laughing. At the fifth, the Haskish giant, just ahead of Hester, put his pinch on his tongue and stood there while tears ran out of his eyes and into his red beard. He was not crying. His face was perfectly calm. The tears simply ran, and in the candlelight they left white lines on his cheeks as they dried to salt.

And the second-years along the walls, all of them, began to bang their hands on the salt, slowly, a long rolling thunder of hands.

The first four were Shelf, Middling and Deep, and she knew them. At the fifth dish she put a few grains on her tongue.

The weight came down. Not so heavy as the *Wren*'s. Near it. The world went white and pressed against her face, and her eyes streamed, and she let them.

Sweet. Deep. And under the sweet, faint but there, the other thing. Bright and metallic, a bitten tongue: the taste of the beads on her brother's casing, standing in their lines toward the east.

Quick.

Teo was beside her, crying like a fountain, white-streaked, laughing at himself through it. He took her arm.

"You've gone the colour of the plate."

"The Fifth," she said. She wiped the salt from her face with her sleeve. "Do they always use the same grain? Every year?"

"No idea. Why?"

She did not answer. She looked up the table to the dais, where the Readers sat in their grey gowns and coloured cords. Near the middle a white head was bent toward a neighbour: Orsolya Thane, laughing at something, her square face pink in the candlelight. Two places along sat Aske, peering down the length of the table through his thick spectacles, searching, she thought, for her. When he found her he lifted his water-glass an inch, gravely, and drank.

* * *

That night the cold came into R-41 through the salt walls as water comes into a boat, slowly and from everywhere. Hester lay in her cot under her mother's coat and every blanket she owned. Across the cell Teo was not asleep either. She could tell, because he was not humming.

"May I ask you a thing?" he said, after a long time.

"You can ask."

"Why did you come? You don't seem like someone who wanted a gown."

She did not answer that. "Why did you leave the Abbey?"

"I had questions." He said it lightly, to the ceiling. "The Order's answer was more vespers. After nine years I found I wanted to know more than I wanted to be told. Which is either the beginning of wisdom or the end of my immortal soul, and I've a bet on with myself which." A pause. "Your turn."

She lay and looked at the window. Out across the White, Ambry's lights were small and orange and few. One of them, she thought, was the plinth-lamp in the Cold House, which Agathe kept trimmed all night.

"I've a brother," she said. "He's kept. At a brinery in the city. Eleven years."

Teo did not say *I'm sorry*. She was grateful for that.

"What's his name?"

"Tobias. Tib."

"What's he like?"

Nobody had asked her that in eleven years. She lay in the dark and found she did not know how to begin; and then she found she did.

"He draws landships," she said. "On everything."
