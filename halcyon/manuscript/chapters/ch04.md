# Chapter 4

## Below the Line

> Step over, and be below the sea.
>
> — *Formula of Matriculation, the Halcyon*

The Causeway began at Eastgate, where Ambry stopped pretending to be a city on a shore. It ran straight out across the White for two miles on a bank of fitted stone and packed salt, the height of a man above the plain, wide enough for two ox-drays to pass if both drivers were polite. At this hour there were no drays. There was Hester, with a carpetbag in one hand and her mother's coat over her arm, and there was the White on either side of her, and nothing else.

The kept-light posts along the Causeway were still lit. They stood every forty yards, iron poles with a glass bulb at the top the size of a child's head, and in each bulb a little of last summer's daylight was spending itself, slow and gold. It came out warm. When she passed close under one she felt it on her cheek, faint, like the side of a face turned toward a window. Ambry could not afford kept light in its own streets. The Halcyon lit two miles of empty road with it. People in Lowside said this as if it were an outrage, and so it was, and Hester walked under the posts, one after another, and felt the summer on her face, and was not as outraged as she had meant to be.

Then the light changed.

It came up behind her first, so that her own shadow went out ahead of her along the Causeway, long and thin, and then wider, and then the whole White went pink. Not the White of the pans along the margin, which was coloured with weed and shrimp and had been pink all her life. All of it. From the Causeway's edge to the edge of the world, flat, the plain took the low sun and turned the colour of the inside of a shell. The polygonal cracks between the salt tiles, which were invisible at noon, ran out in every direction in fine dark lines, a net thrown over the whole plain, and every line had a bright edge where the light caught it.

And the tiles were singing. She had heard it from the sea-wall as a child, on cold nights, a faint ticking out on the White like a great many clocks in another room. Out here it was not faint. All around her, near and far, the night's cold was going out of the salt and the plates were easing against one another. They chimed. It was not a tune. It was the sound of a whole floor settling, a thin glassy chatter that came from everywhere at once and stopped wherever she looked. As the sun climbed it thinned and slowed, the way rain stops: here, and here, and then a long pause, and here.

At the far end of the Causeway the Halcyon took the light on its dome.

From Lowside it had been a shape on a shape, a pale hump with a tower on it. From the Causeway it was a hill, low and broad and white, and it was not white the way the plain was white. It was smooth. It had been shaped. Its flanks were terraced on the side facing her, and the terraces were cut with windows, a great many of them, set deep. In each window there was a pane of salt-glass that the sunrise turned to copper, so that the whole western face of the dome lit up row by row like a ledger being filled in. On the crown, the Lantern. Its mirrors were still. As she watched, they began to turn, slowly, with a sound that came to her across a mile of salt as a low iron grinding, and found the sun. Then they flashed it down.

Hester stopped walking. She stood in the middle of the Causeway with her carpetbag, and for a while she did not do anything at all.

* * *

There was one other person on the Causeway. Hester did not see her until she was close, because she was coming the other way, from the city, with the sunrise behind her, and she was in grey.

She was a woman of about sixty, short and broad, walking fast with her hands pushed deep in the pockets of a grey wool greatcoat. The coat was buttoned to the chin. Her hair under a knitted cap was white, not grey, white, the white of a much older woman, though her face under it was not old: a square, weathered, sensible face with high colour in the cheeks and lines at the eyes from squinting at things. Her boots were caked to the ankle with salt-mud. Not the dry white dust of the Sere, which was on everybody, but wet grey mud that had dried in crusts, the kind you got only down by the old sea-wall where the brine seeped.

She slowed when she saw Hester and looked her up and down: the carpetbag, the coat, the customs blue that was all Hester owned to wear.

"You're early," she said. "Gate's not open till first bell."

"I know."

"Then you're keen, or you couldn't sleep." The woman's voice was Lowside. Not put on, not left over. It was the flat Lowside of the streets behind the Pans, the voice Hester heard every day of her life in her own mouth. "I'd guess couldn't sleep. You've that look." She tipped her head. "Tall, Lowside, customs blue. You'll be Corvin Aske's privilege."

"Hester Vail."

"Orsolya Thane. Fathoming." She took one hand out of her pocket and held it out. Hester shook it. It was cold: not the cold of a morning walk but deep cold, bone-cold, like taking hold of a railing in winter. The woman saw her notice and smiled. "Occupational. You'll have it yourself soon enough. Corvin's been crowing about you all week. Says you graded his tray blind in the dark. Says you tasted quick." The smile went wider. "Well done, chick. That's the stuff."

Hester did not know what to say to that. She had not been called *chick* since her mother died.

"You've come from the city," she said, for something to say.

"Early business. Fathomers keep early hours. The deep doesn't sleep, so we don't." The woman, Thane, glanced back over her shoulder at Ambry, at the black line of the sea-wall and the smoke of Lowside, gold in the sunrise. Something went across her face too fast to read. Then she turned back and tucked her cold hand away. "Go on in, Hester Vail. Tell the porter at the Gate I said you could wait in the warm. He'll grumble. Let him." She started past, and then stopped. "Your brother's kept, Corvin says. I'm sorry for it. I'm Lowside myself. My da was a delver. I know what a fee is."

She nodded once, briskly, and went on along the Causeway toward the Halcyon, and Hester, after a moment, followed her at a distance, and did not catch up.

* * *

The Gatehouse was a squat drum of grey stone at the Causeway's end, older than anything else she could see, with a door in it as high as two men and a porter in it who did grumble, and let her sit by his stove. At first bell the doors opened. By the third bell there were a hundred and more people in the Gatehouse's round hall, with their bags and their boxes and their families, and the hall smelt of wet wool and anxiety and the oranges somebody's mother had brought.

They were not the children she had expected. There were a few sons of the Upper Ward, nineteen and pink, in a knot by the far wall in coats that had cost a year of her wages. But most were older: a woman of forty with a baby on her hip; a bald man in a Delvers' Guild jacket with blue lips; two Quellish sisters arguing in Quellish; a Haskish giant with a red beard who had to duck under the lintel. Everyone had given something up to be here, and they stood as if the floor might be taken away.

The floor was what they were all looking at.

It was grey flagstone, worn, and set into it, running the full width of the hall from wall to wall, was a strip of brass as wide as her hand. It was polished so bright it hurt. There were marks engraved along it at intervals, little waves, and at the centre, where the doors were, the halcyon of the Halcyon's arms: a bird on a straight line. Beyond the Line the floor went on exactly as before, the same grey stones, to a second set of doors in the far wall. Nobody had stepped over it. People had walked up to it, and looked at it, and walked away again.

"The old waterline," said a voice at her elbow. "Before the Ebb. That's where the sea came up to, here, they say. Give or take. Everything past it is below the sea."

The speaker was a man of about twenty-five, round-faced, brown-haired, with a gap between his front teeth and a slab of bread and cheese in one hand from which he had been eating steadily since before he spoke. He had a long grey shawl wrapped round his neck and shoulders, of the coarse undyed kind that Tider novices wore, pinned with a little brass wave like Agathe's.

"You're Tider," Hester said.

"I was. I am. It depends who's asking and how drunk I am." He swallowed. "Teodor Anselm. Teo. I was nine years at Tidewater Abbey, which is to say I can sing the Vigil Office in four modes and I have never once in my life been allowed second helpings. I'm making up for it." He held out the bread. "Would you like some? You look as if you walked here."

"I did."

"From where?"

"Lowside."

"Ah." He looked at her with friendly attention, the way a man looks at a map. "Then you'll be the customs woman. I heard about you at the porter's stove. Graded twelve blind for a Reader in the middle of the night. They're laying odds on how fast Crisp fails you."

"Are they."

"Two to one against inside the hour. I put a fleck on you." He beamed. "Don't let me down. It's my last fleck."

* * *

The Entry Assay was held in a room off the hall, one candidate at a time, and it was over in a quarter of the bell.

There were three Readers behind a table. One was a sleepy old man who never opened his eyes. One was a florid gentleman in a gown with a gold cord, rings on every finger, who smiled at her as if she were a cake. The third was a woman of about fifty with grey hair drawn back tight enough to hurt and a blue cord at her shoulder. Her mouth looked as though it had been ruled on with a straight-edge. The card on the table in front of her said *Reader M. Crisp. Keeping.*

There were twelve covered dishes on the table, and a basin of brine, and, under a glass bell, a single sprig of lavender.

"Grade the grains," said Reader Crisp. "Then tell us which of the objects in the room is kept. Then enter the hold and keep a drop of that brine. You may begin."

Hester graded the grains. They were easier than Aske's tray. She did it fast and aloud, and the florid gentleman began, after the fourth, to make little murmuring noises of pleasure. Reader Crisp wrote on a card and made no noise at all.

"The objects," said Crisp.

There were a dozen in the room: the lavender under its bell, a pewter inkstand, a candle, a chair, a pear on a plate, a book, a pair of spectacles, the Readers' own water-jug and glasses, a clock. Hester looked at them. Then she went and stood beside each one in turn and breathed in. When she got to the pear she stopped.

"Not that," said the florid gentleman, delighted. "Everyone picks the pear."

"No," said Hester. She did not pick the pear. She stood beside it a moment longer, because the pear had a smell of ripeness on it and nothing else, and moved on to the water-jug. The water in the jug was clear and the jug was plain blue pottery, and the third glass beside it on the tray, the one nobody had drunk from, had a little water in the bottom. When she leaned close to it there was sweetness at the back of her tongue and a weight laid on her eyelids, a fleck's weight, a feather. Shelf grain. She looked closer and saw it. The water in the glass was not quite level. It stood a hair higher on one side than the other. It had been kept in the act of being poured.

"The water in that glass," she said.

The florid gentleman stopped smiling and began, instead, to laugh, quite helplessly, into his ringed hand. The sleeping man opened one eye. Reader Crisp wrote on her card.

"The hold," she said.

Hester had never kept anything. She had gone into the hold perhaps a thousand times in her life, but only to read. She had never put her stillness into anything; she did not know how. She stood over the basin and took a pipette of brine. She let one drop fall, and as it fell she went still, the way she knew, all the way down, as she had on the customs stool with the *Wren* grain on her tongue. She did not know what else to do, so she pushed. She pushed the stillness out of herself and at the drop, the way you push a door.

The drop stopped. It hung a quarter-inch below the pipette's mouth, a perfect clear bead that the morning light came through. It did not fall.

Hester stood holding the pipette. Her hands were suddenly very cold. There was a grey edge to the room and a roaring in her ears, and when it cleared, the clock on the wall had moved. It had moved a full minute. The florid gentleman was standing up.

"Sit down, Bassano," said Reader Crisp, without looking at him. She looked at Hester. Then she looked at the drop, still hanging, and took a fine glass rod from the table and touched it. The drop came away on the rod. It was hard. It was a bead of salt-glass, round as a pearl, with water locked in it. Crisp held it to the light.

"Crude," she said. "Deep. Square. You lagged a minute on a drop of brine. That's absurd." She put the bead down on the card. "Pass. Next."

* * *

Teo won his fleck. He was absurdly pleased about it. He was still telling Hester how pleased he was when the Proctor at the inner doors called the intake to order, and the hundred and ten of them who had passed shuffled into a line along the brass, and the porter swung the far doors open.

There was no speech. Hester had thought there would be a speech. There was only the Proctor, a heavy grey man with a list, calling names, and at each name a person stepping forward over the brass Line onto the stones beyond, and being handed a folded grey gown by a porter, and standing aside. Some of them stepped over solemnly. Some hopped. The Haskish giant took it in one stride and then looked back at it, puzzled, as if he had expected it to feel like something. The woman with the baby passed the baby to her husband, stepped over, turned round, and took it back over the brass, so that the baby was below the sea too.

"Anselm, Teodor," said the Proctor.

Teo stepped over carefully, the way he might have stepped into a church. On the far side he touched the brass wave at his throat and, after a moment, laughed at himself.

"Ferrers, Lucan."

One of the young men from the far wall went over. He was tall and fair and very straight, with a long elegant face and an expression of having been asked to attend something slightly beneath him. He took his gown from the porter as if it might be catching. When he turned, his eye passed over the line of waiting candidates and stopped on Hester, on the customs blue, and something in it went faintly amused.

"I see Customs sends its sweepers now," he remarked, to nobody, not quite loud enough to be rude and not quite quiet enough not to be heard.

"Sweepers and assayers," said Hester. "Somebody has to check the weights. Your father's especially."

There was a short silence along the Line. Somebody choked. Lucan Ferrers looked at her for a long, level moment with no expression at all, and then turned away.

"Vail, Hester."

She stepped over. It was a strip of brass. It felt like nothing. She took her gown.

* * *

"Well, that was a good start," said a dry voice, as the intake was herded through the far doors. "You've been below the sea for a count of twenty and you've already insulted the Lord Exchequer's only son. I'd give you a Bench cord for it if I had one to spare."

The woman who had fallen into step beside her was about thirty, small and quick, dark, with a pointed face and dark hair cut square at the jaw. She wore the grey gown already, with a gold cord, and wore it well; she had altered the shoulders. She had a Quellish accent, flat and fast, and the air of someone mentally pricing everything she passed, including Hester.

"Saskia Orme," she said. "Lumen. Bassano's Bench. Thaw Intake, so I've been here half a year longer than you and know where the privies are. I'm told to show some of you round. Bassano likes to inflict me on people." She glanced at Hester's carpetbag, at the darned elbow. "Aske's charity case."

"Aske's privilege."

"Same thing, said nicely." Saskia did not smile. "Keep up. The Stair's nine hundred steps to the Fifth and I'm not carrying anyone."

The tour went down.

That was the whole of it: down. Hester had known, in the way you know things you have never seen, that the Halcyon went downward into its hill. She had not understood what that would mean until the Gatehouse's far doors gave onto a gallery cut in the salt and the gallery gave onto the Stair, and she stood at its rail and looked.

The Stair was a spiral, wide enough for four to walk abreast. It wound down round a central well of air so deep that the bottom of it was lost in a soft brown dusk. The walls were salt. Not salt like the White, crusted and grey, but salt worked and smoothed and polished over nine hundred years of hands until it had the look of very old ivory or of cloudy glass. Where lamps hung on brackets the light went into the walls a little way, so that the walls seemed to glow faintly from inside, pink near the lamps and paling to cream between them. And down the centre of the well, from far above to far below, fell a column of light. It was the Lantern's light, sent down from the mirrors on the crown through a great shaft in the dome, and it fell straight and gold and solid through the dark like a rope. Dust and salt motes turned in it.

On the walls of the Stair, all the way down as far as she could see, there was writing. Names. Thousands of them, in chalk, close-written, in a hundred different hands, the newest white and sharp, the oldest faded almost to the colour of the salt.

"The Settled," said Saskia, following her look. "Everyone who's gone down and not come back up quite right. They rechalk them every year at the Silent Supper. Some of them are nine hundred years old." She went on down. "Don't read them. People stop and read them and then they cry and then they're late for Lumen."

They went down. The First Fathom: the Benches, tiers of salt seats worn into shallow dishes by nine centuries of scholarly behinds; the Long Table, three hundred feet of it, carved from the living floor. The Second Fathom: the Brinestacks, glimpsed through a door, shelves of books going back into the dark and shelves of white tablets that caught the light. At a desk by the door an old man sat so motionless that Hester took him for a carved figure until she saw that he was holding a pen.

"Callow," said Saskia. "Under-Archivist. He's ninety and he's mostly Settled and he hasn't been seen to move since the Thaw. We call him the furniture. Don't knock him over."

The Third Fathom: a long gallery of doors, numbered, with brass plates. Readers' workrooms. "They call them Cells," said Saskia, "which tells you what they think of us." Kilns breathing heat through a grating. A smell of hot glass. A door marked *3-17* with no name on it, only a hand-drawn tongue in chalk which somebody had tried and failed to rub off.

"Aske's," said Saskia. "His aspirants did that. He thinks it's funny."

* * *

The Fourth Fathom was quieter. The air was colder and still. The Stair here was lined not with doors but with alcoves cut into the salt, and in the alcoves, on plinths, behind brass rails, were keepings.

"The Quiet Rooms," said Saskia, and for the first time her voice changed. It went down a little, the way people's voices go down in churches whether they believe or not.

There was a rose. A single red rose, open, in a casing no bigger than a jar, on a black plinth with a card: *Kept 304 Y.E.* It was perfect. There was dew on it. Hester stood and looked at the dew, which had been on that rose for six hundred years.

There was a sphere of salt-glass the size of a fist on a velvet cushion, and in it nothing at all that she could see. The card said *The last breath of Ysolde Marrane, First Reader. Kept 47 Y.E.*

And there was a thing in the largest alcove that made the whole group stop. It was a casing the height of a tall man, roughly shaped, rising from a plinth of black stone. Inside it, from top to bottom, ran a crooked fork of light. It was white, blinding, so bright that it threw shadows on the walls of the alcove, and it did not flicker. It did not move. It was a lightning bolt. Somebody, two hundred years ago, had caught one, out on the White, in a storm, as it struck, and had held it.

*Kept 712 Y.E.,* said the card. *Reader Abelard Fenn and seven of the Choir. Of whom four Settled in the keeping.*

Nobody said anything. Teo had put his hand on the brass wave at his throat. The Haskish giant took off his cap.

"Down," said Saskia, after a while, in her ordinary voice. "There's more."

There was the Choir, which was a vast oval chamber with a domed roof, empty, its floor worn in rings, where the Stay Choir practised. They went past it on the Stair and the sound of their feet came back off the roof a long time after. Then there was the Fifth Fathom, or rather a pair of doors with the Fifth beyond them: tall doors of iron-grey wood with a brass plate that said *FATHOMERS ONLY. BY ORDER OF THE WARDEN OF THE LOWER FATHOMS.*

The Stair went on past the doors a little way and ended in a landing, and on the landing there was another door, smaller, of a grey stuff that was not wood or stone. It had been grown, Hester thought: salt, latticed in a close diamond pattern like a pantry screen, with the halcyon set in the middle of it in brass. It had no handle. There was a keyhole.

"And that," said Saskia, "is the Hush. Below the Fifth. Founders' workings. Nobody goes in, nobody comes out, don't ask."

Hester was not listening. She had stopped on the landing, because something was wrong with her teeth.

It was not pain. It was a pressure, very low, so low it was not a sound, coming up through the soles of her feet and her shinbones and settling in her jaw. It was like standing on a bridge while a heavy dray goes over, or like the feeling in a room where a big bell has just been rung and has not quite stopped. Her back teeth ached with it. When she put her tongue to them she could almost taste it: a sweetness, an enormous weight. Something under the floor was holding itself still with all its might.

"You feel it?" said Teo, beside her. He had gone a little pale. "I hear it. Like a man humming in another room. A very big man. In a very big room."

"The Hush hums," said Saskia. "It always has. You get used to it." She looked at the two of them, and for an instant something that was not sardonic crossed her pointed face. "I didn't, much," she said. "Come on. Up. You'll want to see the Lantern before the Hour."

* * *

The Lantern gallery ran round the head of the tower, two hundred feet above the dome, open to the wind, and the wind up there was the Sere at full strength, howling across the White from the east with salt in its teeth. Hester held the iron rail with both hands. Below her and around her the mirrors turned, each the size of a door, on their great iron arms, and the sun came off them in sheets.

The White lay all round. To the west, two miles off, Ambry: the sea-wall, the smoke of Lowside, the roofs climbing up to the Upper Ward and the spires. She found Coldwater Street without trying. She found the long low roof of the Cold House at the end of it, against the wall.

To the east there was nothing. The plain went out flat and white and blazing to the edge of the sky, and the edge of the sky went white to meet it, so that there was no horizon, only a place where the light was too much to look at. Landships moved on it, far off, tiny, their sails like flakes.

"What's that?" said Teo.

He was pointing down and east. A quarter of a mile out from the foot of the dome, on the open White, there was something dark. A circle, small at this distance, the size of a cart-wheel, perhaps more. It was crusted round the edge with salt so that it was hard to see at all, but at its centre was a dull iron gleam, and something that might have been chain.

"The Well-Cap," said Saskia. "Old founders' shaft. Goes all the way down to the Hush, supposedly. They capped it in the dark ages and chained it, and nobody's opened it since. Proctors walk out once a year and check the chain." She shrugged. "Lowside children dare each other to go and knock on it. Your lot, Vail."

Hester looked at the dark circle on the white. It was, she thought, a very lonely thing to put in the middle of a plain.

* * *

The Lantern Hour came at dusk.

By then she had a key and a cell. It was R-41 in the Rookery, on the dome's western flank: a room cut in the salt, two cots, two chests, a washstand, a window of salt-glass the size of a tablecloth that looked out over the White toward Ambry, and cold. Teo had asked at the Bursar's table whether he might share with her, since he knew no one, and she knew no one, and they had both walked here. The Bursar's clerk, who had plainly heard everything, had written them in without looking up.

"I should tell you I snore," Hester said.

"I'll hum," said Teo. "Hymns, in my sleep, in the third mode. We'll drown each other out."

Now they sat side by side at the Long Table, three-quarters of the way down its length, among a hundred and ten new grey gowns and five hundred old ones. The great hall of the First Fathom was dark. The Lantern's column had gone out when the sun set, and there was no lamp lit anywhere, by custom, so that the only light came from the candles on the tables. It was very little: a long double row of small flames down three hundred feet of salt, and faces bent over them, and the dark going up overhead into the shaft.

Then somebody high above rang a bell, once.

And the light came down.

It came from the head of the shaft, two hundred feet up, where the Glaziers stood on the Lantern gallery with their jars. It came down not as a beam but as a flood, pouring, so that for a moment it seemed to fall like water, splashing gold off the edges of the shaft and spilling into the hall. And then it was simply there. Noon. Full noon, at dusk, in a hall cut under the ground. Summer noon, gold and white and warm, pouring down onto the Long Table from above; Hester felt it on the top of her head and on the backs of her hands like a sudden sun from behind a cloud. All down the table the faces came up into it with their eyes shut. Candles went pale and useless. Somebody near her laughed out loud. There was a smell in it, faint, of cut hay and hot dust. Under the gold she heard, for a breath, a lark, singing, high up, a long way off. The lark had been singing on some summer noon on the Shelf when the jars were filled, and it was singing now.

"Oh," said Teo, beside her. Just that. He had not opened his eyes.

The light lasted perhaps a quarter of the bell. Then it went thin, and gold, and amber, and faded; and the candles came back; and the hall was a hall again, with six hundred people blinking in it, and somebody starting to clap.

* * *

The First Taste came after the soup.

Five dishes were carried in by second-years and set down at intervals along the table, each one a shallow bowl of white salt-glass on a stand. Five little heaps of grain. A placard by each: *First Fathom. Second. Third. Fourth. Fifth.* The second-years stood back along the walls, grinning like people who know what is about to happen to someone else. The new intake rose, one bench at a time, and filed past the dishes. Each of them took a pinch from each dish with a horn spoon and put it on the tongue.

It began at the third dish. Hester heard it before she understood it: a little gasp, from somewhere up the table, and then another, and then a woman laughing in a high startled voice and putting her hand to her face. At the fourth dish people stopped laughing. At the fifth, the Haskish giant, who had gone just ahead of her, took his pinch and put it on his tongue and stood there. Tears began to run out of his eyes and down into his red beard. He was not crying. His face was perfectly calm. The tears simply ran, and as they ran in the candlelight they left white lines behind them on his cheeks. They were salt. They dried to salt as she watched.

And the second-years along the walls, all of them, began to bang their hands on the salt, slowly, a long rolling thunder of hands.

Hester took the first four pinches without trouble. She knew them. Shelf at the First, a feather. Middling at the Second. Deep at the Third and the Fourth, sovereigns on her eyelids. She stood at the fifth dish and took the horn spoon and put a few grains of the Fifth Fathom on her tongue.

The weight came down. Not so heavy as the *Wren*'s. Near it. The world went white and pressed against her face. Her eyes began to stream, and she let them; she did not care. She stood in the white with the grain dissolving and the thunder of hands in her ears, and she tasted it.

Sweet. Deep. And under the sweet, faint, but there, the other thing. Bright and metallic. A bitten tongue. The taste of the beads on her brother's casing, standing in their lines toward the east. The taste of a thing in motion.

Quick.

She came back to the hall with salt drying on her face. The second-years were still drumming. Teo was beside her, crying like a fountain, white-streaked, laughing at himself through it. He took her arm.

"Are you all right? You've gone the colour of the plate."

"Yes," she said. She wiped her face with her sleeve and looked at the white on the grey wool. "That one," she said. "The Fifth. Do they always use the same grain? Every year?"

"No idea," said Teo. "Why?"

She did not answer. She looked up the table toward the dais at the hall's end, where the Readers sat in their grey gowns and coloured cords. Near the middle, a white head was bent toward a neighbour: Orsolya Thane, laughing at something, her square face pink in the candlelight. Two places along sat Aske, peering through his thick spectacles down the length of the table, searching, she thought, for her. When he found her he lifted his water-glass an inch, gravely, and drank.

* * *

That night in R-41 the cold came in through the salt walls the way water comes into a boat, slowly and from everywhere. Hester lay in her cot in her mother's coat and every blanket she owned. On the other side of the cell, in the dark, Teo was not asleep either. She could tell by the fact that he was not humming.

"May I ask you a thing?" he said, after a long time.

"You can ask."

"Why did you come? You don't seem—" He thought. "You don't seem like someone who wanted a gown."

She lay and looked at the window. Out across the White, two miles off, Ambry's lights were small and orange and few. One of them, she thought, was the plinth-lamp in the Cold House, which Agathe kept trimmed all night.

"I've a brother," she said. "He's kept. At a brinery in the city. Eleven years."

Teo did not say *I'm sorry*. She was grateful for that. He was quiet for a while.

"What's his name?"

"Tobias. Tib."

"What's he like?"

Nobody had asked her that in eleven years. She lay in the dark and found she did not know how to begin; and then she found she did.

"He draws landships," she said. "On everything."
