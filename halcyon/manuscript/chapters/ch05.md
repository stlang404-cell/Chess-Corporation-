# Chapter 5

## First Principles

> What is kept is owed.
>
> — *The Halcyon Primer, Law the Fourth*

"The hold," said Reader Crisp, "is a posture. It is not a feeling. Anyone who tells you it is a feeling is selling you something, generally a book. Stand up."

Thirty-one aspirants stood up. They were on the Pans, the shallow evaporation ponds on the dome's southern shoulder, in the open air, in the Sere. The ponds were each the size of a room and a hand's depth deep, and the brine in them was the colour of rhubarb. A crust of grey salt rimmed every one, and duckboards ran between them. Each aspirant stood at a little iron stand on the duckboards with a brass pipette in a rack and a dish of live brine, mixed fresh from Middling grain that morning by a porter and carried up in a steaming kettle. Live brine went dead in a quarter of a bell if it was not worked, Crisp had told them, so they would kindly not waste it.

"Feet," said Crisp. "Under your hips. Knees soft. Not bent; soft. Shoulders down. Lower. Lower than that, Anselm, you are not a coat on a hook. Hands open. Jaw open. The jaw is where most of you keep your fear and I want it out of there. Now breathe in for six and out for nine. Nine, Ferrers, not seven. In for six. Out for nine. Again."

Hester breathed. The wind came across the Pans and lifted the brine into ripples and the ripples caught the low sun. Beside her Teo was breathing with an expression of great effort, like a man trying to remember a name. Beyond him the Haskish giant, whose name was Radik, had his eyes shut and was swaying very slightly, as if listening to music. At the end of the row Lucan Ferrers stood with his heels together and his hands folded, breathing in for six and out for nine with the bored precision of a man who has been taught to do everything correctly the first time and resents being asked twice.

"The heart follows the breath," said Crisp, walking behind them on the duckboards. "The body follows the heart. The attention follows the body. You are not *trying* to be still. Trying is motion. You are arranging yourself so that motion has nowhere to start. When you are arranged, the stillness is already there; it has always been there; you are merely uncovering it. Then you lend it. A drop. One. From the pipette, onto the brine. Keep it as it falls."

All along the row pipettes came up. Drops fell. Most of them fell into the pans with small, ordinary splashes, and the aspirants who had dropped them said various things under their breath. Teo's fell. Radik's fell. One, three stands down, a Quellish girl's, slowed in the air, visibly, like a drop of honey, and then fell anyway. Lucan Ferrers's did not fall. It hung. It hung a quarter-inch under the mouth of his pipette, clear and small and exact, and Crisp, passing behind him, glanced at it and said, "Shallow. Neat. Again," and walked on, and Lucan's jaw tightened.

Hester lifted her pipette. She breathed. She went down, the way she knew how, all the way, and the Pans and the wind and the duckboards went away and there was only the weight at the back of her eyes and the drop forming at the brass mouth, swelling, letting go.

She kept it.

It stopped in the air two inches below the pipette. It did not hang the way Lucan's had hung, small and exact; it stopped dead, and it stopped hard. In the instant before the world came back she felt the stillness go out of her and into it like coins pouring out of a purse, all at once. Then the cold hit. She came up gasping, with her hands like ice on the iron stand, and a grey fog at the edges of everything, and the knowledge that a little time had gone missing. Not a minute, this time. A few seconds. The wind had changed on the pond.

The drop hung in the air over the rhubarb-coloured brine. It was not a drop any more. It was a bead of salt-glass the size of a pea, hard and clear and slightly flattened on the bottom where it had begun to fall. Water trembled in it.

"Vail," said Crisp, behind her. "Return it."

Hester looked at it. She did not know how.

"The release," said Crisp. "We covered it in the first quarter of the bell. Were you attending?"

She had been attending. Crisp had said that the release was the reverse of the hold: that one opened the stillness and let it go, as a hand opens. She had said it in five sentences, briskly, as if it were obvious. Hester had written down all five sentences. She stood now in front of the hanging bead and opened her hands, which were stiff with cold, and tried to do with her mind what her hands were doing.

Nothing happened. The bead hung. She tried again. She went still, and found the bead, the feel of it at the back of her tongue, the small hard weight of her own stillness in it, and she tried to give it up. She found that she could not. She did not know how one gave up a thing like that. Every time she reached for it, something in her closed round it, the way a fist closes round a coin in a crowd.

"Again," said Crisp.

She tried. Down the row, Teo had kept a drop on his fourth attempt, wobbling, and returned it on his first, so that it fell into the pan with a little splash and a puff of steam. He looked at the steam with his mouth open. Saskia, two stands down, kept and returned and kept and returned like someone shuffling cards. Lucan returned his neatly, and then, glancing along the row at Hester's bead, did not smile, quite.

"Again," said Crisp.

It took a quarter of the bell. In the end Crisp leaned past her and touched the bead with a glass rod, and it came away whole, still kept, and Crisp put it in a little dish and set the dish on Hester's stand.

"You hold like a miser, Vail," she said. Not unkindly. As if reading off a scale. "Deep, square, and you'll not let go of a fleck. I've seen it before. It is generally the people who have had to hold on to everything who cannot let go of anything, and it is generally the people who have least who hold hardest." She looked at Hester with her ruled mouth. "That bead's yours. Carry it. When you can return it, you'll have learned something. Next."

* * *

"Horology," said Teo, "is the study of time."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know more than I do. At the Abbey we were told time was a gift of the Tide. I asked what it was a gift *of*, exactly, and was given extra vespers." He was eating a bread roll from the refectory as they went down the Stair to the First Fathom, scattering crumbs on the chalk names. "Reader Sorle is supposed to be a monster. The second-years say he makes people cry. Not salt crying. Actual crying. A Physic aspirant last year walked out in the middle and joined the Glaziers."

"Why does anyone take it?"

"It's required." Teo brushed his gown. "Everyone has to take Horology. It's the theory for everything else. Without Horology, says the Primer, a Keeper is a cook." He considered. "I like cooks."

The Black Bench was a small steep lecture-room off the First Fathom, its tiers so close that the aspirants in the back row had their knees in the necks of those in front. At the bottom, on a little floor no bigger than a kitchen, stood a slate on an easel and a table, and on the table two things under a cloth. Behind the table, as they came in, a man was writing on the slate with his back to the room. He did not turn round.

He was tall, stooped, and thin as a heron. His grey gown had a black cord and was worn to green at the elbows and he wore under it a black waistcoat that had been good thirty years ago. His hair was long and white and tied back with a piece of string. When the last aspirant had sat down he went on writing for some time. Then he put down the chalk, dusted his fingers one by one, and turned round.

"I am Sorle," he said. His voice was a surprise: deep, beautiful, the voice of a man who might have been an actor if he had not been something more disagreeable. "This is Horology. You are compelled to attend it, as I am compelled to deliver it, so we may at least begin from a position of mutual resentment. I have been told by your Readers that you have learned to keep a drop of brine. Congratulations. So has every washerwoman in Quell. Let us discuss what you have done." He turned to the slate. On it, in a fine clear hand, was written: *What is kept is owed.*

"The Fourth Law," he said. "You will have learned it as a jingle, a little rhyme to be chanted on the Choir Steps with a mug of halcyon tea. I am going to tell you what it means." He picked up the chalk. "When you kept your drop, you held it in its moment. The drop was falling; it had somewhere to be; it wished, insofar as a drop wishes, to arrive in the pan. You prevented it. Time, however, did not stop. The pan went on. The wind went on. The world proceeded about its business and left your drop behind. Every instant your drop stays kept, the difference between where it is and where the world has got to grows. That difference is a pressure. We call it arrears." He wrote a line of figures. "It is proportional to the mass of what you keep. To the duration. And to the *liveliness* of the thing kept: how much it was doing, how much it wanted to go on doing, at the moment you stopped it. Who can tell me what happens to the arrears when you return the drop?"

Nobody spoke.

"Ferrers," said Sorle, without looking.

Lucan, in the third tier, sat up. "It discharges, sir. As heat."

"As heat. And?"

"And sound. A small report."

"And? Come, Ferrers. You've had tutors. I knew your tutors. Gerrit Moll taught you your arrears at the age of twelve, at four sols the hour, and he was a fool, but he wasn't that much of a fool. And?"

Lucan's long face had gone faintly pink. "And motion, sir. If the keeping is large enough."

"If the keeping is large enough, the arrears discharge as motion. Large enough to do what?"

"I—to do damage, sir."

"To do damage." Sorle turned round. He looked up at Lucan in the third tier with his pale heron's eyes. "To break bones, Ferrers. To crack stone. To shatter glass at forty yards. A grown man kept ten years and returned in a hurry discharges enough arrears to blow the doors off a Brinery, which is why the Physics return them over six hours in a warm bath, and why it costs as much as it does to have one's grandmother back. You knew that. You had it from Moll. You said *damage* because you have never in your life seen any, and you thought it the polite word. There are no polite words in Horology. Sit down." Lucan had not stood up. He sat, somehow, lower. Sorle turned back to the slate. "Arrears are why a Keeper cannot simply keep everything forever and let her grandchildren sort it out. They are the bill. Time keeps the books, and she is a better clerk than any of you will ever be, and she collects. Every keeping is a lie we tell time, and time collects."

He took the cloth off the table.

Under it were two keepings, each on a little glass stand. One was a moth: a big brown moth with eyes on its wings, kept in flight in a casing the size of a hen's egg, so old that the salt had gone faintly amber. The other was a fresh casing, clear as water, round a single green leaf. Between the two stands, from casing to casing, ran a thread. It was plain white cotton, and it was wet. It had been soaked in brine.

"The Fifth Law," said Sorle. "Who has it?"

"Still runs to quick," said several voices.

"Still runs to quick. Stillness moves. It is not a property, fixed in a casing, like colour in a glass. It is a quantity, and like heat it moves from where there is more of it to where there is less, if you give it a road. A road means live brine. Continuous. Unbroken. Air will not carry it. Dry salt will not. Glass will not. Wishing will not. The moth"—he touched the amber casing with one long finger—"was kept by my late wife, in the year eight hundred and sixty-four. It has been sitting in its casing for thirty-nine years, growing stiller, as old keepings do, saturating, gathering the stillness of the life inside it and of the grain about it, until it is very still indeed. The leaf I kept this morning before breakfast. It is quick. It is hardly kept at all. And I have joined them with a thread of live brine." He stepped back. "Watch."

They watched. For a while nothing happened. The aspirants in the back rows began to shift. Someone yawned.

Then the moth's casing began to weep.

Hester saw it before anyone. She had been watching for it without knowing she was watching for it, and when the first bead came up on the amber salt she was already leaning forward with her hands flat on the back of the tier in front of her. It was a tiny bead, a pinhead, on the near side of the casing, where the thread was tied. Then another. Then a third, a little further round, and a fourth. They were not scattered. They came up in a line. A short line, a thumb's length, running across the curve of the casing toward the point where the thread was knotted. And then another line beside it, running the same way. And a third. All the beads on the moth's casing stood in lines, and all the lines pointed down and across, toward the thread, toward the leaf, toward the quick.

The room was cold. She did not feel it. She was not in the room. She was in the Cold House, in the fourth row, under the third window, looking down at beads on a casing above a boy's curled hand, running in lines toward the eastern foot of the plinth.

"The stiller keeping," said Sorle's beautiful voice, from a long way off, "gives up its stillness to the quicker, along the brine. You will observe the weeping. It is not damp. It is not age. It is the casing being drawn down, in the direction of the draw. If I left them so for a month, the moth would come back and fly, and the leaf would be the stillest thing on this table. Still runs to quick." He dropped the cloth over them both. "It is the only law of the five that has never been disputed, chiefly because it is the only one anybody can be bothered to watch. Questions."

Hester's hand went up. She did not remember deciding to put it up.

Sorle looked at it. Then at her: at the white cord on her shoulder, the darned customs blue under the grey.

"Aske's," he said. "The customs woman. What?"

"The weeping," Hester said. Her voice sounded strange. "If you saw that pattern on a casing. A kept person. Lines, like that, all one way. What would it mean?"

The room had gone quiet. Sorle looked at her for a long moment, his head a little on one side, like a heron at the edge of a pond who has seen something move.

"It would mean," he said, "that someone had run a thread."

Then he turned back to the slate, and wiped it, and wrote across it in large letters: *THE STILLNESS OF THE GRAIN.* And began, in the same beautiful, indifferent voice, to tell them where stillness came from.

He said it was an open question. He said the Conclave held that stillness was a property of the Hollow Sea's salt, formed at the Ebb under pressures no one understood, and that this was what they would be examined on, and that they would kindly write it down. He said that there was another view. He said it in a tone that made it clear the other view was his, and that he did not care what they thought of it. The other view was that grain did not *have* stillness. Grain *was* stillness: a moment, a single moment, held, and the salt was only what that moment looked like when one dug it up. He said that it was possible, on this view, that the Ostrine Sea had not gone anywhere at all. That it was merely paused.

Somebody in the back row laughed. Then several people did. A voice, not quite low enough, said, *Sorle's Folly*, and the laughing got louder.

Sorle waited until it stopped. He did not look angry. He looked, if anything, faintly pleased, as a man is pleased when a dog performs its trick.

"Yes," he said. "That is what they call it. Write that down, too. You will be examined on it, and you will be expected to refute it, and you will receive full marks for doing so." He put down the chalk. "Good morning."

* * *

The days fell into a shape.

Keeping on the Pans at first bell, in the wind, with Crisp walking behind them like a sergeant. Horology at the third. Lumen with Reader Bassano, who was the florid gentleman from the Entry Assay. He taught them in the Glaziers' kilns on the Third Fathom, among racks of jars filled with every kind of kept light: candlelight, firelight, the green light of the Weeps at dusk, a single jar of moonlight that he took down from a high shelf with both hands and did not let them touch. "Light," Bassano said, waving a jar so that his rings flashed, "wants to be spent, my darlings. It is the only thing in the world that does. Everything else you keep is sulking in its casing. Light is *waiting*." Reading with Reader Odile Fane, Teo's Reader, a small dry woman with a green cord who spoke in a whisper and made them sit for an hour at a time with their tongues on tablets of old grain, listening to the Roar.

Hester heard it, a little. A rush, a breathing, like wind at a keyhole. Teo heard it as if someone had opened a door. He sat at the reading-basin with his eyes shut and his face slack and tears running, and afterward he could not say what he had heard. "Water," he said. "And something like voices. But not. Like people talking in the next house through a wall."

The cold, which was everywhere and constant and which no amount of wool could keep out. The thirst. Grain-work dried you, the second-years said, and it did. She drank a jug of water at every meal and woke in the night with her tongue stuck to her teeth, and every morning there was a faint white rim of salt at the corners of her eyes. The Lantern Hour every dusk, which she did not stop noticing.

Teo baking saltcake in R-41 on a spirit stove he had smuggled up the Stair in his laundry. Caramel and butter and Mardens honey and flakes of plain dead Weeps salt on the top, crunching, and the cell full of the smell of it, and second-years knocking on the door at all hours of the night to buy a slice at a fleck apiece. "Commerce," said Teo, pocketing flecks. "The Abbey never taught me commerce. The Abbey was very wrong about a great many things."

And Saskia Orme. Saskia sat behind Hester in Horology and kept a running commentary in an undertone on everything Hester did wrong, which was a great deal. On the fourth day, when Hester's second kept drop sat on her stand unreturned beside her first, Saskia said, quite loudly, "Look, she's starting a collection. They'll put her in the Quiet Rooms." Half the Pans laughed.

"Better than being a jar," said Hester, without turning round. "What are you on Bassano's Bench for again? The light, or the rent?"

There was a silence. Then Saskia laughed, short and startled, as if she had not expected to.

"The rent," she said. "Always the rent. What are you on Aske's for, Vail? Charity?"

"The weights," said Hester.

* * *

On the fifteenth of Russet, a note came down to R-41 in Aske's sloping hand, black ink: *My Cell, 9th bell, if you're free. Bring your tongue.*

She went down the Stair at the ninth bell. The Third Fathom gallery was quiet at that hour, its doors shut, the lamps turned low; somewhere a kiln was breathing. The door of 3-17 stood open a crack, and light came out of it, and humming.

Aske's workroom was his Quill Street lodging over again, made larger and stranger. It was a deep cell cut in the salt, with a vaulted roof, and there were papers in it in drifts and on every wall. Shelves of jars. Tasting spoons in racks. A brine basin in a stand, a spirit stove, a cot in the corner with a heap of blankets on it where he plainly slept more often than in the Readers' Close. In a cabinet with a glass front and a brass lock there were rows of small labelled boxes. On the table under the lamp, among the paper, lay a black leather notebook open at a page covered with a cramped, quick script that was not quite writing. It looked like writing that had been made into a code by someone in a hurry, all hooks and ticks and little circles.

"Ah," said Aske, looking up. "Good. Shut the door. Sit. Have you eaten? I've a pear. Never mind. Look at this."

He unlocked the cabinet and took out a box and opened it, and from a nest of black wool inside he lifted, with a pair of tongs, a crystal.

It was the size of a walnut. Clear, sharp-cornered, a perfect cube with very slightly bevelled edges, the kind of crystal that came only from the deep. It was the same white with a little blue in it that the *Wren* grain had been. There was a bead in it, near the centre. A bead of liquid water the size of a lentil.

It was moving. Not sliding, as the bead in the *Wren* crystal had slid when she tipped it. Aske held the tongs perfectly still, and the bead in the crystal, by itself, swelled very slightly, and shrank, and swelled. Slow. Regular. Like something breathing. Like a pulse.

Hester sat down.

"From the Fifth," said Aske. "From the edge of a stay, three days ago. The Fathomers pull them out with the residue when they re-set a stay, and it goes up in sealed boxes to be disposed of. I took this one before it went." He set the crystal down on a square of black felt under the lamp. The bead went on swelling and shrinking. "Do you know what that is?"

"No."

"Nor do I. I've four of them now. They all do that. Different rates. This one's the slowest." He sat on the edge of the table, among his papers. He looked thinner than he had in Quill Street, and his eyes behind the thick lenses were red-rimmed. "The deep is quickening, Miss Vail. You know that; I told you. Fourteen pockets this summer. But that's not what's keeping me up. What's keeping me up is the stays."

"The stays."

"When a pocket quickens, the Fathomers hold it. They take the Choir down and pour stillness into it until it quiets. Stays. They're bloody hard work; they're what Settle the Choir. You'd think with fourteen pockets the Choir would be on its knees. It isn't." He took his spectacles off and pinched his nose. "I've been through the Warden's returns. The stays are holding. They're holding better than they did when there were four. They're taking less stillness from the Choir per stay, not more. The Fathomers are pleased with themselves. The Choir's had a light summer." He put the spectacles back on. "The stays are easier than they ought to be. Much easier. As if there were stillness coming into them from somewhere else."

Hester looked at the crystal breathing on its felt. She thought of a thread of wet cotton between an amber moth and a green leaf.

"I went to the Cold House," Aske said. "On the third, two days after your seizure, before you came to Quill Street. I didn't tell you then; I wanted to be sure. I told the director I was from the Halcyon and he very nearly wept on me. I counted. Fourteen weeping. All in the charity ward. I went to the Harl Street Brinery and the private house on Varenne Hill, and they've not a bead between them. It's only the charity ward." He looked at her. "And they weep in lines. Don't they? East."

"Yes."

"Sorle showed you the moth." It was not a question. "He shows every intake the moth. I sat in that room when I was twenty and saw it and thought, how pretty. I never thought about it again till this summer." He picked up his pen. He was humming again, under his breath, without a tune. He began to write in the black notebook, in the cramped hooked script, fast. "I tasted the Fifth Fathom dish at the First Taste," Hester said. "It was quick. Like his beads. Like the *Wren*."

Aske stopped writing. He did not look up.

"Yes," he said. "It would be." He sat for a moment with the pen lifted. Then he wrote three or four more marks, hard, and put the pen down. "Not tonight. I'm not ready, and I'm too tired to be careful, and I've told you I'm bad at careful. Tomorrow. Come tomorrow night at the tenth bell. I'll have it laid out for you, all of it: the readings, the returns, the Cold House, the stays. Everything I've got. I want your tongue on all of it before I take it anywhere. And then—" He stopped. "And then I think we'll go and talk to somebody. Together. I'd rather not do that alone."

"Talk to who?"

"Tomorrow," said Aske. "Tenth bell. Bring your tongue." He smiled at her, tired and crooked. "And eat something first. You'll need it."

* * *

She went back up the Stair with her hands in her sleeves. It was late, and the Stair was empty, and the column of the Lantern's shaft was dark; there was only the lamplight in the walls, pink near the brackets and paling to cream between, and the chalk names going up and up.

Near the turn below the Second Fathom someone was coming down. A tall stooped figure in grey, with white hair tied back in a string, moving slowly, one hand on the rail. Reader Sorle. He looked up as she came round the turn, and saw her, and his pale eyes went over her face, and down to the white cord at her shoulder, and back.

Neither of them said anything. She stood to the wall to let him by, as an aspirant should. He went past her and on down, toward the Third Fathom, without a word, and she heard his slow step going down and round below her for a long time after she had stopped hearing anything else.
