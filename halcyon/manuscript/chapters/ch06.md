# Chapter 6

## Kept

> A person kept beyond hope of return shall be accounted dead in law, and their estate shall pass as though they had died on the day of their keeping.
>
> — *Charter of the Grain, §41 ("the Stillborn Clause")*

She ate something first, because he had told her to. Teo watched her do it.

"You're eating like a woman going to a hanging," he said. "Is he going to hang you?"

"He's going to show me something."

"At the tenth bell. In his Cell." Teo raised his eyebrows. "At the Abbey that was what Brother Absalom said to the novices, and it never ended well."

"It's grain, Teo."

"That's what Brother Absalom said."

She left him with his saltcake and went down the Stair a little before the tenth bell. The Halcyon at that hour was a different place. The Long Table was empty and dark, the Lantern's shaft a black hole going up into nothing. The lamps on the Stair had been turned down to small blue flames, so that the walls no longer glowed but only gleamed, like the inside of a shell. Her feet were loud on the steps. Far below, she could feel the Hush in her back teeth, the low enormous hum, as if the whole hill were a bell that somebody had struck a long time ago.

The Third Fathom gallery was empty. The kiln that had been breathing the night before was cold. 3-17 was shut, and there was no light under it.

She knocked.

Nothing. She knocked again, louder, and said, "Reader Aske. It's Vail."

Nothing. She tried the handle. Locked.

The tenth bell rang while she stood there, faint and far off, from the Gatehouse; the sound came down the Stair's well and along the gallery and died. She thought he had fallen asleep. She had seen the cot in the corner with its heap of blankets, and his red-rimmed eyes. She knocked a third time, and then put her ear to the door, which was not wood but grown salt, cold as a cellar step against her cheek. No humming. No breathing. Nothing at all.

She waited. It was the deep still cold of the lower Fathoms, and she put her hands in her sleeves and leaned against the wall opposite the door, because he had said tenth bell and he had said *I'll have it laid out for you*. A man like that did not forget. A man like that might be late; he would come up the Stair two at a time, humming, with ink on his nose and an apology already half out of his mouth.

He did not come.

A little before the half she heard something on the Stair.

It was below her. The sound came up the well and through the open end of the gallery: a slow heavy rolling. Iron wheels on salt steps, *grind* and a bump, *grind* and a bump, as something was hauled up one step at a time. Someone breathing hard. A creak of wood under weight. It was coming up from the Fourth, or lower.

She went to the gallery's end and looked over the rail. The well went down into the dark. On the curve of the Stair a turn and a half beneath her there was a lantern moving, small and blue, and in its light the edge of a trolley, the flat low kind the porters used for brine casks, with something on it under a canvas. Two people, perhaps three, hauling. She could not see their faces, only grey wool and the swing of the lantern. They heaved, and the wheels bumped up a step, and they rested.

Porters, she thought. Brine casks going up to the kitchens for the morning, or residue boxes going up to be got rid of. The Halcyon did a good deal of its carrying at night.

She went back to the door of 3-17 and knocked one last time, without much hope. The wheels below went on coming up, *grind* and a bump. Then they stopped, somewhere on the turn below, as if whoever it was had stopped to rest.

She gave up a little before the half bell. She was cold to the bone and angry in the tired, flat fashion of someone who has been stood up, and she went back up the Stair rehearsing what she would say to him in the morning, and how he would look at her through his thick spectacles and apologise, and mean it, and do it again. She passed nobody. In R-41 Teo was asleep and humming in the third mode, and she got into her cot in her mother's coat and lay awake listening to him, and was not, after all, as angry as she had meant to be.

* * *

She woke to bells.

Not the first bell, one stroke from the Gatehouse at dawn. This was a high hard clanging from somewhere below, rung fast and on and on. Doors were opening all along the Rookery terrace. Teo was sitting up with his hair on end.

"What is it?"

"I don't know." She was already pulling her gown over her shift. "Below."

The Stair was full of people in nightshirts and gowns, all going down, all asking each other what it was. On the Second Fathom someone said *a quickening*, and someone else said *no, that's the Fathom bell, this is the Proctors'*, and someone said *it's the Third*. At the Third Fathom the gallery was blocked by a crowd and two Proctors with iron-grey cords holding them back. Hester pushed. She was taller than most of them and stronger than all of them, and she did not apologise. At the front, a Proctor put his arm across her chest.

"Back, aspirant."

"I'm Aske's," she said. "His Bench. Is it Aske?"

The Proctor looked at her, and at her white cord, and lifted his arm.

The door of 3-17 stood open. The lamp inside was lit. There were two Proctors in the room, a porter in a leather apron with his face the colour of dough, a Physic in a red cord kneeling on the floor by the brine basin, and a heavy grey man with close-cropped iron hair and a soldier's back, standing with his hands behind him, looking at the thing that stood between the table and the cot.

It was Aske.

He was standing in his shirtsleeves in the middle of the floor with his right arm half-reached out in front of him, the fingers open, as though he had been reaching for something or someone a little beyond his grasp. His other hand was half-closed at his side. His head was a little forward. His mouth was open as a man's mouth is open in the middle of a word, the lips parted, the tongue just visible behind the teeth. His spectacles were on his nose and, behind them, his eyes were open and fixed on nothing. There was ink on his fingers, black on the right hand and blue on the left.

He was kept. A casing of salt held him from the soles of his feet to the crown of his head, a close clear skin a finger thick, so clear that the lamplight went straight through it and lay on his face. It fitted him as water fits whatever you drop into it. At his feet its edge was broken short, not quite meeting the floor, and it closed over his head in a rounded cap, his hair standing up inside it as it always stood up.

Hester stood in the doorway. She was aware of the porter being sick, quietly, in a bucket in the corner. She was aware of the heavy grey man turning round to look at her. She was not aware of much else, for a while.

* * *

"And you are?" said the grey man.

"Vail. Hester Vail. I'm on his Bench." She did not take her eyes off the casing. "His privilege."

"Ah." His voice was flat and clipped, a parade-ground voice worn down by years of indoors. "The customs assayer. Chief Proctor Gage." He did not offer his hand. "You'd better come in, Miss Vail, since you're here, and stand there, and touch nothing."

She stood where he pointed, against the wall by the cabinet. The Physic glanced up at her and back down. She was a young woman with a tired face, measuring something in the brine basin with a dipstick.

"Porter found him at first bell," Gage said, to the room; he was a man who reported. "Came to bring Reader Aske's water. Door unlocked. Reader as you see. Brine basin on the floor, empty, spilt. Residue box open on the table: Hush-grade grain, two ounces short of its label. Reader Aske was known for testing his workings on his own person. There's a note in the Proctory book from three years ago when he kept his own left hand for a day to see if he could read it from inside." He looked at the casing. "It would appear he tried something larger. With Hush grain, which he oughtn't to have had, and which keeps fast and hard. And he got it wrong. That right, Doctor?"

"Hush-grade right through," said the Physic, without looking up. "Thick, too. Kept beyond return, I'd say. You'd never get the arrears out of Hush grain without killing him. Not with a bath. Not with anything."

"Then that's how it'll be written," said Gage. "Kept beyond return. Under the Clause." He looked at Hester. "You understand what that means, Miss Vail."

She understood. Everybody in Ambry understood the Stillborn Clause; the Brineries printed it on the back of the forms. A person kept beyond return was dead in law. His estate passed. His casing went to the house that kept it or, in the Halcyon, to the Quiet Rooms, to stand on a plinth with a card.

"He was expecting me," she said. "Last night. At the tenth bell."

Gage's flat grey eyes did not change. "Was he."

"He was going to show me some work. The door was locked. I knocked. Nobody answered. I waited the best part of half an hour."

"Did anybody see you?"

"I don't know. I saw no one." She remembered the lantern on the Stair. "There were porters on the Stair. Below. With a trolley."

"Porters," said Gage. "On the Stair. At night." He sounded neither interested nor surprised. "There generally are. Well. That gives us a time." He turned to one of the Proctors. "Write that the door was locked at the tenth bell and the Reader didn't answer. He'd begun, then, or he'd done it." He turned back to her. "What work?"

She hesitated. It was a short hesitation. She thought she saw him notice it.

"Assay," she said. "He was teaching me. Grain from trade."

"I see. You'll make a statement to the Proctory this afternoon. Third bell. Now stand there and keep out of the way."

* * *

She stood there. She kept out of the way. She looked.

She did not know, afterward, how long she stood against that wall while the Proctors measured and wrote and the Physic dipped her stick and the porter took his bucket away. It was not long. It felt long. And for all of it, while her face stayed still and her hands stayed in her sleeves, a part of her was at the customs bench with the north light on the slate, grading a sample. She could not stop it. She did not try.

The casing was wrong.

She had seen a thousand keepings, in the Cold House and the Quiet Rooms and on the customs bench in a hundred grades of trade grain, and she had kept two drops of brine herself on the Pans, which still sat in their dishes on her shelf in R-41, unreturned. She knew now how a keeping grew. It grew *out*, from the place where the worker's stillness went into the brine. When you kept a drop, the salt set first where your stillness touched it and spread outward from there; Crisp had shown them under a glass. A man keeping himself would keep from the skin outward. The casing would be densest and clearest against his body and roughest, a little frosted, at its outer face.

Aske's casing was the other way round. She could see it in the lamplight from where she stood. The outer surface was smooth, glassy, the smoothest part of the whole thing. Close against his shirt and his face the salt grew cloudier, a fine frost of tiny crystals pointing inward. It had set from the outside first. It had been poured over him, and had grown inward toward the body, as ice grows on a pond from the air down.

And there was a pattern in it.

She would not have seen it if the lamp had not been low and to the side. In the thickness of the salt, a hair below the surface, there was a grain. Every casing had a grain, as wood has; she knew that now, from Crisp. Her own two beads, under the glass, had a dense square grain like a chequerboard, like the tiles on the White. This one was no chequerboard but a spiral: fine curving lines turning round and round in a long slow coil that went up his body from the floor. She followed it with her eyes. It turned left, as a screw turns when you take it out. Widdershins.

She did not know what it meant. She stored it.

And it was clear.

That was the last thing. The thick close skin of Hush-grade salt over a man who was dead in law was clear as the day. Clear as Tib's. Not a breath of grey in it anywhere, not a mist, not a bloom. *The living keep clear,* Agathe had said, drawing her finger through the air, *and the dead keep grey. If one goes grey you'll know it's done.*

"He's clear," she said aloud.

Gage looked round. "What?"

"The casing. It's clear. Agathe Linden at the Cold House says the dead keep grey. That if a casing goes grey—"

"Hush grain keeps everything clear," said Gage, before she had finished. "Ask the Doctor."

"It keeps clear," said the Physic from the floor, wearily. "It's Hush. It keeps fast. It doesn't bloom like trade grain. He's kept beyond return either way, aspirant. Clear or grey, nobody's getting him out of that."

Hester said nothing. She knew nothing about Hush grain except its taste, and she had no reason to doubt them. She did not doubt them. She stored it anyway, beside the spiral.

Then the Physic stood up to speak to Gage, and the Proctors were writing at the table, and the porter had not come back. For a few breaths nobody in the room was looking at the floor by Aske's feet.

She knelt as if to retie her bootlace. The broken edge of the casing was a thin glassy lip a hand's breadth from her knee. She touched the tip of her smallest finger to it, lightly, as you might touch a sleeping animal, and put the finger to her tongue.

The weight came down like a door slamming. White. The pressure on the far side of her face. Sweet so deep it was nearly bitter: Hush, the grain from under the Fifth, that nobody might sell. And under it, faint and bright and wet, the bitten tongue. Quick.

The *Wren*'s cargo. Sample twelve. The fifth dish at the First Taste. The beads on her brother's casing.

She stood up. Her eyes were watering. She wiped them and nobody noticed, because by then several people in that room had wet eyes.

* * *

The black notebook was gone.

She noticed that next, when the room steadied. On the fifteenth it had lain open on the table under the lamp, among his papers, the cramped hooked shorthand running down the page. It was not on the table now. Nor were the papers. The table that had been drifted a foot deep in his small fast hand, black and blue, was nearly bare: the open residue box, his tasting spoons in their rack, his blotter, a slab of green felt in a leather frame, a few stray sheets, a sketch of a crystal, a list of grain grades. Nothing in the shorthand.

"His papers," she said.

"What about them?" said Gage.

"He had papers. All over the table. And a notebook. Black."

Gage looked at the table. "He kept a tidy desk for a man in his line of work," he said, after a moment. "Perhaps he tidied before his experiment. Men do, before they risk themselves." He made a note. "We'll look."

She did not believe him. She did not disbelieve him. She looked at the blotter, which nobody was looking at. Under its leather corner, pushed half out of sight, was the white edge of a sheet of paper, folded once.

The Physic said something to Gage, and he turned to answer. Hester stepped to the table to steady herself against it, which was a natural thing for a woman to do in that room. She put her hand flat on the blotter, and when she took her hand away the sheet was in her sleeve.

She had never in her life taken a thing she was not entitled to take. Not a fleck. Not a grain off the customs bench. She stood with the paper against her wrist and her heart going hard and slow, and looked at the casing. He had said *bring your tongue*. He had wanted her to see it all before he took it anywhere. He had laid it out for her, and someone had taken it away, and this was what was left.

She kept her hand in her sleeve.

* * *

Others came. Readers in gowns over their nightshirts, standing in the doorway with their faces gone grey. The Deputy Chancellor, a tall smooth man silvered at the temples, with an ochre cord, who looked at the casing a long while with no expression, spoke to Gage in an undertone, and went away. Crisp, her ruled mouth set harder than ever, who stood beside Hester without speaking and then touched her arm, once, and went.

Then Orsolya Thane came in from the gallery in her iron-grey coat, and stopped in the door, and put her hand to her mouth.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, Corvin."

She came across the room slowly. She did not touch the casing. She stood in front of it, short and square, looking up into his open face behind the salt. Her own face had gone white under its weathered colour.

"You daft man," she said quietly. "You daft, daft man. What did you go and do."

Gage looked at the floor.

After a long time Orsolya turned away and saw Hester against the wall, and something in her face softened and broke at once.

"Hester Vail," she said. She came over and put her hand on Hester's shoulder, and even through the wool of the gown it was cold, deep cold, like a railing in winter. "You came all this way for him. Sixteen days. I'm sorry, chick. I'm that sorry."

"He asked me to come last night," Hester said. She did not know why she said it to this woman. "At the tenth bell. I came. He didn't answer."

Orsolya's hand tightened on her shoulder.

"Then thank God you didn't get in," she said. "If he'd had you in there when he did this—" She shook her head. "You'll be without a Bench now. They'll tell you today, and they'll not be kind about it; the Registry never is. Come and see me when it's sunk in. I'm on the Fifth, by the Warden's Gallery; anyone'll show you. We'll find you something." She looked back at the casing, and her square face crumpled, briefly, and set again. "We look after our own down there."

* * *

The statement at the Proctory took an hour. A Proctor wrote down what she said: that she had come at the tenth bell, knocked three times, waited, seen no one, gone up before the half. That Reader Aske had been teaching her assay. That she had seen porters with a trolley on the Stair below.

Then Gage came in, and sat down across the table, and asked her again when she had arrived. And again when she had left. Whether she was certain the door had been locked and not merely stuck. Whether anyone could say where she had been between the half bell and first bell. Teo could, if he had been awake, which he had not been. Whether Reader Aske had ever made her uneasy. Whether he had made improper suggestions. Whether she had known, when she accepted his Privilege, that he had a reputation for self-experiment. Whether she had quarrelled with him.

"No," she said, to that one. "Never."

"A porter saw you at the gallery's end, a little before the half bell," said Gage. "Looking over the rail."

"I told you. I heard wheels."

"So you did." He wrote. "You'll understand I ask everyone the same questions, Miss Vail. Reader Sorle was on the Third Fathom at about the same hour; I've asked him. A young man named Ferrers was heard having words with Reader Aske in the gallery yesterday afternoon; I'll ask him. I ask. It's what I'm for." He closed his book. "It was an accident. A clever man, careless with dangerous grain, alone at night. It happens down there oftener than they put on the Stair. But I ask."

"And the papers?"

"We'll look," said Gage, and stood up.

* * *

The Registrar was a thin anxious man in an office on the First Fathom with a ledger the size of the Cold House's. He found her name in it and cleared his throat, and told her, in a voice that tried to be kind and did not know how, what she had already been told.

An aspirant of the Halcyon must be attached to a Reader's Bench. Her Reader was—he cleared his throat again—no longer able to sponsor her. The Charter permitted an aspirant whose sponsor had died, or been *otherwise lost*, to seek another Bench until the Midwinter Assay on the twenty-first of Rime. An aspirant not attached to a Bench by then could not sit the Assay, and an aspirant who did not sit the Assay was sent down.

"Sent down," she said.

"Removed. From the rolls." He made a small gesture at the ledger, as if crossing out. "The Privilege covers your fees while you seek a Bench, but only until then. I'm sorry, Miss Vail. Reader Aske never used his Privilege before. It's rather sad, really. That he chose you, and then—" He stopped. "The twenty-first of Rime."

She thanked him. She went up the Stair.

* * *

Teo was in R-41, sitting on his cot, with the spirit stove unlit and a cake of saltcake on a plate in front of him that he had not touched. He stood up when she came in. He did not say anything. He looked at her face, as he had looked at it on the first night when she mentioned Tib, and then he went and lit the stove and put the kettle on.

She sat on her cot. The window was full of the White, blazing at noon, and the Sere was throwing salt against the glass in little rattling handfuls. On the shelf over her cot sat her two beads in their dishes, the two drops of brine she had kept and could not return. Somebody had kept Aske like that. Somebody had poured brine over him and kept him, mid-word, reaching, and then gone away and left him in his own room for a porter to find with his water-jug.

Somebody. She sat with the word, and with what she had stored beside it: the spiral, the clear salt, the casing grown from the outside in, the wheels on the Stair coming up from below. Then she took the folded sheet out of her sleeve, where it had been all morning against her wrist, and unfolded it on her knee.

It was one leaf, torn from the black notebook along its stitching. It was covered on one side, in black ink and a little blue, with the cramped hooked script: ticks and loops and little circles, here and there a number, something that might have been an arrow. She turned it to the light. She could read none of it, except, at the very bottom, in the same quick hand but in plain letters, a single capital: *O.* followed by a full stop.

"I can't read it," she said.

Teo came over with two cups of halcyon tea, salt and milk. He set one down beside her and looked over her shoulder at the paper on her knee. Then he put his own cup on the floor, carefully, and crouched beside her, and took the edge of the page between his fingers and tilted it to the window.

"That's brinescript," he said. "Shorthand. The old copyists' hand; we used it at the Abbey for taking down readings from the tablets, before they faded." He looked up at her. His round face was serious. "It's been a long time. But I can read that."
