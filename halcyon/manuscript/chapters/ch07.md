# Chapter 7

## Ask O.

> Ask, and the tide shall answer.
> Ask not, and the tide shall answer still.
>
> — *Hymnal of the Order of the Returning Tide, "For the Vigil," verse four*

Cell R-41 was eleven feet by eight. Hester had paced it on her first night, from habit, and had not needed to pace it since. Two cots, one under each slope of the ceiling. A table between them, under the window, with a brine basin let into it that nobody used for brine. Two pegs. A shelf. The window was a single pane of salt-glass as thick as a thumb, set into the dome's western flank. By day it looked out over the White toward Ambry, and by night over nothing at all, because the White at night gave back no light and Ambry was only a smudge of orange on the edge of the dark.

It was the eleventh bell. Teo had lit both candles, which was extravagant. He sat on the end of his cot with Aske's sheet on his knee, a pencil behind his ear and his old grey Tider shawl round his shoulders, and he had been silent for nearly a quarter of an hour. For Teo this was something close to a medical event.

Hester sat on the other cot with her back against the salt and her hands under her arms and watched him.

"You're sure you can read it," she said.

"I'm sure I can read it. I'm not sure I can read it *quickly*." He held the sheet up to the candle. "It's Tidewater short-script. Or it started as Tidewater short. The Abbey copyists used it for brine-tablet drafts, so you could get a whole homily onto a tablet the size of a biscuit. I spent four years doing nothing else. My hand still cramps at the sight of a vowel-hook." He squinted. "But your Reader's done things to it. He's dropped half the vowel-hooks, and he's using an ink-dot for the plural, which is heresy, and—oh, look. This sign's meant to be *tide*. He's using it for *line*."

"Does it matter?"

"Only to God and the Abbot. Give me a minute." He wrote something on the back of his hand. "He's left-handed, isn't he? For this. The slope's all backward. He wrote this fast, in the left hand, and he was cold."

"How do you know he was cold?"

"Because I've written short-script with cold hands for four winters in a stone cell in Harl, and my strokes went exactly like this. See how the long downstrokes break? That's shivering."

Hester looked at the marks: small, dense, sloped hard to the left, hooks and dots and little crossed lines like the tracks of a bird that had walked through ink. She had slid the sheet off Aske's blotter into her sleeve that morning while Chief Proctor Gage was talking to the Physic. She had felt, doing it, exactly as she had once felt at nine years old, stealing a bun from the Corn Exchange: calm, sure, and sick.

She had not told the Proctor. She had not decided not to tell him. She had only not done it, all through the long cold questioning, where he asked her four times what time she had come to the door of 3-17 and four times wrote down the same answer. And then it was too late to do it without explaining why she hadn't, and by then she had begun to think she would not do it at all.

"Got him," Teo said.

He read it out slowly, his finger moving along the lines.

"*Cold House. Fourteen weeping.* Then a line. *Harl Street Brinery. Nought.* Line. *Varenne Private. Nought.* Line. *Charity only?*" He glanced up. "Question mark. *A draw?* Question mark again. Then a sign I'd read as *still*, then *runs*, then a hook that means *toward* or *into*, and then this—" he tapped it, "—*quick*. *Still runs to quick.* That's the Fifth Law, isn't it? That's what Sorle was banging on about on Thursday with his two little casings and his bit of string."

"Brine thread."

"Brine thread. Then. *Cistern.* Two question marks. *Old lines.* And then right at the bottom, on its own, underlined twice." He turned the sheet to the candle. "*Ask O.*"

Hester held out her hand. He gave her the sheet. She looked at the bird-tracks as if by looking hard enough she could taste them.

"I know the first part," she said. "He told me. On the fifteenth, in his Cell." She put her finger on the first line, where the marks meant *fourteen*. "He went to the Cold House on the third and counted. Fourteen. That's Agathe's number; she told me fourteen on the first, counting Tib. Then the other brineries. Harl Street's for shopkeepers, and Varenne's where the Upper Ward keep their grandmothers. Nought, and nought. Only the charity ward."

"Only the poor."

"Only the poor," she said. "Weeping in lines, toward the east. That's what he was going to lay out for me." She looked at the lower lines, the ones he had not told her. "This is the rest. *A draw.* *Cistern.* *Old lines.* He'd got further than he said."

"You'd better tell me the rest of it, then. The brother." Teo pulled the shawl tighter. "You've said *Tib* nine times since the Lantern Hour, and I've been good and not asked, but my goodness is a finite resource."

* * *

So she told him. Not all of it. She told him as she would have read a manifest aloud: the facts, in order, with the weights.

Tobias Vail, fourteen. Bloom fever, the autumn of eight-ninety-two. The Cold House, the charity ward, casing two hundred and eleven. Six sols a month for eleven years. Her father, who had stopped visiting. Mother Agathe, who said the living keep clear. The beads on the first of Russet, standing in their little lines.

She did not tell him what Tib had said. She came to the place in the story where it would have gone and stepped round it, and went on to the Customs House and the cargo and the cut ledger, and she did not think Teo noticed.

He listened without interrupting, which she had not known he could do. When she had finished he sat for a while with his chin on his knees.

"Eleven years," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Six sols a month for eleven years. That's—" He was counting. "Near on eight hundred sols."

"It was five, for the first two. Seven hundred and seventy-eight," Hester said. "And four flecks. I don't round."

He looked at her with an expression she could not read. "The Abbey would call that a vigil. Sitting up with someone who can't wake. They'd give you a candle for it and a line in the book." He rubbed his face. "I'm not sure what I'd call it. I'll think. Do you want saltcake? I have contraband saltcake."

"You have what?"

"I bribed a cook." He reached under his cot and brought out a thing wrapped in a handkerchief: a slab of dark caramel cake crusted with flakes of dead salt. It smelled of burnt sugar and butter, and of the kind of kitchen Hester had not stood in since her mother died. "It's a recognised remedy. For grief, shock, bad news, and Horology."

She took a piece. It was good.

"So," Teo said, with his mouth full. "*Ask O.* Who's O?"

"I don't know."

"Let's be scholarly about it. We're scholars now. I have the gown to prove it." He held up a finger. "Could be the Order. My Order. *Ask the Order.* The Tiders keep half the old charity foundations in Ambry; if anyone has the old water-board papers from before the Cistern was bricked up, it'd be some dusty abbess in a cellar." A second finger. "Could be a person. Could be a place. Could be *ocean*." He grinned. "*Ask the ocean.* That's very Tider. We'd have put it on a banner."

"There isn't an ocean."

"There isn't a lot of things. We pray to them anyway."

Hester did not smile. She was thinking.

"Orme," she said.

Teo stopped chewing.

"Saskia Orme. She's on Bassano's Bench. She runs his light samples round the Fathoms because Bassano can't be bothered with stairs; everyone's seen her with the box. Aske tested grain for anyone who asked. She'd have stood in that room."

"That's not evidence. That's a woman with legs."

"I didn't say it was evidence." But she was folding the sheet along its creases, small, smaller, as she folded a short weight into the back of the ledger to come back to. "O. Somebody he was going to ask about the Cistern, about the old lines. Somebody he thought would know. Before he—" She stopped.

"Before he did what he did," Teo said gently. "Or before what was done to him."

She looked at him.

"You think so too," she said.

"I think," Teo said, "that a man who writes *ask O.* and underlines it twice the night before he's kept was planning to be about in the morning." He brushed crumbs off the shawl. "But I was brought up to believe the sea will come back when we're worthy, so you mustn't lean too hard on my opinions."

* * *

Hester went down to the Brinestacks at the third bell of the eighteenth, between Keeping and the Lantern Hour, with Lucan Ferrers's name in her mouth like a stone.

Everybody knew about the argument. A first-year on Fane's Bench had it from a second-year on Crisp's, who had been in the corridor outside 3-17 on the afternoon of the sixteenth and heard raised voices, Aske's and a young man's, cold, well-bred. The young man had come out white about the mouth and walked off so fast he knocked a sample-tray out of a porter's hands. Everyone agreed it had been Ferrers. Everyone agreed it was interesting. None of them had said so to Chief Proctor Gage, because none of them wanted to be the one who named the Lord Exchequer's son in the Proctory.

Hester did not much care about being the one.

She found him where the porter at the Stacks desk said he would be, in one of the Reading Wells off the north gallery: small round rooms carved out of the salt like the inside of an egg, each with a stone basin of brine at its centre, a lamp, a single chair, a reading-slope, and a curtain of heavy grey wool across the door to keep in a warmth there was not. Lucan was sitting at the slope with a brinescript tablet in front of him and his hands in gloves. He was not reading it. He was looking at it.

She put the curtain back without knocking. He looked up.

"Miss Vail." He did not rise. "The Customs House. You've come to impound me."

"You argued with him."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Reader Aske. On the sixteenth, in the afternoon, in his Cell. Half the Turning Intake knows it, and the other half is on its way. You argued with him, and that night he was put in a casing, and you've said nothing to the Proctor."

Lucan Ferrers looked at her for a long moment. He had a narrow, handsome, guarded face, the kind that had been taught early not to do anything in public. His gown was the same undyed grey as everyone's, but it fitted him, as if the wool knew whose it was. He wore the ochre cord of Vey's Bench at the shoulder.

"*Was put,*" he said. "Not *put himself*."

"I said what I said."

"You did." He turned the tablet face down on the slope. It made a soft heavy sound. "Sit down, Miss Vail. No, there's only the one chair. Stand, then. You're good at it." He folded his gloved hands. "Yes. I argued with Reader Aske on the afternoon of the sixteenth. He had asked me to his Cell. I assumed it was about my Assay work; he examined Brinescript students in discernment. It was not. He told me my father's firm had been buying deep grain under false declaration. He asked me whether I knew about it, and whether I would be prepared to say so in writing."

"And what did you say?"

"I said my father buys what the Halcyon sells him." His voice did not rise. "I said that every grain of stillsalt on the Rim comes out of the ground under a Halcyon licence, is graded under a Halcyon schedule and moved under a Halcyon seal. If Ferrers and Lisk had bought something it shouldn't have, the question was who had sold it, and that question belonged to the Halcyon and not to me. And I said that if he wished to accuse my family of fraud he could do it before the Assize like a gentleman, and not ambush the son in an examination room." A muscle moved in his jaw. "It may be that I said it more loudly than that."

"And then you knocked a tray out of a porter's hands."

"I did. I apologised to the porter." He looked at her. "Ask *your* Halcyon, Miss Vail. That's what I told him, and it's what I'll tell you. You've been here twelve days. You think because you've stepped over a brass line in a floor you know which side of the ledger you're on."

That landed. She let it land and did not show it.

"The *Constant Wren*," she said.

Something happened to his face. It was small: a check, as in a horse that sees something in the road and decides not to shy at it.

"I don't know what that is."

"A three-masted freighter. Out of Quell. Chartered to Ferrers and Lisk. On the first of Russet she came into the dry harbour with forty crates of what the manifest called Shelf grain for curing. I put it on my tongue. It was Hush grain, from under your feet, and it was quickened. I seized it. That night six men with an Exchequer paper took it out of the bond-shed, and somebody cut the page out of my ledger, and in the morning I was suspended without pay. The only other man who'd tasted it was Reader Aske, and he'd offered me his Privilege." She watched his face. "And a fortnight later he's in a casing, and you're the last person anybody heard shouting at him."

Lucan's gloved hands had not moved on the slope.

"I don't know what that is," he said again, but differently.

"Then perhaps you should ask your father," Hester said. "Since you're so keen on people asking."

She let the curtain fall behind her. She had gone ten paces along the north gallery before she heard the chair scrape in the Well, and then nothing, and she did not look back.

* * *

On the nineteenth they moved Corvin Aske to the Quiet Rooms.

She would not have known, except that she came down the Stair at the second bell on her way to Horology and found the Third Fathom landing blocked by aspirants and Fellows standing about with the particular silence of people who have stopped to look at something and are ashamed of it.

Down the corridor toward 3-17 six Fathom porters in leather aprons and iron-grey armbands were working with a long low brine-cask trolley. They had the casing on it already, on its back on a bed of folded sacking, strapped, and they were easing it out through the Cell door a few inches at a time. Each time they eased it a woman's voice said, "Steady. Steady, now. Mind his hand."

It was Warden Thane. She stood at the head of the trolley in her iron-grey gown with one hand flat on the casing, over Aske's chest. She was not doing anything with the hand. It was simply there, as you would put a hand on a horse to let it know you were near.

"Left a bit, Harro. That's it. Easy."

They got it out and turned it in the corridor, slowly, the wheels grinding on the salt floor. Hester had told herself she would not look, and she looked.

He was as he had been. One arm raised, the hand open, reaching for something past the end of the trolley. His mouth open on a word. His spectacles kept on his face, slightly askew, and behind them his eyes open and swimming, not frightened but surprised. He looked like a man who had been interrupted. The casing was so clear that the lamplight went through it without bending. You saw him, and not what he was in.

*The living keep clear, my dear.*

*Hush grain keeps everything clear*, the Proctor had said. He had said it with the finality of a man repeating a thing he had been told.

"Miss Vail."

Orsolya Thane had seen her. The crowd parted a little, from habit, to let the Warden's look through, and Hester found herself in a small cleared space with everyone watching.

"Come here, chick," Orsolya said. "Come and walk with him. He'd want somebody of his to walk with him."

Hester went. She took a place at the side of the trolley, opposite the Warden, and the porters took up the straps, and they went down the corridor toward the lift at the pace of a funeral. Nobody spoke. The wheels ground. At the lift the porters stopped and conferred about the weight. Orsolya stood with her hand on the casing, and her face, which had been composed, came apart. It did not crumple; it simply ceased to be held. She looked old. She looked as tired as any woman Hester had ever seen, and Hester had seen her mother at the end.

"Always tasting things," Orsolya said quietly. Not to Hester. To the casing. "Always going where you weren't wanted, with your nose and your notebooks." She drew a long breath. "Well. You're safe now. Nobody'll trouble you now."

The lift came. It took four porters and the Warden to load him, and the cage sank a hand's breadth on its chains as the weight came on. Orsolya stepped in after. She looked back at Hester through the bars.

"You're on nobody's Bench. I know; Registrar told me. Don't you fret about that today." The cold hand came through the bars and touched Hester's wrist, lightly, and was gone. "My door's open. You know where."

The lift bell rang twice. The cage went down into the dark with a sound of brine running in the counterweight tanks, and the casing went with it, glinting, until there was only the empty shaft and the chains moving.

Hester found that her wrist was cold where the Warden had touched it. She rubbed it, and it went on being cold.

She did not go to Horology. She went down the Stair to the Fourth and along the curving corridor to the Quiet Rooms, and arrived as the porters were leaving. The door-warden, a stooped Fellow with a ring of keys, let her in for a minute because she said she had been his student, which was nearly true.

The Quiet Rooms were long and dim and full of things that had stopped. She did not look at them. She found him at the end of the second room, already on his plinth between a Reader in a gown of a cut three hundred years old and a girl kept in the act of laughing. The label was already lettered, in a neat clerk's hand: *C. ASKE, Reader. Kept 16 Russet 903. Beyond return (Ch. §41).*

She stood with him for a minute, as she had stood with Tib every month for eleven years, with her hands in her pockets.

"You said you'd show me," she told him. "You said, bring your tongue."

He reached past her for whatever it was. His spectacles were crooked.

"I brought it," Hester said.

* * *

At the Lantern Hour she saw Saskia Orme crying.

It was not much. It was the kind of crying that has been done somewhere else and mostly put away. Hester came up the Stair late, with the Hour already begun, the kept noon light pouring down the Lantern Well into the dusk of the Long Table, gold and warm and smelling faintly of hay from whatever summer day it had been pressed on. Every face at the table was turned up into it. Saskia was not at the table. She was standing in the arch at its far end, in the shadow outside the fall of the light, with her back to the wall. Her face was red and swollen about the eyes. She had a handkerchief balled in one fist, and she was looking at nothing.

Hester went toward her. They had not exchanged a civil word since Matriculation. Saskia had called her a charity case on the first morning and Hester had called Saskia a rent-collector, and both of them had been, in their way, accurate.

"You knew him," Hester said, when she was close enough not to be overheard. "Aske. You'd been to his Cell. With Bassano's samples."

Saskia's head came round. Her swollen eyes went over Hester's face in one hard, quick, Quellish look, a glazier checking a pane for bubbles.

"What?"

"I'm sorry," Hester said. It came out stiff. She was not practised at it. "If you knew him. I'd only known him a fortnight. But I'm sorry."

For an instant Saskia's face did something complicated. Then it shut, like a kiln door.

"I didn't know him," she said. "I knew his *door*. I knocked on it, and I left things on his bench, and I went away. That's all I did. That's all I ever did." Her voice cracked on the last words and she stopped it, angrily, as you stop a cup that is going to spill. "Mind your own business, Vail. You've enough of it."

She went past Hester into the corridor and was gone.

Behind Hester, at the Long Table, the light was fading as it always faded, all at once at the end, like a lamp turned down. A sigh went along the table, as it did every evening, a whole intake letting go of the same summer afternoon.

*I knew his door.* She turned it over. She did not like how it weighed.

* * *

That night, in R-41, while Teo slept with his mouth open under the slope of the ceiling, Hester took out the ledger she had brought from Tanner's Row. It was her own, a cheap quarto in marbled boards, half-filled with eleven years of household accounts. She turned to a clean page at the back.

She ruled it as she had ruled ten thousand schedules on the slate bench in the Dry Harbour: four columns, a narrow one for the number, a wide one for the description, one for the weight, one for remarks. At the head of the page she wrote, after some thought: *Declaration of Interest, 3-17, 16 Russet.*

Then she sat with the pen above the paper for a long time, while the candle went down and the night-chime began faintly, tile by tile, out on the plain.

She wrote:

*1. — L. FERRERS, aspirant (Vey). — Argued with C.A. afternoon 16th re. Ferrers & Lisk deep grain. — Weight: motive, temper, family. — Did not know the* Wren*. Or says he did not. Face moved.*

*2. — B. SORLE, Reader (Horology). — On 3rd Fathom at tenth bell, 16th (Proctory). Passed me on the Stair 15th, going down. Known enemy of half the Conclave. — Weight: opportunity. — No motive known.*

*3. — S. ORME, aspirant (Bassano). — Courier, since summer. Knew his door. Crying at the Hour. "O." — Weight: access; the initial. — Grief? Fear? Not grief.*

*4. — "O." — Somebody who knows the old lines. The Order? Somebody in Ambry? — Weight: unknown.*

*5. — E.D. / W.L.F. — Shipper's mark on the* Wren *bill. A man in grey wool at the bond-shed with a lantern and a paper. — Weight: the cargo. — Not a firm. Not a person I know.*

*6. — The Proctor. — Ruled it a self-keeping before he'd looked. "Hush grain keeps everything clear." — Weight: haste. — Who told him so?*

It was not a good schedule. Half the weights were guesses and none of the remarks would have stood up before the Customs Board. Mr. Thorne would have struck it through and told her to come back when she had something she could put on the scale.

At the bottom of the page, under the last line, she wrote one more entry, smaller, and did not number it.

*— H. VAIL. — At the door at tenth bell. Saw nothing. Heard something heavy on the Stair, coming up from below. Looked over the rail: blue lantern, grey wool, a load under canvas. Thought: porters, casks. Went up. — Weight: —*

She left the weight blank. She sat with the pen in her hand until the candle drowned in its own wax and the cell went dark. Then she lay down in her clothes, with the ledger on her chest, and listened to the White ticking out in the cold, and to Teo breathing, and to nothing at all from the dome beneath her, which was large and full and did not make a sound.
