# Chapter 8

## The Stay Choir

> Hold the line, hold the line,
> breathe it slow and breathe it fine;
> hold the line, hold the line,
> so the others can sleep.
>
> — *Work-song of the Stay Choir, refrain (traditional)*

Reader Maudelaine Crisp refused her in eight words, on the twentieth of Russet, at the edge of the rose-coloured practice pan with brine to the wrists of her rolled sleeves: "I'm full, and you're unfinished. Next pan, please." The rest of the list went the same way, more slowly. Bassano took her hands in his warm rings and wept a little for Corvin and could not; Rusk of Weathering explained that Lowside constitutions settled early, and that it was not personal; Pym of Cellaring was full with his door open eight inches. Reader Odile Fane of Reading, who had Teo, was full and sorry for it. She was a small dry woman who spoke in a whisper, and she held Hester's eyes and added, lower still: "For what it's worth, you were seen in the Quiet Rooms. Go carefully. The Halcyon is a good place to be noticed, and a bad one." Hester left Benedek Sorle till last, because she expected nothing from him, and found him alone in the Black Bench lecture room wiping slates, a stick hooked over the lectern. He heard her out without turning round. "I am told you were at Corvin's door at the tenth bell on the night he was kept," he said to the slate, "and that you have been visiting him since in the Quiet Rooms, like a widow. I do not take charity cases from the dead. Good afternoon."

* * *

The Warden's Gallery was on the Fifth Fathom, and aspirants did not walk to it.

The Stair went on below the Fourth to the tall iron-grey doors she had seen on the tour, *FATHOMERS ONLY*, with a Proctor beside them now, and beside the doors a single small brine-lift cage, its bars worn bright at the height of a hand. A Fathom porter rode down with her and looked at her once, to see whether she would be sick. The salt of the shaft walls went clearer and darker as they sank, with a grey in it like smoke in glass. The air grew colder, and then, unexpectedly, warmer, and damp, and she tasted under it, faint and far off, the bitten tongue.

"Warden's Gallery," the porter said, drawing the gate. "Straight on, miss. You'll hear her."

She heard her. Orsolya Thane was laughing, down the corridor, through an open door where lamplight lay on the floor. It was a big unguarded Lowside laugh, the laugh of the women on Tanner's Row when the washing was in and the children were somebody else's for an hour. She had not heard one since she crossed the Line.

The Warden's office was a long low room cut into the salt, and it was warm. A real iron stove stood in the corner with a kettle on it and grey wool stockings drying on the rail. Rag rugs on the floor. A dresser of chipped blue-and-white Lowside crockery. On the walls, among the charts of the Fifth Fathom's galleries in coloured inks, were pinned sheets of names and dates, and some of the names had small crosses beside them in pencil. At the big table sat the Warden of the Lower Fathoms in her iron-grey gown with the sleeves pushed up, a teacup in one hand and a pen in the other, laughing at something said by the man at her elbow with a ledger.

"Miss Vail." The laugh changed to a smile without losing any warmth. "There you are. Mr. Dunmore, this is Corvin's girl. Sit down before you fall down, chick. That lift does it to everybody the first time. It's the warm. Nobody expects the warm."

Hester sat on a kitchen chair with a cushion on it. Orsolya poured her tea from a brown pot without asking, and put salt in it without asking, and milk, and pushed it across.

The man with the ledger nodded to her. He was perhaps forty, with a pleasant forgettable face, thinning fair hair combed carefully across, and a clerk's black coat over his grey gown, its cuffs banded with ink. He said "Miss Vail" in a voice that matched the face, and turned a page, and wrote something.

"Mr. Dunmore keeps me honest," Orsolya said. "Or keeps me in order. I can never remember which." Her hands round the cup were white at the knuckles and bluish at the nails. Hester had noticed them on the Causeway, the first morning: the hands of someone who had been cold for a long time. "Now. Let's not dance. You've been round the Benches, and they've all said they're full, and Crisp said you were unfinished."

Hester looked at her.

"She says it to everyone," Orsolya said. "She said it to me, in eight-seventy. She was right then too." She set her cup down. "I can't sponsor you. I'd like to. I've six on my Bench and two of them are useless, and if I had my way I'd send the useless ones home to their mothers and take you in an afternoon. But the Conclave counts heads, and Vey counts them twice." She said the Deputy Chancellor's name as a woman in Tanner's Row says the name of the landlord. "What I can do is put you in the Choir."

"Reader Aske mentioned the Choir."

"I expect he did. He knew I was short." Her face moved, briefly, at the name, and settled. "Three sols a week, in silver, no deductions; Mr. Dunmore sees to it. Four shifts a week, two of them nights. It's hard work and it's cold work and it'll put years on you if you do it long enough, and I'll not lie to you about that, because I've never lied to a chorister in my life." She leaned forward. "But while you're in the Choir you're on the Halcyon's books, and the Registrar can't send down a working chorister without the Warden's say. And I'll not say. That'll hold you till midwinter. By midwinter something'll turn up. It generally does."

Three sols a week. Twelve a month. Tib's fee was six.

Hester did the sum and found she had done it before she had decided anything, which meant she had decided.

"Why me?" she said. "You don't know me."

"I know you." The Warden looked at her across the table, steady and amused. "You walked the Causeway at dawn on your first morning with a carpetbag in one hand and your other hand in your pocket on your purse. Only Lowside girls walk like that. I walked like it myself, to the Coldbank pithead every morning with my da's dinner in one hand and my pennies in the other, till the Coldbank seam came down on him. I was eleven." She said it plainly, a fact with the weight already taken off it, and went on. "And I hear you've got a hold like a dropped anvil. Crisp says you can't let go of a thing to save your life, but by God you can grip it. That's a chorister. That's what the Choir *is*. Gripping." She turned her cup. "And Corvin asked me. A fortnight ago, before—" She stopped. "He came down here with you in his mouth, as he came down with everything, and he said *Orsolya, I've found a tongue*. So."

Hester looked down into her tea.

"Dunmore," Orsolya said. "The forms."

He had them ready: a stipend agreement, a declaration of fitness, and a release. *The chorister acknowledges that holding in the Stays may result in lag, cold injury, and the Settle, and holds the Halcyon blameless.* She read every word, because she was a customs assayer and had never signed a thing she had not read. Then she signed. Dunmore wrote her name at the head of each in his own hand, for the file, in small, upright, plain letters. Something about the letters tugged at her, as a familiar face tugs in a crowd. She looked again and it was gone. A clerk's hand. Ten thousand clerks wrote like that.

"Your first shift's Friday," Orsolya said. "Night. Choir hall, Fourth Fathom, the ninth bell. Wear everything you own. Eat first." She drank her tea. Then, in the voice of a woman thinking aloud: "You've come in a good season, at least. The Stays have been kind this season. Kinder than they've any right to be." She shook her head. "I'll not complain. God knows we've had our share of the other kind."

At her elbow, Dunmore turned a page.

* * *

She had seen the Choir hall from the Stair on the tour, empty, its floor worn in rings. Full, it was another room.

It was oval and old, its tiers of shallow steps worn by centuries of sitting into something nearer a bowl. There were no benches. You sat on the salt. A dozen lamps of kept light hung from the ceiling on long chains, pressed on some clear midwinter noon years ago, and they gave the hall the cold lucid look of a snowfield.

There were sixty-one choristers. Hester counted. They came in by twos and threes at the ninth bell of Friday in grey gowns and iron-grey armbands, bundled in shawls and mufflers and fingerless gloves, carrying flasks, and sat down on the steps in no order she could see, and did not talk much. Most were older than her. A man of seventy with a white beard sat down on the lowest step with a slowness she had only ever seen in Mr. Thorne. They had what she had seen on choristers in Lowside taverns, which was not a look so much as a lack of fidget. When they sat down, they sat down all the way.

A woman sat down next to her: sixty-five or so, small and square, with a face as brown and creased as a walnut and a mass of white hair bound in a red scarf. She wore three shawls, one on top of another, and she held out a fourth.

"You'll want that," she said. "Not now. After. Ama Tessaly." She put out a hand in a fingerless glove. "Forty-one years I've been holding, and I've never seen a first-nighter yet who thought she'd want a shawl. And then she wants it."

"Hester Vail."

"I know. Corvin's girl. The Warden said." Ama looked at her sidelong with small bright black eyes. "Strong hold, she says. Well. That's lucky for you and unlucky for you. The strong ones she puts nearest the line."

"What does that mean?"

"Means you'll find out, lamb." Ama patted her knee. "Here she is."

Orsolya came down the steps into the middle of the floor. She did not raise her voice; the shape of the hall carried everything. She looked round the tiers as a woman looks round her kitchen table at a family she has fed for thirty years.

"Fourteen holding tonight," she said. "Same as last night. Four's warm. Nine's warm. Eleven's warmer than I'd like. We'll go down to Eleven first and give it a long hold and then do the round. Mackey, you're lead on Nine. Ama, you'll take the new girl on Eleven, by the line." Her eyes found Hester. "Don't be frightened, chick. Ama's held more stays than anybody alive. Just do what she does. Right." She lifted her chin. "Let's have the breath."

Sixty-one people breathed in.

It was not loud: sixty-one people drawing breath together over a count of perhaps eight, and letting it out over twelve, a long low *hhhhhhh* that went round the walls and came back. Again, slower. And again. On the fourth breath someone low down on the left was singing.

It was a single note held for the length of the outbreath and dropped a tone at the end. On the next breath a dozen voices took it; on the next, all of them. They were not singing to make music; Hester understood that at once. They were singing to hold sixty-one bodies to the same slow count, so that sixty-one hearts would slow together. The note was the clock. Then the words came in on the outbreath, ragged and soft:

*Hold the line, hold the line,*
*breathe it slow and breathe it fine—*

By the third time round she did not need the tune. Her breath had gone with theirs without asking her, and her heart had gone with her breath. She sat on the cold salt with Ama's shoulder against hers and felt her body slowing, as you feel a cart slowing on a hill: the whole weight of it easing.

"Good," Ama said, between lines. "Good girl. Now come on."

* * *

They went down singing.

The Choir left the hall by a low door on its eastern side and went down a passage cut in steps, in a long grey file. The salt of its walls went clear and dark, and at one turning a door of iron-grey stood open with a Proctor beside it. Beyond it the air changed.

Warm. Wet. And the taste.

It came up her throat from the back of her tongue before she had put her tongue to anything: sweet, enormously heavy, so that the passage dimmed in front of her eyes. Under the sweetness, bright as a struck match, quick. Quick as the cargo. Quick as the beads on Tib's casing. Quick as the fifth dish at the First Taste, the one that had made half the intake weep.

The Fifth Fathom.

The rest of the Halcyon had been carved. The Fifth had been dug. Its galleries were low and rough, propped here and there with timber gone white with salt, running off in every direction into the dark. Along the floor of each, against the left wall, ran a channel two hands wide, sheathed in brass and full to the brim with brine that did not move. Where the sheathing joined there were valve-wheels the size of plates, painted with white numerals. At every junction more channels came in from the side and joined, as gutters join in a street, until they ran together into a single great channel as wide as a man's body that went away along the main gallery toward the east, out of the lamplight.

"Stay-lines," Ama said, between breaths, seeing her look. "That's the eastern trunk, that big one. All the lines go into her and she goes along to the Old Tap and round. You don't need to know. You need to know your line." She pointed with her chin down a side gallery. "That's ours. Eleven."

Stay Eleven was at the end of a short dead-end gallery where the roof came down low. Most of the Choir went on with Orsolya and Mackey toward the other stays. Twelve turned with Ama into Eleven.

At the end of the gallery the salt was wet.

Not damp. Running. Water stood in shallow pools on the floor and ran down the walls in threads that glittered in the lamplight. The salt had gone soft and grey and translucent, like ice in a thaw. And it was warm; she could feel it on her face from three paces off, a hearth's warmth. In the end wall, at the height of a person's chest, the salt bulged outward in a rough dome the size of a cartwheel, laced with fine cracks and glistening wet. From the cracks came a sound, low and continuous. It was not the Choir coming back off the walls. It came from *inside* the salt.

The brass stay-line ran up the wall from the floor and ended against the bulge in a broad collar sealed with grey wax. Over the whole face of the bulge was a casing, a thick clear shell of new-grown grain like a scab over a wound, and it was weeping. Beads of brine stood out all over it, and ran.

"Hands on the line," Ama said. "Where it comes up the wall. Both hands. Flat. Don't grip, lamb, it's not a rope, it's a bath. Lay them in." She put her own hands low on the brass where the line climbed the wall, and Hester put hers above, and the others ranged out along the line on either side. The brass was cold. Through a slot at the top of the sheathing Hester's fingertips touched the brine inside. That was cold too, and still.

"Now hold," said Ama. "Give it the hold. And sing."

They sang. Hester held.

It was like the Customs House, when she had gone into the hold to read the *Wren* grain: the breath going down, the heart going down, the whole of her settling round a single point like silt in a glass. But on the stool she had held alone. Here, through the line, she could feel the others: not as people but as weight, twelve stillnesses settling with hers. And she felt the stillness *go*. It went out of her hands into the brine, up the wall into the collar, out of the collar into the casing over the bulge, as warmth goes out of your hands into a cold cup, steadily, without your doing anything. *Still runs to quick.* Sorle's bit of string. She felt the quickening at the far end of the line pull like a drain. It wanted what she had. It drank it.

And as she held, the sound from inside the salt came up through the line into her hands, and through her hands into her, and it was no longer a sound behind a wall. It was all round her.

It was the roar. She knew it from the *Wren*: the vast low rushing, rise and fall, the rise coming again before the fall was done. But on the customs stool it had been the sound in a shell. Here she was inside the shell. It had *shape*. The rises had heads that curled and broke, and the falls had a long drawn hiss after them, like a broom across a floor, like sand pulled back. There was a wind over it, a long steady push, cold, and under the wind a slapping, irregular, close, as of something hollow and wooden knocked against and knocked against. The roar came in, and broke, and drew back hissing, and came in again. It had been doing that before she was born and before Ambry was built and before there was a Halcyon, and it was doing it now, in her hands.

Far off, under all of it, so faint she could not swear afterward that she had heard it at all, a bell rang once.

She did not know she was weeping until the salt of it stung the corners of her mouth. The tears ran into the neck of her gown, and where they dried they dried white. She was not sad; she was holding, and singing, and the sea was in her hands, and she had never in her life heard anything she wanted so much to go on hearing.

*Hold the line, hold the line—*

The beads on the casing slowed, and stood, and dulled, and dried. The cracks in the bulge went white and closed. The warmth on her face cooled. The sound drew back, and back, as a tide draws off a long shore, until it was the roar in a shell again, and then a murmur, and then only the Choir's voices in the low gallery.

"Ease off," said Ama softly. "Ease it now. Lift your hands. *Slow.*"

Hester lifted her hands.

* * *

She came to on the floor of the gallery with her back against the wall and Ama's fourth shawl round her shoulders.

She did not know how she had got there. That was the worst thing. She had been lifting her hands from the brass; now she was sitting, wrapped up, and the lamps had been moved, and the brass of the line was dry where it had been wet. Ama was crouched in front of her holding a tin cup to her mouth.

"Drink," Ama said. "Don't talk. Drink."

It was water, with salt in it. She drank it and was still so thirsty that her tongue stuck to the roof of her mouth. She was cold in her bones and her teeth, and she could not stop shaking.

"How long," she said. Her lips were clumsy.

"Couple of minutes. Two, maybe three." Ama tucked the shawl tighter. "That's lag, lamb. You gave it more than you had, first time, like they all do. You'll get most of it back." She sat back on her heels. "Told you you'd want the shawl."

Hester put her hand to her face. Her cheeks were stiff with dried salt, and it came away on her fingers in white flakes.

"It was the sea," she said.

Ama looked at her.

"I heard it. In the line. There was wind, and something knocking, and the—" She did not have the words. She had never had any reason to learn them. "It was the sea."

"Aye," said Ama. "It sounds like it, doesn't it." She said it peaceably, as you would agree with a child about the shape of a cloud. "Everybody hears something their first night. Residue, the Readers call it. Noise of the Ebb, caught in the grain." She got up slowly, with a hand on the wall, all the way, as the old man with the white beard had sat down. "Forty-one years I've heard it. Some nights I could swear it was the sea too. Up you come. Round's not done. Four and Nine yet."

Hester took the hand and came up. Her legs held her, mostly. At the mouth of the gallery Orsolya Thane was standing with a lamp, watching them come out. When she saw Hester, something came into her face that Hester had seen in her mother's once, long ago, at a school prize-giving in Lowside: a thing that had nothing to do with the prize.

"There she is," Orsolya said. "There's my girl. Eleven's quiet as a church, Ama says. First night." She put her cold hand on Hester's cheek, on the dried salt. "Didn't I say? Like a dropped anvil."

Hester could not answer. Her teeth were chattering.

"Go on," the Warden said gently. "Do the round. Then up, and eat something hot, and sleep." She turned away down the main gallery, lifting her lamp. Its light ran along the great brass channel of the eastern trunk, full and still, going away east into the dark, toward wherever it went.
