# Chapter 8

## The Stay Choir

> Hold the line, hold the line,
> breathe it slow and breathe it fine;
> hold the line, hold the line,
> so the others can sleep.
>
> — *Work-song of the Stay Choir, refrain (traditional)*

Reader Maudelaine Crisp refused her in nine words, standing at the edge of the rose-coloured practice pan, with brine to the wrists of her rolled sleeves.

"I'm full," she said, "and you're unfinished. Next pan, please."

Hester had expected it, and had prepared a speech. Crisp did not wait for the speech. She had already turned back to the first-year at the next pan. He was trying to keep a falling drop of brine and had kept, instead, most of his own left thumb. She was explaining to him in a flat clear voice that the hold was a posture, not a feeling, and that he would get the use of the thumb back in an hour or so if he stopped crying.

Hester stood a moment on the salt walk between the pans in the cold sun. The Sere was blowing. It came off the White in long grey shoves and wrinkled the brine in the pans into little shuddering ridges, pink and green. She thought that *unfinished* was fair. She thought she would rather have been told *Lowside*. You could argue with *Lowside*.

She went to the next name on her list.

* * *

Reader Ottavio Bassano was kind. He was in his workroom on the Third Fathom with the shutters of three kilns open and his gold cord over one shoulder. He wore rings on every finger, including both thumbs, and he took her hands in both of his before she had said a word, and his rings were warm from the kilns.

"Oh, my dear. Corvin's girl. I *know*. Sit, sit. You'll have something—no? Water, then. Everyone down here is perishing of thirst; it's the light, it drinks you." He poured it himself. "Now listen. If it were up to my heart, I'd take you this minute. You have the most marvellous jaw. But I have six, and the Conclave counts. I can't. I simply can't. Have you tried Imelda Rusk? Weathering? No, don't try Imelda, she thinks Lowside's a disease. Try Corriston. No—Corriston's in Hask until Cinder. Oh, *God*, what a week." He pressed his ringed hand to his chest. "Did you know he hummed? Corvin? All through Conclave. Like a kettle. We used to throw pens at him. I *loved* him." His eyes filled. "Drink your water."

She drank her water.

* * *

Reader Imelda Rusk of Weathering did not think Lowside was a disease. She thought it was a weather pattern.

"We find," she said, without looking up from her charts, "that candidates from the industrial wards have an unfortunate tendency to settle early. It's the constitution. Generations of brine-steam. I've written on it." She made a mark on a chart. "I'm sure you understand. It's not personal."

"No," Hester said. "It's statistical."

Rusk looked up for the first time. She was a thin woman with a hard, healthy, wind-reddened face and the sky-blue cord of the Weathering Bench, and for a moment she seemed to consider whether she had been insulted.

"Quite," she said, and looked down again.

* * *

Reader Hollis Pym of Cellaring said he was full, which may have been true. He said it with his door open eight inches and his body in the gap.

Reader Odile Fane of Reading, who had Teo, said she was full, which was certainly true, because Teo had told Hester that he had taken her last place. Fane said it with real regret. She held Hester's eyes while she said it, a tall stooped woman with green ink on her lips from licking her pen, and added: "For what it's worth, you were seen in the Quiet Rooms on Thursday. Go carefully. The Halcyon is a very good place to be noticed, and a very bad one."

* * *

She left Benedek Sorle until last, because she had expected nothing from him and wished to be able to say she had tried.

He was in the Black Bench lecture room after the fourth bell, alone, wiping slates. The room was empty and cold, its tiers of salt benches worn into hollows by nine hundred years of scholars, and his arrears calculus from Thursday was still half visible on the great slate behind him in ghosts of chalk. He did not stop wiping when she came in. He did not stop when she said his name. She stood at the foot of the tiers and said what she had come to say: that she had been on Reader Aske's Bench, that Reader Aske was in the Quiet Rooms, and that she was without a sponsor and would be sent down at the Assay without one.

Sorle finished the slate. He folded the rag in four. He turned round.

He was a big, heavy, stooped man of sixty-odd, with a broad Harlish face gone pouchy and grey. He had a great deal of white hair and a black cord, and his eyes were as small and clever and unkind as a crow's.

"Miss Vail," he said. "I am told you have a remarkable tongue. I am told it by people whose judgement I despise, which inclines me to believe it. I am also told that you were at Corvin's door at the tenth bell on the night he was kept, and that you have been visiting him since in the Quiet Rooms, like a widow." He set the rag on the lectern. "I do not take charity cases from the dead. Good afternoon."

She stood a moment longer at the foot of the tiers.

"You were on the Third Fathom too," she said. "At the tenth bell. I passed you on the Stair the night before."

His small eyes rested on her. For a long breath nothing happened in his face at all.

"So you did," he said. "Good *afternoon*, Miss Vail."

* * *

The Warden's Gallery was on the Fifth Fathom, and you could not walk to it.

The Stair stopped at the Fourth. Below that there was a door of grey iron set into the salt, with a Proctor beside it, and beyond it a single brine-lift cage. It was smaller than the others and older, its bars worn to brightness at the height of a hand. A Fathom porter rode down with her, a silent man with an iron-grey armband who looked at her once, to see whether she would be sick, and then did not look at her again. The lift sank. The light from the lamp in the cage roof slid down the shaft walls, and the salt of the walls changed as it went. At the Fourth it was white with a faint pink in it, like all the Halcyon. Below the Fourth it went clearer and darker, with a grey in it like smoke in glass. The air in the cage grew colder, and then, unexpectedly, warmer, and damp. Hester tasted it without meaning to. The weight came down behind her eyes, gentle and enormous, and under the weight was something she knew.

*Quick.*

Faint, here. Very faint. Like a bitten tongue a long way off.

The cage stopped. The porter drew the gate.

"Warden's Gallery," he said. "Straight on, miss. You'll hear her."

She heard her. Orsolya Thane was laughing. It came down the corridor from an open door at the end, where lamplight lay on the floor. It was a big unguarded Lowside laugh, the laugh of the women on Tanner's Row when the washing was in and the children were somebody else's for an hour. Hester had not heard a laugh like it since she crossed the Line.

The Warden's office was a long low room cut into the salt, warm as a kitchen. That was the first thing: it was warm. There was a stove in the corner, a real iron stove that worked, with a kettle on it and a pair of grey wool stockings drying on the rail. There were rag rugs on the floor. There was a dresser with blue-and-white Lowside crockery on it, chipped. On the walls, among the charts of the Fifth Fathom's galleries in coloured inks, were pinned sheets of paper with names on them and dates, and some of the names had small crosses beside them in pencil. At the big table in the middle of the room sat the Warden of the Lower Fathoms, in her iron-grey gown with the sleeves pushed up, with a teacup in one hand and a pen in the other. She was laughing at something said by the man standing at her elbow with a ledger.

"Miss Vail." The laugh changed to a smile without losing any warmth. "There you are. Mr. Dunmore, this is Corvin's girl. Sit down before you fall down, chick. That lift does it to everybody the first time. It's the warm. Nobody expects the warm."

Hester sat. The chair was a kitchen chair with a cushion on it. Orsolya poured her tea from a brown pot without asking, and put salt in it without asking, and milk, and pushed it across.

The man with the ledger nodded to her. He was perhaps forty, of middle height, with a pleasant forgettable face and thinning fair hair combed carefully across. He wore a clerk's black coat over his grey gown. His cuffs were stained at the edge with ink, in two or three neat bands, as a clerk's cuffs are after years of resting on the same desk. He said "Miss Vail" in a voice that matched the face, and turned a page in his ledger, and wrote something.

"Mr. Dunmore keeps me honest," Orsolya said. "Or keeps me in order. One or the other. I can never remember which. Do you take sugar? No? Lowside girl. Good." She drank her own tea. Her hands, wrapped round the cup, were white at the knuckles and bluish at the nails. Hester had noticed them on the Causeway, the first morning, and noticed them again now: the hands of someone who had been cold for a very long time. "Now. Let's not dance. You've been round the Benches."

"Yes."

"And they've all said they're full."

"Yes."

"Crisp said you were unfinished."

Hester looked at her.

"She says it to everyone," Orsolya said. "She said it to me, in eight-seventy. She was right then too." She set her cup down. "I can't sponsor you, chick. I'd like to. I've six on my Bench and two of them are useless, and if I had my way I'd send the useless ones home to their mothers and take you in an afternoon. But the Conclave counts heads, and Vey counts them twice." She said the Deputy Chancellor's name the way a woman in Tanner's Row says the name of the landlord. "What I can do is this. I can put you in the Choir."

"Reader Aske mentioned the Choir."

"I expect he did. He knew I was short." Orsolya's face moved, briefly, at the name, and settled. "Three sols a week. Paid Fifthday, in silver, no deductions, Mr. Dunmore sees to it. Four shifts a week, two of them nights. It's hard work and it's cold work and it'll put years on you if you do it long enough, and I'll not lie to you about that, because I've never lied to a chorister in my life." She leaned forward. "But it's not nothing, chick. While you're in the Choir you're on the Halcyon's books. The Registrar can't send down a working chorister without the Warden's say. And I'll not say." She sat back. "That'll hold you till midwinter. By midwinter, something'll turn up. It generally does."

Three sols a week. Twelve a month. Tib's fee was six.

Hester did the sum in her head and found that she had done it before she had decided anything, which meant she had decided.

"Why?" she said.

"Why what?"

"Why me. You don't know me."

"I know you." The Warden looked at her across the table, steady and amused. "You walked the Causeway at dawn on your first morning with a carpetbag in one hand and your other hand in your pocket on your purse. I watched you. Only Lowside girls walk like that." She smiled. "And I hear you've got a hold like a dropped anvil. Crisp says so. She says you can't let go of a thing to save your life, but by God you can grip it. That's a chorister. That's what the Choir *is*. Gripping." She turned her cup. "And Corvin asked me. A fortnight ago, before—" She stopped. "He came down here with you in his mouth, the way he came down with everything, and he said *Orsolya, I've found a tongue*. So."

Hester looked down into her tea. She could not think of anything to say.

"Dunmore," Orsolya said. "The forms."

Dunmore had them ready. He laid them on the table in front of Hester: a stipend agreement, a declaration of fitness, and a release. *The chorister acknowledges that holding in the Stays may result in lag, cold injury, and the Settle, and holds the Halcyon blameless.* She read every word, because she was a customs assayer and had never signed a thing she had not read. Then she signed. Dunmore took the forms back and wrote her name at the head of each in his own hand, for the file, in small, upright, very plain letters. She watched him do it, and something about the letters tugged at her, the way a familiar face tugs in a crowd. She looked again and it was gone. A clerk's hand. Ten thousand clerks wrote like that.

"Your first shift's Fifthday," Orsolya said. "Night. Choir hall, Fourth Fathom, the ninth bell. Wear everything you own. Eat first." She drank her tea. Then, in the voice of a woman thinking aloud: "You've come in a good season, at least. The Stays have been kind this autumn. Kinder than they've any right to be." She shook her head. "I'll not complain. God knows we've had our share of the other kind."

At her elbow, Dunmore turned a page.

* * *

The Choir hall was the largest room Hester had ever stood in.

It was on the Fourth Fathom, under the Quiet Rooms, and it was oval, and it was very old. The salt of its walls had been worked once, centuries ago, into tiers of shallow steps that ran all the way round. Over time the steps had been worn by sitting and rubbing into something nearer a bowl, so that the whole room curved up from its floor like the inside of a shell. There were no benches. You sat on the salt. A dozen lamps of kept light hung from the high curved ceiling on long chains, and the light they gave was the white, slightly blue light of winter noon. They had been pressed on some clear midwinter day years ago, and they gave the whole hall the cold lucid look of a snowfield.

There were sixty-one choristers. Hester counted. They came in by twos and threes from the Stair at the ninth bell of Fifthday in their grey gowns and their iron-grey armbands. They were bundled in shawls and mufflers and fingerless gloves, carrying flasks, and they sat down on the steps in no order that she could see, and did not talk much. Most of them were older than her. Many were much older. There was a man of seventy with a white beard who sat down on the lowest step with a slowness she had only ever seen in Mr. Thorne. There were three women who looked like sisters, with the same blue lips. There was a boy of about twenty with a Lowside face she nearly knew. They had the look she had seen on choristers in Lowside taverns. It was not a look exactly. It was a lack of fidget. When they sat down, they sat down all the way.

A woman sat down next to her. She was perhaps sixty-five, square, small, with a face as brown and creased as a walnut and a mass of white hair bound in a red scarf. She wore three shawls, one on top of another, and she held out a fourth.

"You'll want that," she said. "First night, you'll want it after. Not now. After. Ama Tessaly." She put out a hand in a fingerless glove. "I've been holding forty-one years and I've never seen a first-nighter yet who thought she'd want a shawl. And then she wants it."

"Hester Vail."

"I know. Corvin's girl. The Warden said." Ama looked at her sidelong with small bright black eyes. "You've a strong hold, she says. Well. That's lucky for you and unlucky for you. The strong ones she puts nearest the line."

"What does that mean?"

"Means you'll find out." Ama patted her knee. "Here she is."

Orsolya came down the steps from the Stair door into the middle of the floor and stood there. She did not raise her voice. She did not need to; the shape of the hall carried everything. She looked round the tiers as a woman looks round her kitchen table at a family she has fed for thirty years.

"Fourteen holding tonight," she said. "Same as last night. Four's warm. Nine's warm. Eleven's warmer than I'd like. We'll go down to Eleven first and give it a long hold and then do the round. Mackey, you're lead on Nine. Ama, you'll take the new girl on Eleven, by the line." Her eyes found Hester. "Don't be frightened, chick. Ama's held more stays than anybody alive. Just do what she does. Right." She lifted her chin. "Let's have the breath."

Sixty-one people breathed in.

It was the strangest thing Hester had ever heard. It was not loud. It was sixty-one people drawing breath slowly, all together, over a count of perhaps eight. The sound of it filled the bowl of the hall like water filling a basin. Then they let it out over a count of twelve, and the breath out was a long low *hhhhhhh* that went round the walls and came back. They did it again, slower. And again. On the fourth breath, someone low down on the left began to sing.

It was not a song exactly. It was a single line, low, without words at first, a note held for the whole length of the outbreath and then dropped a tone at the end. On the next breath out, a dozen voices took it. On the next, all of them. They were not singing to make music. Hester understood that at once. They were singing to time the breath, to hold sixty-one bodies to the same slow count, so that sixty-one hearts would slow together. The note was the clock. Then the words came in on the outbreath, ragged and low and soft:

*Hold the line, hold the line,*
*breathe it slow and breathe it fine—*

Hester did not know the tune. By the third time round she did not need to. Her breath had gone with theirs without asking her. Her heart had gone with her breath. She sat on the cold salt step with Ama's shoulder against hers and felt her own body slowing down, and down. She felt it the way you feel a cart slowing on a hill: the whole weight of it easing.

"Good," Ama said, between lines, without looking at her. "Good girl. Now come on."

* * *

They went down singing.

The Choir went out of the hall by a low door on its eastern side that Hester had not noticed. They went down a passage cut in steps, sixty-one of them in a long grey file, singing, holding the breath. The passage went down steeply and turned, and turned again. The salt of its walls went clear and dark, and at one turning there was a door of the same grey iron as the lift door, standing open, with a Proctor beside it who did not look at them. Beyond the door the air changed.

Warm. Wet. And the taste.

It came up Hester's throat from the back of her tongue before she had even put her tongue to anything. Sweet, enormously heavy, so that the passage dimmed for a moment in front of her eyes. Under the sweetness, bright as a struck match: quick. Quick as the cargo. Quick as the beads on Tib's casing. Quick as the fifth dish at the First Taste, the one that had made two hundred people weep.

The Fifth Fathom.

It was not like the rest of the Halcyon. The rest of the Halcyon had been carved: cut and smoothed and finished, rooms and halls and stairs. The Fifth had been dug. Its galleries were low and rough, irregular, propped here and there with timber gone white with salt. They ran off from the passage in every direction into the dark. Along the floor of each gallery, against the left wall, ran a channel two hands wide, sheathed in brass. It was full to the brim with brine that did not move. Where the brass sheathing joined there were valves, wheels the size of plates, painted with white numerals. At every junction, more channels came in from the side and joined the first. They all ran together, the way gutters run together in a street, into a single great channel as wide as a man's body. That one ran away along the main gallery toward the east and out of the lamplight.

"Stay-lines," Ama said, between breaths, seeing her look. "That's the eastern trunk, that big one. All the lines go into her and she goes along to the Old Tap and round. You don't need to know. You need to know your line." She pointed with her chin down a side gallery. "That's ours. Eleven."

Stay Eleven was at the end of a short gallery, a dead end, where the roof came down low. The Choir divided at its mouth. Most went on toward the other stays with Orsolya and Mackey, still singing, their voices going away down the dark galleries like a tide going out. Twelve of them turned with Ama into Eleven. Hester went with her.

At the end of the gallery the salt was wet.

It was not damp. It was running. Water stood in shallow pools on the floor and ran down the walls in threads that glittered in the lamplight. The salt of the walls had gone soft and grey and translucent, like ice in a thaw. And it was warm. Hester could feel the warmth on her face from three paces off, the warmth of a hearth. In the middle of the end wall, at the height of a person's chest, there was a place where the salt bulged outward. It made a rough dome the size of a cartwheel, laced all over with fine cracks and glistening wet. From the cracks came a sound.

She thought at first it was the Choir's singing coming back off the walls. It was not. It was low and continuous and it came from *inside* the salt.

The brass stay-line from the gallery floor ran up the wall and ended against the bulge in a broad brass collar, sealed with grey wax. Round the collar, crusted on the salt, was a casing. It was a thick clear shell of new-grown grain over the whole face of the bulge, like a scab over a wound, and it was weeping. Beads of brine stood out all over it, and ran.

"Hands on the line," Ama said. "Where it comes up the wall. Both hands. Flat. Don't grip, chick, it's not a rope, it's a bath. Lay them in." She put her own hands on the brass where the line climbed the wall, low down, and Hester put hers above, and the others ranged out along the line on either side, hands flat on the sheathing. The brass was cold. Through the brass, through a slot at the top of the sheathing, Hester's fingertips touched the brine inside. That was cold too. It was very still.

"Now hold," said Ama. "Give it the hold. And sing."

They sang. Hester held.

It was like the Customs House, when she had gone into the hold to read the *Wren* grain: the breath going down, the heart going down, the whole of her settling round a single point like silt in a glass. But on the stool she had always held alone, and here she did not. On either side of her, through the line, she could feel the others. Not as people. As weight. Twelve weights, twelve stillnesses, settling with hers, the way the choristers' breath had settled with hers in the hall. And she felt the stillness *go*. It went out of her hands into the brine in the line, out of the line up the wall into the collar, out of the collar into the casing over the bulge. It went the way warmth goes out of your hands into a cold cup, steadily, without your doing anything. *Still runs to quick.* Sorle's bit of string. She felt the pull of the quickening at the far end of the line like the pull of a drain. It wanted what she had. It drank it.

And as she held, the sound from inside the salt came up through the line into her hands and through her hands into her, and it was no longer a sound behind a wall. It was all round her.

It was the roar. She knew it from the *Wren*. The vast low rushing, rise and fall, rise and fall, the rise coming again before the fall was done. But on the customs stool it had been the sound in a shell. Here she was inside the shell. It had *shape*. The rises had heads that curled and broke, and the falls had a long drawn hiss after them, like a broom across a floor, like sand pulled back. There was a wind over it, she could hear the wind, a long steady push, cold, and under the wind a slapping, irregular, close, as of something hollow and wooden knocked against and knocked against. The roar came in, and broke, and drew back hissing, and came in again. It had been doing that before she was born and before Ambry was built and before there was a Halcyon, and it was doing it now, in her hands.

Very far off, under all of it, so faint she could not swear afterward that she had heard it at all, a bell rang once.

She did not know she was weeping until the salt of it stung the corners of her mouth. The tears were running down her face and into the neck of her gown, and when they dried they dried white. She did not know why she was weeping. She was not sad. She was holding, and singing, and the sea was in her hands, and she had never in her life heard anything she wanted so much to go on hearing.

*Hold the line, hold the line—*

The beads on the casing slowed. They stopped running. One by one they stood, and dulled, and dried. The fine cracks in the bulge went white and closed. The warmth on her face cooled. The sound in the salt drew back, and back, as a tide draws back off a long shore, until it was the roar in a shell again, and then a murmur, and then only the Choir's voices singing in the low gallery.

"Ease off," said Ama softly. "Ease it now. Slow. Lift your hands. *Slow.*"

Hester lifted her hands.

* * *

She came to on the floor of the gallery with her back against the wall and Ama's fourth shawl round her shoulders.

She did not know how she had got there. That was the first thing, and it was the worst thing. One moment she had been lifting her hands from the brass; the next she was sitting down, wrapped up. The lamps had been moved. The choristers were standing about drinking from their flasks, and the brass of the line was dry where it had been wet. Ama was crouched in front of her holding a tin cup to her mouth.

"Drink," Ama said. "Don't talk. Drink."

She drank. It was water, with salt in it. She drank all of it and was still so thirsty that her tongue stuck to the roof of her mouth. Ama filled the cup again from her flask and she drank that too. She was cold. She had never been so cold. She was cold in her bones and her teeth, as if she had been lying in the snow on the White, and she could not stop shaking.

"How long," she said. Her lips were clumsy.

"Couple of minutes. Two, maybe three." Ama tucked the shawl tighter round her. "That's lag, chick. That's all that is. You gave it more than you had, first time, like they all do, and it took a bit of you with it. You'll get it back. You'll get most of it back." She sat back on her heels and looked at Hester with her small black eyes. "Told you you'd want the shawl."

Hester put her hand up to her face. Her cheeks were stiff with dried salt. She rubbed, and the salt came away on her fingers in white flakes.

"It was the sea," she said.

Ama looked at her.

"I heard it. In the line. In the salt. It was—there was wind, and something knocking, and the—" She did not have the words. She had never had any reason to learn them. "It was the sea."

"Aye," said Ama. "It sounds like it, doesn't it." She said it peaceably, as you would agree with a child about the shape of a cloud. "Everybody hears something their first night. The roar. Residue, the Readers call it. Noise of the Ebb, caught in the grain." She got up, slowly, with her hand on the wall, the way the old man with the white beard had sat down: carefully, all the way. "Forty-one years I've heard it. Some nights I could swear it was the sea too." She held out her hand. "Up you come. Round's not done. Four and Nine yet."

Hester took the hand and came up. Her legs held her, mostly. At the mouth of the gallery Orsolya Thane was standing with a lamp, watching them come out. When she saw Hester her face did a thing that Hester had seen in her mother's face once, long ago, at a school prize-giving in Lowside. It was a thing that had nothing to do with the prize.

"There she is," Orsolya said. "There's my girl. Eleven's quiet as a church, Ama says. First night." She put her cold hand on Hester's cheek, on the dried salt. "Didn't I say? Like a dropped anvil."

Hester could not answer. Her teeth were chattering.

"Go on," the Warden said gently. "Do the round. Then up, and eat something hot, and sleep. You've earned it." She turned away down the main gallery, lifting her lamp. Its light ran along the great brass channel of the eastern trunk, full and still, going away east into the dark, toward wherever it went.
