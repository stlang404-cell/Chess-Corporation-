# Chapter 9

## The Brinestacks

> Readers are reminded that the tablets remember being touched. Gloves are to be worn. The Stacks accept no responsibility for what a careless hand may leave behind for the next reader to find.
>
> — *Notice, Brinestacks entrance desk, Second Fathom*

The Brinestacks smelled of paper, and under the paper, of the sea.

Hester had not noticed that on the tour. She had been looking at the shelves then, everyone had, because the shelves were the thing you were meant to look at. They ran away from the entrance desk in long radiating galleries like the spokes of a wheel, each gallery three tiers high with iron walkways and ladders. They were cut straight into the salt of the Second Fathom, and they held, the Proctor-guide had said, three hundred thousand bound volumes and forty thousand tablets. You could believe it. You could not see the end of any of the galleries. They went off into a soft amber dusk of kept-light lamps, and in the dusk there were the shapes of people on ladders, and the small endless sounds of a library: pages, a cough, a ladder's wheel turning on its rail.

But on the twenty-fourth of Russet, when she came down the Stair at the second bell with Teo and her customs ledger, her mouth was still raw from the Stay. She tasted the air as she came in at the great arched door, and under the leather and the paper and the lamp-oil there was the faint wet sweetness she had had in her hands on Friday night. It was faint and old. The tablets, she thought. Forty thousand tablets of grain, every one of them holding its scrap of the roar.

"Where do we start?" said Teo.

"With what we can weigh," she said.

* * *

What they could weigh was the Exchequer returns.

Every licensed brinery on the Rim made a return to the Salt Exchequer each quarter, and a copy came to the Halcyon under the Charter, and the copies were bound by the year and shelved in the Cellaring gallery. Cellaring was the dullest gallery in the Stacks, and therefore the emptiest. It had kept goods, trade preservation and the arithmetic of fish. Hester and Teo had a long table at the end of it to themselves, under a lamp that had been pressed on a grey day and gave a light like weak tea.

The returns were in columns. Hester liked them at once. Name of house. Number of keepings at quarter's start. New keepings. Returns by family request. Returns by casing failure. Deaths following return. Number at quarter's end. Fees received. Each column was ruled, each figure was in a clerk's hand, and each page was signed at the foot by the house's director and countersigned by an exciseman. She had been reading documents like it since she was twelve years old. She sat down to them the way another woman might sit down to a piano.

"I can't read these," Teo said, after a quarter of an hour, with real sorrow. "I can read Tidewater short-script and High Marrane and the old Ostrine place-names off a map, but I look at a column of numbers and it simply lies there."

"Then read me the dates. I'll do the rest."

He read her the dates. She did the rest.

It took them till the fifth bell. The Cold House had been making returns since 788, the year of the Great Bloom. For a hundred years, quarter after quarter, the column headed *returns by casing failure* had stood mostly empty. A nought. A nought. A one, in 812, with a note in the margin: *casing cracked in the earthquake of Rime*. A nought. A one, in 851, an old woman kept eighty years whose casing had simply worn thin. Noughts. Hester ran her finger down the column through a century of noughts and ones and found a rate so slow you could set a clock by it. One casing failure every three years, roughly. One every three years, out of three hundred sleepers.

Then she came to the last two years, and the column was not empty.

*Second quarter, 902: 1.* A man called Abel Corrie, kept in 879. *Third quarter, 902: nought. Fourth quarter, 902: 1.* Mary Ann Teesdale, kept in 864. *First quarter, 903: nought. Second quarter, 903: 1.* Ezra Hallam, kept 890. And in the next column along, *deaths following return*, the same three numbers, in the same three rows. One, one, one.

Hester sat back.

"Three," she said. "In ten months. Against one in three years for a century."

Teo looked at the page over her shoulder. "Hallam," he said. "Didn't your Mother Agathe say something about a Hallam?"

"The Hallam boy. She said Pell had him recast. Forty sols, from the charity fund." Hester looked at the date beside the name. "This is his father. Or his brother. Kept in eight-ninety. Returned by casing failure in the second quarter of this year. Died."

"Then they've recast the son, because—"

"Because the son's weeping too." She ran her finger back to the director's signature at the foot of the page. *J. Pell, Director.* The exciseman's countersignature under it was an illegible scrawl. "Three of them. And nobody's written it anywhere but here, in a column nobody reads." She thought of what Aske had said in Quill Street. *It's in the Warden's returns to the Conclave, if anyone reads them, which nobody does.* "Is there anybody in this whole building who reads anything?"

"I'm told Reader Callow does," Teo said. "But he's very slow."

* * *

The Stacks catalogue was in the long hall behind the entrance desk. It was a wall of narrow wooden drawers, three thousand of them, each with a brass label-holder and a brass ring. Each drawer was full of cards written in two hundred years of different hands. Hester stood in front of it on the twenty-fifth with Aske's sheet of shorthand folded in her pocket and one line of it in her head.

*Cistern. Old lines.*

She found the drawer under C. It held three cards for the Cistern. Two were for printed books, a Water Board history of 610 and a pamphlet of 455 complaining about the price of brine. The third card was larger and older, ruled in red, and read in a beautiful faded hand: *The Founders' Cistern & Lines of Supply. Plans, sections & elevations, 41 sheets, fol. Founders' Gallery, Press 9. Restricted: Reader's countersignature.*

Below that, ruled off in the same red, was a column headed *Requisitions*. It was nearly empty. In more than a hundred years three people had asked to see the Founders' Cistern plans, and each had signed the card, with the date.

The first was a Reader called Anselm Brack, in 788. Hester supposed it was the Great Bloom year and the city had wanted to know about its water.

The last, in small fast blue ink sloping hard to the right, was *C. Aske, R. — 1 Russet 903.*

The first of Russet. The day she had walked down Coldwater Street and put her finger to a bead on her brother's casing. In the morning, while she was tasting the *Wren*'s grain, he had been here, before ever he came to the Customs House at the ninth bell with salt-mud on his gown.

And between Brack and Aske, a year earlier, there was one more line. It was in a hand she did not know, plain and black and neat, not a signature at all:

*W.L.F. (office) — 9 Fallow 902.*

She read it three times. Then she took the bill of lading out of the back of her ledger, where it had lived since the second of Russet. She unfolded it, and held it beside the card under the catalogue lamp, and looked at the shipper's box.

*W.L.F. per E.D.*

The hands were not the same. The card was written in round black copybook; the bill in small upright letters that she had seen somewhere lately and could not place. But the letters were the same three letters, in the same order, with the same full stops.

"Teo," she said.

He came. She showed him. He looked from one to the other for some time.

"Well," he said at last. "That's not a firm."

"No."

"*Office.* It says *office*. It's an office. Somebody's office signed for it." He frowned. "We could ask at the desk. They'd know their own abbreviations, surely."

They asked at the desk. The clerk on duty was a young Fellow with a cold and a stack of returns to file. She looked at the card for a while with her head on one side, sniffing.

"Departmental," she said. "Could be anything. Works. Lattice Fund. One of the Wardens. Lantern Fund, maybe. They all have codes; they change them every few years when somebody new comes in and wants their initials on things." She sniffed again. "Registry keeps the list. Write to the Registrar. They'll answer. Eventually."

"How eventually?"

"Fallow," said the clerk. "If you're lucky. Cinder if you're not."

Hester wrote to the Registrar. She wrote at the desk, standing, with the clerk's pen, a short polite request in the best hand she had, for the list of departmental requisition codes current in the year 902. She knew as she wrote it that it was the second note she had written in a month so that she could say afterward that she had written it. She posted it in the box for internal letters. It fell with a sound of paper landing on paper.

* * *

The plans themselves she could not have. *Restricted: Reader's countersignature.* She had no Reader. But there was a second set, Teo found by the end of the afternoon. It was a reduced copy, made by the Water Board in 400 at the time of the bricking-up, nine sheets instead of forty-one, and it sat unrestricted in a dusty folio in the Lattice gallery under *Municipal Works (superseded)*.

They spread it on a reading-slope in the Lattice gallery at the sixth bell, with the lamps going amber around them and the galleries emptying toward the Lantern Hour. The paper was brown and split along the folds and smelled of mice.

Sheet one was Ambry: not Ambry as it was now, but Ambry in the year 400, smaller, with fields where the Middle Ward was and the old shoreline drawn in a heavy line. Sheet two was Lowside. Hester's finger found Tanner's Row without looking. She found Pell Lane, Chandler's Lane, and Coldwater Street, running down to the sea-wall, with a warehouse at its foot marked *Excise Store*. And under the warehouse, under the whole lower end of Coldwater Street, there was a shape drawn in faint red ink. It was a long oval, ribbed like the inside of a ship, with a legend in the old Water Board hand:

*The Great Cistern. Capacity 40,000 tuns. Founders' work. Bricked 400.*

"The Cold House," said Teo slowly, "is sitting on the old reservoir."

Hester said nothing. She was looking at the eastern end of the oval. From it a single red line ran out under the sea-wall and off the edge of the sheet. Beside the line was written: *Main Line of Supply, E. — cont. sheet 6.*

Teo found sheet six. It was nearly blank, a long sheet of white paper representing the White, with nothing on it at all but a ruler-straight red line running across it from west to east, two miles at the scale. There were tiny cross-ticks every furlong, and at its eastern end a small drawn dome with a tower on it. Beside the dome, the same hand had written:

*Halcyon. Line enters at 5th Fm. per Founders' sections (restricted).*

They stood and looked at it in the amber light.

"Water," Teo said at last. "It was the water supply. The founders built the Halcyon and they piped water into it from the city. Or out of it to the city. Either way." He did not sound as if he believed it.

"Brine," said Hester.

"What?"

"Not water. It says *supply*, but the Water Board in four hundred called everything water. The founders didn't." She touched the red line where it crossed the sea-wall. "The Cold House was an excise store. Before that, the founders. Under it there's a reservoir that held forty thousand tuns of something. From the reservoir a line runs straight out under the White, two miles, into the Fifth Fathom of the Halcyon." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, as it did at the bench when a weight came out wrong. "And on my brother's casing the brine runs in lines toward the east."

Neither of them spoke. Down the gallery a Fellow was coming along with a long brass snuffer, putting out the reading lamps one by one for the Lantern Hour.

"It's bricked up," Teo said. "Five hundred years. It says."

"Aske didn't think so," said Hester. "*Cistern, two question marks. Old lines.*"

She folded the sheets back into their folio carefully, along their old splits, and put the folio back on its shelf exactly where it had been, with its spine flush to the next. Then she stood with her hand flat on it, as she put her hand flat on a cargo to feel its weight.

* * *

On the twenty-seventh, Teo heard the cry.

They had gone back to the Stacks to look for anything about the Cistern in the founders' period. It was a hopeless task, Teo said cheerfully, since nearly everything of the founders' period had burned in the fire of 230, and what hadn't burned was restricted, and what wasn't restricted was in short-script so archaic it would take him a month a page. But there were a few unrestricted brinescript tablets from just after the fire, in the Reading gallery. They were copies of copies, Water Board accounts, nothing that mattered. Teo, who was on Fane's Bench, was allowed to read them.

He took one into a Reading Well and Hester went in with him and drew the curtain.

She had never watched Teo read. He was a poor hand at the hold; everybody knew that. Crisp had said in front of the whole Turning Intake that he held like a man trying to sit still on an ant-hill. He fidgeted, his knee went, his fingers drummed. She had seen him at the Pans trying to keep a drop and lose it three times in a row. But Reading was not the same as Keeping. Reading was listening, Fane said, and listening was a thing you could do with your knee going.

He sat in the Well's one chair with the tablet in his gloved hands. It was a slab of grey-white salt the size of a large book, cut on one face with the close lines of brinescript, its edges worn soft. He took off one glove and laid his bare palm on the uncut face. He shut his eyes. His knee went for a while, and then it stopped.

Hester stood against the curved wall and watched him settle. It was not a deep stillness, not the anvil Orsolya had said Hester had. It was something lighter and stranger. His face went open, as if he were listening to someone speak across a room and was trying not to miss a word. His lips moved. After a minute he was speaking aloud, softly, what he heard. It was not the text cut into the tablet. It was the other thing, the moment the tablet had been made in, held in the grain.

"Scratching," he murmured. "Stylus. Someone's cutting it. A cough. A man with a cold. Fire in a grate. A cart, outside, far off—iron wheels on stone. And—the roar, under, the residue. It's always there in the old ones, you get used to it, you hear past it—" He stopped.

His face changed.

"What's that," he said.

"What?"

"Hush." He held up his free hand without opening his eyes. "There. In the roar. Under the scratching. There." His lips parted. "Something crying."

Hester came off the wall.

"High," Teo said slowly, "and falling. Two notes. Then a third that—breaks. Like a—" He frowned with his eyes shut. "Like a hinge, or a—"

"A child," Hester said. "A long way off."

His eyes opened.

She was standing over him. She did not remember crossing the Well. Her hands were cold.

"I heard that," she said. "In the *Wren*'s grain. On the first of Russet, on my customs stool. In the roar. Two notes and a third that cracks." She took a breath. "What is it, Teo? You've been reading old salt for four years. What is it?"

He sat with the tablet on his knees and stared at her. Then, without another word, he got up and went out through the curtain. She heard him walking fast down the gallery, and then running.

* * *

He came back a quarter of an hour later with a book.

It was a hymnal: not the small black pocket hymnal every Tider carried, but a great old illustrated folio from the Order's shelf in the Religion gallery. It was printed in Harl two hundred years ago, with woodcuts at the head of every psalm, and the leather of its binding was rubbed through at the corners. He laid it on the reading-slope over the Water Board tablet and turned the pages fast and then slowly.

"The Abbey had this edition," he said. "The novices weren't allowed to touch it. I used to sneak in and look at the pictures. They're all of the sea. Everything. Every psalm. It's a book of *things that aren't here*." He found the page. "There."

It was the woodcut at the head of the *Song for the Vigil*. It showed a wave, drawn as a curling line of crests, the way a child draws the sea, and above it a sky with a few lines for wind. In the sky there were three birds. They were not salt-larks. They were long-winged, angled, with heads drawn small and beaks hooked. The woodcutter had carved their wings bent at the middle like a drawn bow. Beneath them, in the small italic of a caption, the printer had set a line from the psalm:

*And the gulls, the birds of the sea, crying.*

Hester looked at it for a long time.

"A gull," she said.

"A gull."

"I've never seen one."

"Nobody has." Teo's voice was not quite steady. "Hester. There are no gulls. There haven't been gulls on the Rim since the Ebb. They lived on the sea. They're in the ballads and the hymns and the Ostrine place-names. Gull Point, out by the Weeps, where there isn't a point and there aren't any gulls. They went when the sea went. Everybody knows that. The Abbot used to say they flew away with it to wherever it went, and they'd come back with it when it came home." He laughed, not happily. "We used to sing about them at the Vigil. Hundreds of us, with candles, out on the White. *And the gulls crying.* I never once thought about what one would sound like."

"It sounds like that," said Hester. "It sounds like a hinge."

They looked at each other across the open hymnal.

"The residue," Teo said. "The roar. The Readers say it's the noise of the Ebb, of the salt setting. Crisp says so. Fane says so. Every textbook in this building says so." He put his finger on the woodcut. "The salt setting doesn't make a sound like a *bird*."

"No," said Hester.

"Then what—"

She did not answer. She did not have an answer. She had a cargo of warm grain that should not exist, and a brother whose casing wept toward the east. She had a red line on a mouse-eaten map running two miles under the White into the Fifth Fathom. She had a sound in a stay-line that she would have sworn on Mr. Thorne's stool was waves, and wind, and something wooden knocking. And she had a gull. She had all of it laid out like samples on a slate, and she could not put a grade to any of it. She did not know what it weighed.

* * *

They went out of the Stacks at the eleventh bell, when the lamps were half of them out and the galleries were dark and the porters were going round with their keys. The entrance hall was empty except for the desk, which was empty too, and the long table beside the desk where the under-archivist sat.

She had seen him on the tour. The Proctor-guide had pointed him out with a wink: *Reader Callow, our under-archivist, or as the aspirants call him, the furniture.* She had seen him every time she had come to the Stacks since, in the same chair at the same table, under the same lamp. He was an old man, so old that age seemed to have stopped being a process in him and become a material. He was white-haired, white-browed and white-lashed, his skin gone the colour and texture of good paper. His hands lay on the table in front of him, one on top of the other, and in five visits she had never seen them move. His eyes were open. They were pale grey, and as far as she could tell they did not blink. Aspirants said he had been Settled for twenty years and the Stacks had simply never got round to moving him. Aspirants said a great many things.

She had stopped noticing him, as everyone did. She was passing the table, a pace behind Teo, with the hymnal's woodcut still in her eyes, when he spoke.

"Corvin sat there too."

It was so slow that it hardly seemed a sentence. Each word came out separately, with a breath before it, as if each had had to be fetched from a long way off and set down with care. His voice was dry and light, like paper turning. His head had not moved. His hands had not moved. Only his eyes had come round, grey and steady, to rest on her.

Hester stopped.

"I'm sorry?"

The old man took a long time. In the silence the lamp over his table hissed, and somewhere far off down a gallery a porter's keys jingled and stopped. Teo had turned back and was standing quiet at Hester's shoulder.

"There," said Reader Callow. His eyes went, slowly, to the catalogue hall behind her, to the wall of drawers. Then they came back. "On the first of the month."

He said nothing else. They waited. After a long while his eyes went back to wherever they had been looking before, at the lamp or the table or nothing, and stayed there. He did not seem to know they were still standing in front of him.

Hester went up the Stair to the Rookery with her ledger under her arm, Teo silent beside her. The Stair turned round and round in the dark, with the chalked names of the Settled going by on the walls in the light of the landing lamps. She thought about the old man at his table. He had sat there on the first of Russet while Corvin Aske signed his name in the catalogue with blue ink. He had sat there on the ninth of Fallow last year while somebody wrote *W.L.F. (office)* in round black copybook. He had sat there, she supposed, for every requisition on every card in three thousand drawers, for twenty years, and nobody had ever asked him what he had seen, because he was the furniture.

At the window of R-41, before she slept, she stood a while and looked out. The White lay black to the edge of the world. Two miles off Ambry was a smudge of orange on the dark. Somewhere between here and there, under the salt, under the dark, a line ran straight as a rule from her brother's floor to the Fifth Fathom. She could not see it. She stood with her forehead against the cold glass, and for the first time since she had stepped over the Line she let herself think a thing she could not weigh. Then she went to bed.
