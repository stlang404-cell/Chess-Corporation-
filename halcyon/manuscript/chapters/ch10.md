# Chapter 10

## Lantern Night

> Summer in a jar, summer in a jar,
> pour it on the rooftops where the cold folk are.
>
> — *Ambry street-song, sung by children on the last night of Russet*

Saskia Orme was a different woman on the Ambry side of the Causeway, and Hester could not decide whether she liked her better.

On the Shelf, Saskia moved through the Halcyon as if every corridor charged a toll. She answered questions in as few words as would pass, and looked at Hester, when she looked at her at all, as a glazier looks at a bubble in a pane. But when they came off the Causeway at the Eastgate at the fourth bell on the thirtieth of Russet, into the smell of hot fat and the shouting of the lantern-sellers, something in her shoulders let go. She pulled the pins out of her hair as she walked and talked round them. She bought three twists of burnt sugar from a barrow without haggling, which Hester had never seen a Quell woman do, and handed them out as if it were nothing.

"Don't thank me," she said. "It's for the walk. The Glassmarket's uphill and Teodor complains."

"I don't complain," said Teo. "I lament. It's a liturgical form."

"You lament uphill."

"Most of the great laments are uphill. It's the incline that does it." He bit his sugar and closed his eyes. "Oh. Oh, that's burnt properly. That's burnt by somebody who understands burning."

Saskia almost smiled. Hester watched it arrive and be put away again, and thought: *so she can*.

Ambry on the last day of Russet was a city getting ready to be lit. Hester had stood in these streets on thirty-three Lantern Nights and it still surprised her. Ladders leaned on every house-front on the Corn Exchange. Men at the tops of them hung iron brackets under the eaves, and women came behind with the jars: Quell glass, round-bellied, the size of a child's head, the stoppers sealed in wax and stamped with the halcyon. Inside each, a pound or so of white grain sat in the bottom. Kept light always looked like nothing until it was let out. You could hold a jar of it up to your eye at noon and see only salt.

"There's eleven thousand of them this year," Saskia said. "Bassano says. Pressed in the Pans between Seedmonth and Highsummer, every one carried over the Causeway by hand in the last three days. Two broke on the Causeway this morning and a carter lost his eyebrows."

"Broke how?" said Hester.

"Badly." Saskia took a pin out of her mouth. "I'll show you. After."

* * *

The Glassmarket was a steep street of narrow shops that climbed from the Corn Exchange to the Middle Ward, all Quell people: glaziers and lens-grinders and the women who painted fine lines on kept-light lanterns for the rich. In the window of every shop, today, there was a jar on a stand, and a child guarding it.

Saskia's mother kept a shop near the top. It had a green door, a bell that rang two notes, and a window of spectacle-glass laid out on black velvet like a jeweller's tray. Hennie Orme was a small square grey woman with Saskia's jaw and Saskia's hands, broader. She came round the counter wiping them on her apron, looked Hester up and down once, as Saskia did, and then looked at Teo and laughed.

"You'll be the monk," she said.

"Former," said Teo. "Lapsed. Fallen, in the technical sense."

"You've crumbs on your gown, fallen." She turned to the back of the shop. "Pip. *Pip.* Your mother's here, and she's brought a monk."

A child came out from under the counter. She did not run. She was six, thin, with a cloud of pale hair that had got loose from two plaits, and she held in both hands a small jar of kept light, her own, the size of an apple. She stopped in the middle of the floor and looked at her mother.

Saskia went down on her knees on the shop boards.

Hester had not seen that face before. Nobody at the Halcyon had seen it, she was nearly sure. It was not softer, exactly. It was more open, as a shutter is more open: the same wood, swung back. Saskia did not reach for the child. She waited, on her knees, with her hands on her thighs, and the child came to her across the floor at her own pace and held out the jar.

"I didn't drop it," Pip said.

"I can see you didn't."

"Gran said if I dropped it there'd be no summer."

"Gran's a liar," said Saskia. "There'd be a bit less summer. Come here."

Then the child was in her arms and the jar was somehow safe between them. Hennie turned to the counter and began rearranging spectacles that did not need it, and Hester looked at the window.

They had tea in the back room, Quell-fashion, which meant no salt and a great deal of sugar. Teo sat on the floor and let Pip explain a game involving buttons, which he lost thoroughly and with evident pleasure. Saskia sat with her cup and her daughter's hair in her free hand, plaiting it, unplaiting it, plaiting it again.

On the shelf above the stove was a small painted portrait of a young man in a pilot's leather coat, laughing, his cap pushed back. It was the only painted thing in the room. Hester looked at it once and away.

"Jory," said Saskia, without looking up from the plait. "My husband. He's dead. You were going to ask."

"I wasn't."

"No. You weren't. You're like me, you don't ask." She tied the plait off with a bit of thread. "He sailed freighters for the Lodge. Quell to Harl and back, the long southern run. Two years ago in Cinder his ship went through the crust on the eastern White, forty miles out. A sink. The whole ship. They found the mainmast sticking up out of the salt like a grave-post, and they found him, because he was at the masthead when she went and he climbed. He'd got as far as the truck." She said it in the voice for prices. "A sink's only open for a minute. Then it skins over and sets. He was standing on the top of the mast with the salt round his chest. They had to cut it."

Pip, on the floor, was teaching Teo to flick a button into a cup. Teo was bad at it.

"That's why the Halcyon," Hester said.

"That's why the Halcyon. A Lodge widow gets a pension of nine sols a quarter. A Reader of Lumen in Ambry gets four hundred a year and a house in the Close. I did the sum." Saskia drank her tea. "I don't do causes, Vail. I do rent."

"I know," Hester said. "I do it too."

Something passed between them over the teacups. It was not friendship. It was something earlier than that, a recognition of weights.

When they left, Saskia gave her mother money for the week. Hester saw the coin go across: a sol, a new one, bright, the edges still sharp from the mint, though there was no reason for that to mean anything. Pip stood in the door with her jar and watched them down the street.

* * *

The Glassmarket's lantern-jars were being loaded off a dray at the bottom of the street, into baskets for the ladder-women. Saskia stopped there and showed Hester what she had promised.

She took a jar out of a basket, over the carter's protests, and held it up against the late sun. "Look at the belly. Through it. At the edge."

The glass was clear and slightly greenish, as Quell glass was. There was nothing in it.

"Now this one." Saskia took another from a basket off a different dray, with a cheaper mark on the stopper.

Hester saw it at once, and was annoyed not to have seen it in the first. Near the base of the second jar there were faint lines, almost not there, like the lines in water that has been stirred and not quite settled. They ran in a stretched curl.

"Stress," Saskia said. "That's the glass remembering how it cooled. When you blow a jar it's all one heat. Then the outside cools first, because the air's on it, and shrinks, and the inside's still hot and big and gets squeezed. Let it cool fast, out on the bench in the draught, and the outside sets hard round an inside that's still pushing. The push stays in it. Months. Years. And one day you knock it, or you warm it, and it just goes. All at once." She tapped the jar with a fingernail. "This one's going to go. You pour kept summer out of a jar and the arrears come off it warm. That's enough. It'll be in pieces in somebody's gutter by Midwinter."

"And the first one?"

"Annealed." Saskia put it back gently in its basket. "You take it off the pipe and put it in a box of hot ash and let it come down slow. A day. Two for a big one. The outside and the inside cool together, near enough. The stress walks out of it a bit at a time, and when it's cold there's nothing left in it pushing. You can drop that jar on a stone floor and it'll bounce." She glanced at the carter. "Don't drop it on a stone floor."

"The two on the Causeway," Hester said.

"From Hallam's works. They save on ash." Saskia shrugged. "Cool it slow and the stress walks out. Cool it fast and it waits for you. That's the whole of glass. My father used to say it's the whole of most things."

Hester thought, for no reason she could name, of her father at pan nineteen, drawing the coals a shovelful at a time. She did not say so.

* * *

They went down to the Cold House at dusk because Hester said she would like to, and nobody argued. She had expected Saskia to leave them. Saskia walked beside her down the long slope of Coldwater Street with her hands in her coat pockets and said nothing about it at all, which was, Hester was coming to understand, how a Quell woman was kind.

Ennis was not at the desk. A girl Hester did not know sat there, with the Cold House's one charity jar on the felt beside the ledger, unopened.

"We're not open. It's the holiday."

"I'm Vail. Two hundred and eleven."

"Oh. Mother Agathe said you might." The girl looked at Teo and Saskia, then at Hester's gown and the white cord at the shoulder, and decided it was not worth the trouble.

The ward at dusk was not the ward in the morning. The high windows had gone indigo. A small oil flame burned in brass at the foot of every casing, and each casing had taken its flame into itself and held it, so that the long room was full of held lights, three hundred and four of them, in rows. Teo stopped at the bottom of the six steps.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, Hester."

"Don't," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

Tib was where Tib was. The beads on his casing had multiplied. In the lamplight they were a fine dew over the whole eastern flank, running in lines, and in three places the lines had joined into runnels. There was a damp stain on the plinth's stone at the eastern foot, the size of her palm. It had not been there four weeks ago.

She did not touch it. She had no need to.

"This is your brother," said Saskia. It was not a question.

"Tobias. Tib."

Saskia looked at him for a long moment: at the hand curled on his chest, the pencil-grip, and then at the beads. Her face did nothing at all.

"How old?"

"Fourteen."

"How long?"

"Eleven years."

Saskia nodded slowly. "That's a lot of rent," she said, and Hester, astonishingly, laughed.

Agathe came up the aisle with her brush. She was slower than at the start of the month, or Hester was seeing her differently. She took in Teo's shawl, and the brass wave of the Order still pinned to it, and her face changed.

"A brother," she said.

"Was," said Teo. "Tidewater. I'm sorry, Mother. I left."

"Many do." She took his hand in both hers, briefly, the Tider way. "Tide willing, it'll bring you round again, one way or another." Then, to Hester, low: "Come and see, my dear. I want you to see."

She took them to the sixth row. In the third casing from the end lay a stout woman of thirty or so in a brown dress, on her side, knees drawn up, both hands pressed to her mouth as if she had coughed and been surprised by it. Her hands and chin were dark. Not with shadow.

"Bett Linnow," Agathe said. "Twenty years this Rime. Haemorrhage of the lungs."

Bett Linnow's casing was not beaded. It was running. The whole eastern side was wet, and the wet ran into a groove cut in the plinth and from there into a little brass channel along the floor. And the casing was thinner. Over Bett's shoulder, where it should have been a hand's breadth thick, it was the thickness of a slate. Light came through it differently there. The shoulder of the brown dress looked closer to the air than the rest of her, as a thing looks closer when it lies near the surface of water.

"The brinewright recast her in Highsummer," Agathe said. "And again on the twelfth. Forty sols each time, from the charity fund, and Mr. Pell put up a hand and said there's no more after this. It doesn't hold."

"How long?" said Hester.

Agathe only looked at the casing.

At the end of the sixth row, on a stool beside the plinth, sat a very old woman Hester had taken for a heap of shawls. She had one hand flat on the stone, an inch from the casing. She did not look round.

"Ruth," Agathe said quietly. "Her mother. She'll sit through the light."

Hester followed the brass channel with her eyes. It ran along the base of the plinths toward the eastern wall of the ward, and there it went into a drain, a small square grating in the floor. Every row had one. She had never looked at them.

Somebody was crouching by the drain in the fourth row.

* * *

He was in a long dark coat with a fur collar that cost more than the Cold House's charity fund. His hair was the colour of wet straw and well cut. He had a hand on the grating and his face close to it, and he did not hear them until Hester was standing over him.

"Ferrers," she said.

Lucan Ferrers came to his feet faster than she would have expected. For a moment his face was entirely unguarded, startled, young, guilty as a boy caught in a larder. Then the face she knew came down over it, smooth as a shutter.

"Miss Vail," he said. "How unfortunate."

"For which of us?"

"I'd have said for me. But I'm open to argument." He looked past her at Teo and Saskia. "Is this a deputation?"

"What are you doing in the charity ward of the Cold House on Lantern Night with your hand down a drain?"

"I might ask what you're doing here."

"My brother's in the fourth row."

That stopped him. She watched him look down the row toward the third window and find casing two hundred and eleven, the boy with the pencil hand, and the beads. He did not say *I'm sorry*. She would have thought less of him if he had.

"Keep your voice down," he said instead, low. "Please." It was the *please* that did it. She had not heard him use the word since Matriculation.

They talked in whispers by the eastern wall, the four of them, while the plinth-lamps burned and the old woman at the end of the sixth row sat with her hand on the stone.

"The *Constant Wren*," Lucan said. "You said the name to me in the Stacks. I'd never heard of it. I don't attend to the firm's ships; it's not considered my department." He said *considered* with a little twist. "I went home last week. I asked my father."

"And?"

"And he told me to drop it. He wasn't angry. That was the strange part. My father is angry about most things; it's how he shows interest. He put his hand on my shoulder, which he hasn't done since I was nine, and told me the Halcyon's business was the Halcyon's business and the firm's was the firm's, and that some laundry kept the lamps lit. Then he went into his study and shut the door." He paused. "So naturally I waited until he'd gone to the Assize, and went through the firm's books."

"Naturally," said Saskia.

"The *Wren*'s cargo isn't there. Not under that name. But there's a supplies ledger: brine, mostly, and Middling, delivered to brineries all over the city. And there's a line in Highsummer." He took a folded paper from inside his coat, a page torn from a ledger. He had torn it badly, Hester noticed, with the clumsiness of a man who had never needed to tear evidence from anything. "Delivered to the Charitable Brinery, Coldwater Street. Forty firkins live brine, Deep grade. For cistern maintenance. Charged not to the brinery. To a Halcyon account."

Hester tilted it to the nearest plinth-lamp.

*Cold House. 40 fk. live brine (Deep). Cistern mntce. Acct: Halcyon — per W.L.F.*

"Cistern," said Teo. He had gone pale under his freckles.

Hester was looking at the three letters at the end of the line, in a clerk's copperplate. She had seen them before, in a different hand, at the foot of a bill of lading. *W.L.F. per E.D.* She did not know what they stood for. She knew they had now turned up twice where they had no business being.

"What cistern?" Lucan said. "The Cold House hasn't got one. I've been round the whole building, and the yard. No tank, no reservoir. There's only these." He touched the grating with his boot. "Every row has a drain, running east. I put my hand down this one. It's wet. It's running. Water doesn't run in a dry house in Russet." He looked at her. "What's under this building, Vail?"

"I don't know," Hester said.

It was true. She had a map in her head now, copied in the Brinestacks a week ago: a great vaulted cistern under Coldwater Street from the founders' time, and a line running east from it under the White. But she did not *know*. Knowing was a thing you did with your tongue, on a bench, with a reference jar. It was not a thing you did with a drawing.

She gave him back the paper. He looked surprised.

"You'll want that," she said. "It's your father's."

"Yes," he said slowly. "I suppose it is."

Outside, faintly through the high windows, the city's bells began to ring the eighth.

* * *

The light was to be poured at the ninth bell. They fetched Pip from the Glassmarket at a run, because Hennie would not climb a wall at her age and Pip had been promised, and then they went up onto the old sea-wall at the end of Coldwater Street, because Hester said it was the best place and nobody wanted the crowds on the Corn Exchange. Lucan came. Nobody asked him; he simply walked with them. Saskia gave him one look and decided, visibly, that he was not worth the effort of getting rid of.

So there were four of them on the wall, and a child. Lowside had always come up onto the wall on Lantern Night. The broad top was crowded with families in their coats on blankets on the cold stone, passing bottles, telling children to stop climbing on the parapet. On one side lay Lowside and the slope of the city, dark and full of ladders. On the other, far below, lay the White.

The White at night was not white. It was grey-blue, faintly luminous, as if it had kept a little of the day and was letting it out slowly. Out on it, the tiles had begun to sing. In the cold after sunset the great plates of salt contracted and ticked against each other, millions of them, out to the edge of the world, a sound like a cathedral full of glass beads being poured from one hand to another.

Pip, on Saskia's hip, put her hands over her ears and then took them away.

"It's talking," she said.

"It's cooling," said Saskia.

"Same thing," said Teo, and Saskia, Hester saw in the dark, did not argue.

Two miles out, the Halcyon was a dark shape on the dark plain, and the Lantern on top of it was dark too, the only night of the year it was. The mirrors had been turned away at sunset and the tower stood unlit against the stars like a pin stuck in a map.

Lucan stood a few feet off, at the parapet, with his hands in his expensive pockets. Hester went and stood beside him. She did not know why.

"Have you seen it from up here before?" she asked.

"From the roof of Ferrers House. We have a terrace." He did not look at her. "My mother used to take me up. She'd hold the jar while I broke the wax. She died when I was eleven." He said it in the same tone as *we have a terrace*. "I haven't been on the terrace since."

Hester thought of several things to say and said none of them.

The ninth bell began, from the Halcyon first, faint across two miles: a single deep note. Then the Lantern lit.

It did not flash. It *opened*. Light came out of the top of it as if someone had uncorked the sky: a thick honey-gold, the gold of a wheatfield at four in the afternoon in Highsummer, of the light on a wall when you have been asleep in the heat and woken. It poured from the Lantern's head in every direction and lay across the White in a widening disc, and the salt threw it back up, so that the whole plain between the Shelf and the city was a floor of gold under a gold sky.

And then Ambry answered.

On every roof, under every eave, eleven thousand jars were unstoppered at once and tipped. The light did not come out like water, or like lamplight. It came out like summer. It spilled over the gutters and ran down the slates and filled the streets to the first-floor windows. Lowside below them went from black to gold in the time it takes to say so. Each roof was its own colour: the white-gold of noon, the red-gold of evening, the green-gold of a June morning under trees, all running together in the streets until the city was a pan of molten light. And it was warm. Hester felt it on her face, standing on a sea-wall above the White on the last night of Russet: the sun on the side of her face, as on a summer afternoon when you turn toward it with your eyes shut.

There was sound in it. Every year she forgot that, and every year it came back to her like a smell. Out of the gold in Lowside's streets came birdsong, faint and enormous: the larks of Highsummer over the pans, sparrows at dawn in the eaves, a blackbird somewhere at the end of a long June evening. It was a hundred summer days that had been put in jars and carried over the Causeway and poured out on a winter city.

On the wall, people were crying, not loudly. An old man on a blanket with his face turned up, wet. A woman laughing with her hand over her mouth. Children shrieking through the gold, which fell on them like everything else. Pip had both arms out from Saskia's hip and was trying to catch it. Saskia was not looking at the light. She was looking at her daughter, with the face from the shop floor.

Teo stood straight, as he must once have stood in the abbey choir. His lips were moving. Hester did not ask what he was saying.

She thought of the Cold House: the one jar on the felt beside the ledger, the girl at the desk unstopping it. Down there in the gold, under Coldwater Street, the light would be pouring down the six worn steps and over the rows, over three hundred and four casings, over Bett Linnow and the old woman with her hand on the stone, and over a boy with a pencil hand. The casings would take it into themselves and hold it, because that was what they did, and the beads on them would shine.

The light lasted a little under a quarter of an hour. Then it thinned, gold to amber, amber to a kind of rose, and the birdsong went quiet one bird at a time, as an evening goes. Lowside came back, roof by roof, to the ordinary dark of a city at the end of Russet with the Sere blowing salt along its streets. Somebody on the wall began, raggedly, to sing the jar-song, and the children took it up.

Lucan had not moved from the parapet. In the last of the rose light his face was quite still.

"I don't think it was an accident," he said, low, so that only she could hear. "Aske."

"No," Hester said.

Two miles off, the Lantern went out, and the dark came back over the White, and the tiles went on ticking into it, cooling, millions of them, as if nothing had been poured at all.
