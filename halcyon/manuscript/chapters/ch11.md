# Chapter 11

## The Sink

> The White does not forgive. The White does not hate. The White is a floor with a hole in it.
>
> — *Weathering field manual issued to first-year aspirants, the Halcyon, rev. 880 Y.E.*

The *Gannet* was a school skiff, which meant she was old, slow by the standards of the White, and built to forgive the stupid. She was a two-master, fifty feet from bowsprit to stern, with a broad flat deck and iron runners as thick as a man's thigh, and she had been repainted so many times that the blue of her hull had layers in it, like the strata in a delver's cut. At the second bell on the sixth of Fallow she lay on the salt ramp below the Halcyon's eastern Gatehouse with her sails slatting in a stiff north-easterly, and twenty-two first-year aspirants stood beside her in borrowed wool coats and glare-goggles, looking at her as cattle look at a river.

Hester had spent twenty-one years at a desk above a harbour full of landships. She had tasted their holds and never once set foot on a deck, because a single crossing to Quell cost more than she earned in a month. She looked at the *Gannet* and felt something she had not felt since she was a child: a plain, uncomplicated want.

"Tib would have given his teeth for this," she said, before she could stop herself.

"Then you'll have to enjoy it twice," said Teo. He was green already, and they had not moved. "I'm told one is sick. Copiously. Over the side."

"You're thinking of water."

"I'm thinking of the theology of motion. It's my area."

The Warden of the Lower Fathoms came down the ramp at a walk, in an iron-grey coat with the collar turned up and a fur hat that had seen better winters, and the aspirants straightened without being told.

"Right," said Orsolya Thane. "Morning, all. You've read the manual. Forget it. Three rules on the White. One: you go where the pilot says and nowhere else. Two: if Ballast lies down, you stop. Three: nobody keeps anything unless I say so." Her eye stopped on Hester, and something in it warmed. "And nobody's sick on my deck. Over the side. Into the wind is a lesson you only need once."

Ballast turned out to be a lurcher, a long grey dog with a rough coat and a face like an apologetic horse. He lay on a coil of rope by the mainmast with his chin on his paws and watched the aspirants board with deep weariness.

"Best sink-sniffer on the Shelf," said the pilot, a lean brown Fellow called Marten Coyle with a sky-blue Weathering cord and a face cracked by glare. "Fourteen years on the White and never put a foot wrong. If he lies flat, you freeze, and you don't move till I tell you. He hears the crust go hollow before the crust knows it."

Lucan Ferrers was the last to board, in a coat that fitted him and tortoiseshell goggles of his own. He stepped over the rail as though he had been doing it all his life, which, Hester supposed, he had. He nodded to her as he had nodded since Lantern Night: a nod with nothing in it but the fact of it. She nodded back.

Coyle shouted, two Fellows on the ramp knocked out the chocks, the sails came round with a crack like a slammed door, and the *Gannet* began to move.

* * *

The runners ground on the ramp and the deck jolted and Teo sat down on a hatch-cover. Then they came off onto the open White, and the grind became a hiss, a long continuous rush under the hull like silk being torn slowly, and the jolting smoothed into a hum she felt through her boots and up her shins. The wind took the mainsail, Coyle put the helm over, and the *Gannet* leaned.

She had not known it would be so fast. The ridges between the tiles flickered past so quickly they became a pattern and then a texture and then nothing at all, a smooth white rushing. The wind came over the bow full of fine salt that stung her lips and made her eyes run behind the goggles. Ahead there was no road, no rail, no edge; only the white going out flat under a hard blue sky to a horizon that was not where it ought to be.

She laughed. She held the rail with both hands and laughed into the wind like a child on a swing, and did not care who heard.

"Nine knots," Coyle shouted from the helm, to nobody. "Ten. She'll make twelve on this reach. Old cow."

They ran east and a little north for most of an hour. The Halcyon fell away to a white smudge with a needle on it, and Ambry sank below the rim of the world. The light came off the salt from below as well as above, so that there were no shadows anywhere, and even through the smoked glass the glare was a white hand laid flat on her eyes.

Then the Pull began. It started as a shimmer at the horizon, as on a road in summer, but it did not stay there. It came in. It lifted the far edge of the plain into the sky, so that the world curved upward at its rim like the inside of a bowl, and a line of low dark shapes appeared ahead, rocks or ships or a city, upside down, hanging above their own reflections.

"Pay it no mind," Coyle called. "That's Harl. Ninety miles off. The White likes to show you things."

"It's not real," said a first-year near Hester, a girl named Imke Dorran with a brown Bench cord.

"It's all real," Coyle said. "It just isn't where it says it is. You walk toward what you see out here, you'll walk till you die. Look at the dog."

Ballast had raised his head. He was regarding the upside-down city with contempt.

* * *

Orsolya came and stood at the rail beside Hester somewhere in the second half-hour. She did not hold on. She stood with her feet apart and her hands in her coat pockets and let the deck do what it liked under her.

"You've a face on you," she said, "like a girl at her first fair."

"I've never sailed."

"No. Lowside doesn't, much." Orsolya looked out at the glare. "My da took me out once. On a delvers' skiff, to the Coldbank cuts, when I was ten. I sat on a crate of picks and shrieked the whole way. He laughed till he cried." She was quiet. "Year after, the cut came in on him."

"I'm sorry."

"Long time ago, chick." She turned her head. Under the fur hat her face was weather-reddened and lined, and her eyes behind the goggles were pale and steady. "Choir's treating you right?"

"Yes."

"You're good. You know that? Ama says you've the deepest hold she's sat beside in ten years. Deep and square, like a well. She says you hold like you're carrying something up a ladder and you'll not put it down." She smiled. "That's not always a compliment, mind. But in the Choir it is."

Hester watched the white rushing past.

"How's your brother?" Orsolya said. "Still at the Cold House?"

"Yes."

"Coldwater Street." Orsolya nodded, looking out at the salt. "They're good there. That old Tider woman. She keeps them like a garden."

Hester turned her head.

She had not told Orsolya about the Cold House. She was nearly sure she had not. On the Causeway, the first morning, Orsolya had said *your brother's kept, Corvin says*, and in the Warden's office Hester had said *I've a brother who's kept*, and that was all. She had not said where.

But Aske had known where. Aske had counted the weeping casings himself, and Aske had been Orsolya's colleague for twenty years, and Readers talked. Corvin told her, Hester thought. He told everybody everything; he couldn't help it.

"She does," she said. "Agathe. Like a garden."

"Good." Orsolya patted the rail twice, as if it were a horse. "You hold on to that."

Then Coyle called *site in sight*, and she went forward.

* * *

The bloom-site lay five miles out from the Shelf on the north-eastern reach. From a mile off it was a faint discolouration in the white, as if someone had breathed on a mirror. From a quarter-mile off it was a stain. Coyle spilled the wind and dropped the brakes, and the *Gannet* came down off her hiss into a long shuddering scrape, and stopped. In the silence Hester heard the tiles ticking in the sun, and Teo, behind her, being quietly and thoroughly sick over the side.

"Into the wind," said Orsolya, without turning round. "Well done, Anselm."

They went down a rope ladder onto the salt in pairs, Coyle first with Ballast on a long lead. Orsolya laid out the rules again, flatly: walk in the dog's track and nowhere else; stop when he stops; touch nothing with a bare hand. Each of them carried a mixing flask on a strap across the chest, a squat brass thing with two chambers, grain in one and water in the other, divided by a wax seal. You cracked the seal with your thumb, shook it, and for about the time it took to say the Ebb-prayer you had live brine. After that it was salt water.

They walked in single file toward the stain.

A hundred yards out, the hard ringing crust, which gave back each step like a plank floor, went dull, then soft. The colour went from white to grey to a faint green-grey. Then she smelled it. It was a green smell, a wet smell, rotten-sweet like the Weeps at low water in summer, but not rotten: under it was something sharp and clean, the smell of a thing that is growing.

"Weed," said Orsolya, from the front. "That's what the old ballads called it. Don't ask me how they knew."

The site was a rough circle about forty yards across. In the middle the crust had gone dark and wet and soft, like snow in a gutter in Thaw. Small pools of brine stood in it, the size of plates, and every few breaths the surface of every pool shivered at once, as if something had been dropped into it from a height. Steam came off the dark crust faintly. Hester felt the warmth on her face from twenty feet off, as she had felt the light on Lantern Night.

"This is a bloom," Orsolya said. They stood in a half-ring at the edge of the dark crust, behind the dog. "A quickening that's come up near the top. We've a dozen like it on the north-east reach and three on the south. The Fifth's full of them, in the deep. Most never come up. When they do, they do this, and then they skin over in a month or a year and you'd never know." She crouched and put a gloved hand flat on the dark salt. "Feel it. Gloves on. Go on."

Hester put her gloved palm on the crust. It was warm through the wool. And under the warmth, so faint that she thought at first it was her own blood in her ears, there was a sound. A long slow hush, and a pause, and a hush. Like breathing. Like the noise in a shell held to the ear.

She had heard it in the Choir, in the deep, with sixty voices round her and the stay-line cold under her hands. *The sea.*

"What is it?" said Imke Dorran. Her voice was small.

"Pressure," Orsolya said. "Residue. Ask Reader Sorle and he'll tell you it's the Ebb trying to un-happen, and charge you a sol for the privilege." Some of them laughed. "It's grain that's gone quick. It's warm, it's wet, it moves, and if you see it on the White, you go round."

"And the sound?"

Orsolya was quiet, with her hand on the salt.

"The sound's the sound," she said. "Up you get."

Ballast whined.

Hester heard it because she was nearest. The dog had been standing at the end of his lead with his ears up. Now he did not lie down so much as fall, all four legs going out at once, chest and belly pressed to the salt, ears flat to his skull.

"*Freeze*," said Coyle.

Twenty-two aspirants froze. For a moment there was no sound but the tiles ticking and the dog whining and the long slow hush of the bloom.

Then the White opened under Lucan Ferrers.

* * *

It did not crack. There was no sound of breaking. Lucan was standing at the end of the half-ring, three paces to her left, a little forward of the line, where he had stepped to see the pool better. The crust under his boots simply went soft and down, like a sheet drawn off a bed. A hole the width of a cart opened in the white, black and wet, and he went down into it.

He did not cry out. His arms went up. His face, as it went, was surprised.

Hester had the flask off her chest before she knew she had moved. She cracked the seal with her thumb, shook it once, hard, and threw.

Not the flask. The brine. She flung it out of the open neck in a long flat arc, as she had flung slops across a yard every morning of her childhood. It went out of the brass in a sheet, glittering, and came down across Lucan's raised arms and head and shoulders and the black wet lip of the sink at the instant his chest went under the rim.

And she held.

There was no time to decide. She went down into the hold as you go down a stair in the dark, all at once, by the feel of your feet. Breath gone. Heart slowed to nothing. Every muscle set like mortar. She had kept a falling drop of brine in Crisp's class, a candle-flame, a moth. She had never kept anything larger than her hand. She had never kept a living person.

She kept him.

It came out of her like water through a broken sluice. The stillness poured from her into the brine, down her own arm, still outstretched from the throw, and across the air between them, and she felt the brine take it and set. Then there was a weight on her, enormous, as if she had caught the whole falling man by the wrists and was holding him over the drop with nothing under him but black.

When she could see again, Lucan Ferrers was hanging in the mouth of the sink.

A casing had set round him, rough and lumpy where the brine had splashed, clear in places and white in others. It ran from the top of his head to just below his ribs, and below that there was only black, wet, open nothing. His arms were up, the hands spread, and where the brine had splashed across the lip of the sink the casing had fused to it. It held him by its own edges at the rim of the hole, like a stopper in the neck of a bottle. His mouth was open. One lock of the wet-straw hair stood up from his head, held, mid-fall.

Then Orsolya was beside her, and Coyle was flat on his belly at the rim with a rope, and somebody in the half-ring was screaming, and Ballast was howling.

"*Don't touch him*," Orsolya was saying, sharp, not to Hester. "Coyle. Rope round the casing, below the arms. Don't you *touch* that rim. Anselm, Dorran, on the rope with him. Gloves." Then, close to Hester's ear: "Hester. Chick. Can you hear me?"

"Yes."

"Good. You kept him. You did right." Orsolya's voice was steady and fast. "Now listen. That casing's on the rim, and the rim's soft, and it's going to go. When it goes, the weight takes him down, and the sink skins over in a minute and he's under the White kept, and there's nobody on the Rim can get him out. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"So you've to return him. Now. While they've the rope on him."

The rim was going. A thin black crack had opened in the softened crust a hand's breadth from where the casing was fused, and wet was coming up through it. The casing tilted a quarter of an inch. Coyle, Teo, Imke and two others had the rope round it and were leaning back with their heels dug in, white-faced.

"Return him," Orsolya said.

Hester tried.

She knew how. Crisp had taught it again and again, and she had failed it nearly every time. You stayed in the hold; you put your attention on the keeping; you *let it go*. Crisp's word was *unclench*. Hester tried, and what came instead was the feeling of a fist closing tighter. Her hands were shaking out in front of her, and she could not tell if they were open or closed. If she let it go he would fall. That was the thing her body knew and would not be talked out of. She held.

"I can't."

"Yes, you can."

"He'll fall—"

"He'll not fall. They've the rope. Look at them, they've got him." The crack widened. The casing tilted another half-inch. The rope creaked. "Hester. Don't push it out. You're trying to shove it out of you like a cart. That's not how. You're holding him in your fist and trying to throw him, and you can't throw what you won't let go of." Orsolya's cold hand came down over Hester's shaking one. It did not grip. It lay there. "Open your hand."

"I—"

"Open your hand, chick. That's all. Just open it."

Orsolya's hand on hers was cold as stone and perfectly still. Hester thought, for no reason, of Saskia on her knees on the shop boards, not reaching, waiting with her hands on her thighs, while the child came across the floor at her own pace.

She opened her hand.

The weight went out of her all at once. The casing went with a sound like a pane of ice dropped on a stone floor, one great flat *crack* that came up through her boots and slapped her in the face. White dust burst from the sink's mouth, and out of the dust came a hot wind, hot as an oven door opening, that knocked her back on her heels. In the middle of it Lucan Ferrers, whole and wet and gasping, was hauled over the lip on the end of a rope and dragged across the soft crust on his belly, five yards, ten, coughing, alive.

Behind him the sink skinned over. She watched it happen: a slow wet white closing, like a mouth.

Lucan lay on his side on the hard salt with Coyle kneeling on him, making a sound she had not heard a man make before, a high, airless, astonished wheeze, one hand clutching at his side.

"Ribs," said Coyle. "Two at least. The snap. Hold still, sir, you've no breath to waste. Hold *still*—"

"Good," Orsolya said beside her. "Oh, that's good, chick. That's a lovely release. That's—"

Hester turned her head to look at her, and the world stopped.

* * *

It did not go dark. She did not faint, and there was no pain. There was just no time.

She was looking at Orsolya's face, at the cold red cheeks under the fur hat and the mouth open on *that's*. And then she was looking at the sky.

It was a different sky. The same hard blue, but the sun had moved; she could see it had moved. The few shadows there were had swung round, as shadows swing on a sundial. She was lying on her back on the salt with a coat under her head. Teo was kneeling beside her with her hand in both of his, his face grey under the freckles, his eyes wet. Lucan was not on the salt any more.

Her mouth was so dry her tongue stuck to her teeth.

"Water," said Teo, and she drank all of it, and it was not enough.

"What—"

"You lagged," Orsolya said. She was crouched on Hester's other side. "You kept a grown man, chick, first time, with a throw, and then you returned him, and the lag waited till you'd put the weight down. That's how it comes. Four minutes. You've been out four minutes."

Four minutes.

She lay on the salt and looked up at the moved sun. She had been here, all of her, eyes open; Teo said afterward that they had been open the whole time. Orsolya had talked to her and she had not answered. Teo had held her hand and it had been as cold and stiff as a gate-latch. And there was nothing. Not even the memory of an absence. Orsolya had said *that's*, and then the sun had moved.

She had stood beside Tib's casing a hundred and thirty-two times and told him the news. *Sere's in. Daisy's had another one.* She had never once in eleven years thought about what it was like for him, inside. Whether it was like this. Whether it was like anything at all. Whether, if they returned him tomorrow, he would think he had blinked.

She started to shake.

"Here." Orsolya was wrapping her in a blanket, in Coyle's coat. "It's the cold, chick. It's only the cold. It takes everyone like this the first time. Anselm, rub her hands. Not like that. Like you mean it."

Teo rubbed her hands like he meant it.

"Is he—"

"Ferrers? Two ribs, maybe three, and a bruise where his pride was. He's on the *Gannet*, telling Coyle how to splint himself."

Hester shut her eyes. That was a mistake. The dark behind her lids felt too much like the four minutes she did not have, and she opened them again at once.

"Four minutes," she said.

"Aye." Orsolya's hand rested on her hair, cold and light. "You'll not remember them. But you'll get used to there being a gap. Everyone below has a few." She looked out toward the bloom, steaming quietly in the sun. "I've lost about a month, all told, in thirty years. A minute here, an hour there. You stop counting."

* * *

They sailed home at a walking pace under a single reefed sail, because Coyle said the deck was full of invalids and he would not have them shaken. Lucan lay on a pallet by the mainmast with his chest strapped and Ballast's chin on his ankle. He did not look at her. Hester sat on a hatch-cover in two blankets, drinking from a flask Teo kept refilling, and did not look at him. She did not suppose there was a correct thing to say. She had kept the son of the Lord Exchequer in salt over a hole in the world and then dropped him on the White like a sack of meal, and broken him.

Halfway home, when the Halcyon was a needle on the horizon again, Lucan spoke without opening his eyes.

"Vail."

"What."

"You threw it. The brine. You didn't pour it, you threw it, like—" He breathed in carefully, around the ribs. "Like a scullery maid. With a bucket."

"Laundress," Hester said. "My mother was a laundress. Slops go across the yard."

He was silent a while.

"Remarkable," he said, and went back to sleep.

* * *

The aspirants scattered off the ramp quickly and quietly, as people leave a funeral. Orsolya took Lucan to the Physic ward herself on a litter. Before she went she put her cold hand on Hester's shoulder and said, "Hot bath. Hot food. Drink till you're sick of it. You did well, chick." Then she was gone down the Stair, and Teo was steering Hester up through the Rookery passages, talking the whole way about the probable theological status of a lurcher, and not once letting go of her elbow.

There was someone waiting at the door of R-41.

He was leaning on the passage wall in a black-corded gown that had been good once, with a stick in one hand and his white hair standing up in a fan, as though he had been out in the Sere without a hat. He was older than she had thought him from the lecture benches, and smaller. He looked her up and down, blankets, salt-crust, blue lips, without any expression at all.

"Reader Sorle," Teo said, and stopped walking.

Sorle looked at Hester.

"You kept a man," he said. "On the open White. With a *throw*."

She was too tired to be anything but honest. "Yes."

"Badly."

"Yes."

"Lumpy casing, splash-fused to the rim. A release like a cart going over a cliff. Two of the Lord Exchequer's son's ribs, which I gather the Lord Exchequer will want to discuss with somebody." He tilted his head. "And four minutes of lag, Coyle says, on a first keeping of a grown man. Some don't come back at all." He looked at her a moment longer, his mouth turned down at the corners in an expression she could not read. "Interesting."

He pushed himself off the wall with his stick.

"My study. Second Fathom, the black door at the end of the Horology gallery. The day after tomorrow, at the fourth bell. Don't be late. I've no patience and less time." He limped away down the passage, and stopped, and said over his shoulder: "And bring your tongue, Miss Vail. Corvin always said it was the best thing about you."

His stick tapped away toward the Stair.

"Did he just—" Teo began.

"I don't know," said Hester. She went into the cell and lay down on her bed in her boots and her coat and her two blankets, with her eyes open, looking at the salt ceiling, faintly pink in the last of the window light, and did not close them.
