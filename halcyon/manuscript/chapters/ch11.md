# Chapter 11

## The Sink

> The White does not forgive. The White does not hate. The White is a floor with a hole in it.
>
> — *Weathering field manual issued to first-year aspirants, the Halcyon, rev. 880 Y.E.*

The *Gannet* was a school skiff, which meant she was old, slow by the standards of the White, and built to forgive the stupid. She was a two-master, fifty feet from bowsprit to stern, with a broad flat deck and iron runners as thick as a man's thigh. She had been repainted so many times that the blue of her hull had layers in it, like the strata in a delver's cut. She lay on the salt ramp below the Halcyon's eastern Gatehouse at the second bell of the sixth of Fallow. Her sails were loose and slatting in a stiff north-easterly, and twenty-two first-year aspirants stood on the ramp beside her in borrowed wool coats and glare-goggles, looking at her the way cattle look at a river.

Hester had never been on a landship. She had spent twenty-one years at a desk above a harbour full of them. She had signed for their cargoes and tasted their holds, and she had never once set foot on a deck, because there had never been a reason, and because a single crossing to Quell cost more than she earned in a month. She looked at the *Gannet* and felt something she had not felt since she was a child: a plain, uncomplicated want.

"Tib would have given his teeth for this," she said, before she could stop herself.

"Then you'll have to enjoy it twice," said Teo, beside her. He was green already, and they had not moved. "I'm told one is sick. Copiously. Over the side."

"You're thinking of water."

"I'm thinking of the theology of motion. It's my area."

The Warden of the Lower Fathoms came down the ramp at a walk, in an iron-grey coat with the collar turned up and a fur hat that had seen better winters, and the aspirants straightened without being told.

"Right," said Orsolya Thane. "Morning, all. You've all read the manual. Forget it. Three rules on the White. One: you go where the pilot says and nowhere else. Two: if Ballast lies down, you stop. Three: nobody keeps anything unless I say so." She looked them over, all twenty-two. Her eye stopped on Hester for a moment, and something in it warmed. "And nobody's sick on my deck. Over the side. Into the wind is a lesson you only need once."

Ballast turned out to be a lurcher. He was a long grey dog with a rough coat and a face like an apologetic horse. He lay on a coil of rope by the *Gannet*'s mainmast with his chin on his paws and watched the aspirants board with an expression of deep weariness.

"Best sink-sniffer on the Shelf," the pilot said, a lean brown Fellow called Marten Coyle with a white weathering cord and a face cracked by glare. "Fourteen years on the White and never put a foot wrong. If he lies flat, you freeze, and you don't move till I tell you. He hears the crust go hollow before the crust knows it."

Lucan Ferrers was the last to board. He had a coat that fitted him and goggles of his own, tortoiseshell-rimmed. He stepped over the rail as though he had been doing it all his life, which, Hester supposed, he had. He nodded to her, briefly, the way he had nodded to her since Lantern Night: a nod with nothing in it but the fact of it. She nodded back.

Then Coyle shouted, and two Fellows on the ramp knocked out the chocks. The sails came round with a crack like a slammed door, and the *Gannet* began to move.

* * *

For the first hundred yards it was nothing much. The runners ground on the ramp and the deck jolted and Teo sat down suddenly on a hatch-cover. Then they came off the bottom of the ramp onto the open White, and the grinding stopped.

The sound changed first. The grind became a hiss, a long continuous rushing hiss under the hull, like silk being torn very slowly, and the jolting smoothed out into a hum. Hester felt it through the soles of her boots and up her shins. Then the wind took the mainsail properly, and Coyle put the helm over, and the *Gannet* leaned. The deck tilted under her, and the salt began to go by.

She had not known it would be so fast. She had watched landships come into harbour under bare poles, slowing, grinding up the ramps, and she had thought of them as heavy, deliberate things, like drays. The *Gannet* was not deliberate. The plain went by under her rail in a white blur. The ridges between the tiles flickered past so quickly they became a pattern and then a texture and then nothing at all, a smooth white rushing. The wind came over the bow and hit her in the face like cold water. It was full of fine salt that stung her lips and made her eyes run behind the goggles. Ahead there was nothing, no road, no rail, no edge; only the white going out flat and bright under a hard blue sky to a horizon that was not where it ought to be.

She laughed. She heard herself do it. She held the rail with both hands and laughed into the wind like a child on a swing, and did not care who heard.

"Nine knots," Coyle shouted from the helm, to nobody. "Ten. She'll make twelve on this reach. Old cow."

Twelve knots. Tib had told her once that the big freighters could make thirty on a broad reach in a winter gale. She had not believed him. She believed him now.

They ran east and a little north for most of an hour. The Halcyon fell away behind them to a white hump and then a white smudge with a needle on it. Ambry sank below the rim of the world. After that there was nothing at all to measure by. The sky was very large. The light came off the salt from below as well as from above, so that there were no shadows anywhere, not under the boom, not under Hester's own chin. She understood for the first time why the goggles were not a suggestion. Even through the smoked glass the glare was a pressure, a white hand laid flat on her eyes.

Then the Pull began.

It started as a shimmer at the horizon, the kind you see on a road in summer. But on the White the shimmer did not stay at the horizon. It came in. It lifted the far edge of the plain up into the sky, so that the world seemed to curve upward at its rim like the inside of a bowl. A line of low dark shapes appeared in the sky ahead. They were rocks, or ships, or a city, upside down, hanging in the air above their own reflections. They wavered and doubled and stretched into towers and broke apart into nothing.

"Pay it no mind," Coyle called. "That's Harl. Ninety miles off. The White likes to show you things."

"It's not real," said a first-year near Hester, a girl named Imke Dorran with a Bench cord of brown.

"It's all real," Coyle said. "It just isn't where it says it is. That's the Pull. You walk toward what you see out here, you'll walk till you die. Look at the dog."

Ballast had raised his head. He was looking at the upside-down city with an expression of contempt.

* * *

Orsolya came and stood at the rail beside Hester somewhere in the second half-hour. She did not hold on. She stood with her feet apart and her hands in her coat pockets and let the deck do what it liked under her, like a woman who had been doing this for thirty years.

"You've a face on you," she said, "like a girl at her first fair."

"I've never sailed."

"No. Lowside doesn't, much." Orsolya looked out at the glare. "My da took me out once. On a delvers' skiff, to the Coldbank cuts, when I was ten. I sat on a crate of picks and shrieked the whole way. He laughed till he cried." She was quiet a moment. "Year after, the cut came in on him."

"I'm sorry."

"Long time ago, chick." She turned her head. Under the fur hat her face was weather-reddened and lined, and her eyes, behind her goggles, were pale and steady. "Choir's treating you right?"

"Yes."

"You're good. You know that? Ama says you've the deepest hold she's sat beside in ten years. Deep and square, like a well. She says you hold like you're carrying something up a ladder and you'll not put it down." She smiled. "That's not always a compliment, mind. But in the Choir it is."

Hester did not know what to say to that. She looked at the white rushing past.

"How's your brother?" Orsolya said. "Still at the Cold House?"

"Yes."

"Coldwater Street." Orsolya nodded, looking out at the salt. "They're good there. That old Tider woman. She keeps them like a garden."

Hester turned her head.

She had not told Orsolya about the Cold House. She was nearly sure she had not. She had said *brother* once, in the Warden's office on the day she joined the Choir, when Orsolya asked her why a woman of thirty-three with a good stool at the Customs House would want to go below for three sols a week. She had said, *I've a brother who's kept*. She had not said where.

But Aske knew. Aske had known about the weeping, and Aske had been Orsolya's colleague for twenty years, and Readers talked. Corvin probably told her, Hester thought. He told everybody everything; he couldn't help it. She remembered him in his room in Quill Street, talking with a pen in each hand.

"She does," she said. "Agathe. Like a garden."

"Good." Orsolya patted the rail, twice, as if it were a horse. "Good. You hold on to that."

Then Coyle called *site in sight*, and she went forward.

* * *

The bloom-site was five miles out from the Shelf on the north-eastern reach. From a mile off it looked like nothing at all, a faint discolouration in the white, a smudge, as if someone had breathed on a mirror. From a quarter-mile off it looked like a stain. Coyle spilled the wind from the sails and dropped the brakes, and the *Gannet* came down off her hiss into a grind and then a long shuddering scrape, and stopped. In the silence Hester heard the tiles ticking in the sun, and the slap of a loose sheet, and Teo, behind her, being quietly and thoroughly sick over the side.

"Into the wind," said Orsolya, without turning round. "Well done, Anselm."

They went down a rope ladder onto the salt in pairs. Coyle went first, with Ballast on a long lead. Orsolya came after and laid out the rules again, flatly: walk in the dog's track and nowhere else; stop when he stops; touch nothing with a bare hand. Each of them carried a mixing flask on a strap across the chest. It was a squat brass thing with two chambers, grain in one and water in the other, divided by a wax seal. You cracked the seal with your thumb, shook it, and for about the time it took to say the Ebb-prayer you had live brine. After that the stillness leaked out of it and it was salt water.

"You'll not need them," Orsolya said. "You'll not need them because you'll not keep anything without my say. But the White doesn't ask my say. So you carry them."

They walked in single file toward the stain.

The salt changed under Hester's boots a hundred yards out. The hard ringing crust, which gave back each step like a plank floor, began to sound different. It went duller, then softer, the ring going out of it. The colour changed too, from white to a faint grey and then a faint green-grey. Then she smelled it.

She did not know what it was. It was a green smell, and a wet smell, and a rotten-sweet smell like the Weeps at low water in summer, but not rotten. Under the rot there was something sharp and clean and alive, the smell of a thing that is growing. It came up out of the salt in the sun. Every aspirant in the line lifted their head and breathed it in and looked at each other.

"Weed," said Orsolya, from the front. "That's what the old ballads called it. Weed. Don't ask me how they knew."

The site itself was a rough circle about forty yards across. In the middle the crust had gone dark and wet and soft, like the snow in a gutter in Thaw. It gleamed. There were small pools of brine standing in it, the size of plates, and the brine in them was moving. It was not running anywhere. It shivered, every few breaths, the surface of every pool trembling at once, as if something had been dropped into it from a height. Steam came off the dark crust in the cold air, faintly. It was warm. Hester felt it on her face from twenty feet off, as she had felt the light on Lantern Night.

"This is a bloom," Orsolya said. They stood in a half-ring at the edge of the dark crust, behind the dog. "A quickening that's come up near the top. We've a dozen like it on the north-east reach, and three on the south. The Fifth's full of them, in the deep. Most never come up. When they do, they do this, and then they skin over again in a month or a year and you'd never know." She crouched, carefully, and put a gloved hand flat on the dark salt. "Feel it. Gloves on. Go on."

They crouched. Hester put her gloved palm on the crust. It was warm through the wool. And under the warmth, so faint that she thought at first it was her own blood in her ears, there was a sound. A long slow hush, and a pause, and a hush. Like breathing. Like the noise in the shell held to the ear.

She had heard it before. In the Choir, in the deep, with sixty voices round her and the stay-line cold under her hands. *The sea.*

"What is it?" said Imke Dorran. Her voice was small.

"Pressure," Orsolya said. "Residue. Ask Reader Sorle and he'll tell you it's the Ebb trying to un-happen and charge you a sol for the privilege." Some of them laughed. "It's grain that's gone quick. That's all you need to know today. It's warm, it's wet, it moves, and if you see it on the White, you go round."

"And the sound?"

Orsolya was quiet for a moment, with her hand on the salt.

"The sound's the sound," she said. "Up you get."

Ballast whined.

It was not loud. Hester heard it because she was nearest. The dog had been standing at the end of his lead beside Coyle with his ears up. Now he flattened. He did not lie down so much as fall, all four legs going out at once, his chest and belly pressed to the salt, his ears back flat against his skull. He whined again, high and thin.

"*Freeze*," said Coyle.

Twenty-two aspirants froze. Hester felt the whole half-ring stop around her, held, every breath stopped. For a moment there was no sound but the tiles ticking and the dog whining and the long slow hush of the bloom.

Then the White opened under Lucan Ferrers.

* * *

It did not crack. That was the first thing she would remember afterward, and the thing that would wake her for weeks: it did not crack. There was no sound of breaking. Lucan was standing at the end of the half-ring, three paces to her left, a little forward of the line, where he had stepped to see the pool better. Then the crust under his boots simply went soft and down, like a sheet drawn off a bed. A hole the width of a cart opened in the white, black and wet, and Lucan went down into it.

He did not cry out. He did not have time. His arms went up. His face, as it went, was surprised.

Hester had the flask off her chest before she knew she had moved. She cracked the seal with her thumb, shook it once, hard, and threw.

Not the flask. The brine. She threw the brine out of the open neck in a long flat arc, as she had flung slops across a yard every morning of her childhood. It went out of the brass in a sheet, glittering, and came down across Lucan's raised arms and his head and shoulders and the black wet lip of the sink at the instant his chest went under the rim.

And she held.

She did not decide to. There was no time to decide. She went down into the hold the way you go down a stair in the dark, all at once, by the feel of your feet. Breath gone. Heart slowed to nothing. Every muscle in her body set like mortar. She had kept a falling drop of brine in Crisp's class; she had kept a candle-flame, a moth. She had never kept anything larger than her hand. She had never kept a living person.

She kept him.

It came out of her like water out of a broken dam. She felt it go. The stillness poured from her into the brine, through the air between them, down her own arm, which was still outstretched from the throw. She felt the brine take it and set. And then there was a weight on her, an enormous weight. It was as if she had caught the whole falling man in her two hands by the wrists and was holding him there over the drop, with nothing under him but black.

When she could see again, Lucan Ferrers was hanging in the mouth of the sink.

He was kept. A casing had set around him, rough and thick and lumpy where the brine had splashed. It was clear in places and white in others. It ran from the top of his head to just below his ribs, and below that there was only black, wet, open nothing. His arms were up, the hands spread, and the casing had caught the lip of the sink, where the brine had splashed across it. It had fused there. It held him by its own edges, at the rim of the hole, like a stopper in the neck of a bottle. His face inside it was still surprised. His mouth was open. One lock of the wet-straw hair stood up from his head, held, mid-fall.

Nobody moved.

Then Orsolya was beside her, and Coyle was flat on his belly at the rim with a rope, and somebody was screaming in the half-ring, and Ballast was howling.

"*Don't touch him*," Orsolya was saying, sharp, not to Hester. "Coyle. Rope round the casing, below the arms. Don't you *touch* that rim. Anselm, Dorran, on the rope with him. Gloves." Then, quieter, close to Hester's ear: "Hester. Hester, chick, can you hear me?"

"Yes."

"Good. That's good. You did it. You kept him. You did the right thing." Orsolya's voice was very steady and very fast. "Now listen. That casing's on the rim. The rim's soft. It's going to go. When it goes, the weight takes him down, and the sink skins over in a minute and he's under the White kept, and there's nobody on the Rim can get him out. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"So you've to return him. Now. Before the rim goes. While they've got the rope on him."

The rim was going. Hester could see it. A thin black crack had opened in the softened crust, a hand's breadth from where the casing was fused to the lip, and wet was coming up through it. The casing tilted. A quarter of an inch. Coyle, Teo, the girl Imke and two others had the rope round it, under Lucan's kept arms. They were leaning back on it with their heels dug into the salt, and their faces were white.

"Return him," Orsolya said.

Hester tried.

She knew how. She had been taught, in Crisp's class, again and again, and she had failed every time but twice. You were still in the hold; you put your attention on the keeping; you *let it go*. That was all. Crisp's word was *unclench*. Hester did not unclench. She tried, and what came instead was the feeling of a fist closing tighter. Her hands were shaking now, both of them, out in front of her, and she could not tell if they were open or closed. The weight was still on her. If she let it go he would fall. That was the thing her body knew and would not be talked out of. If she let him go he would fall. She held.

"I can't."

"Yes, you can."

"He'll fall—"

"He'll not fall. They've the rope. Look at them, they've got him." The crack in the rim widened. The casing tilted another half-inch. The rope creaked. "Hester. Listen to me. Don't push it out. You're trying to push it out of you like you're shoving a cart. That's not how. You're holding him in your fist and you're trying to throw him, and you can't throw what you won't let go of." Orsolya's cold hand came down over Hester's shaking one. It did not grip. It lay there. "Open your hand."

"I—"

"Open your hand, chick. That's all. Just open it."

She felt Orsolya's hand on hers, cold as stone and perfectly still. She thought, for no reason, of Saskia's mother's shop, and Saskia on her knees on the boards, not reaching, waiting with her hands on her thighs, while the child came across the floor at her own pace.

She opened her hand.

The weight went out of her all at once. The casing went with a sound like a pane of ice dropped on a stone floor: one great flat *crack* that came up through her boots and slapped her in the face. White dust burst out from the sink's mouth in a cloud. A hot wind came out of the cloud, hot as an oven door opening, and knocked her back on her heels. In the middle of it, Lucan Ferrers, whole and wet and gasping, was hauled up over the lip of the sink on the end of a rope. He was dragged across the soft crust on his belly, five yards, ten. He was coughing, and somebody was shouting, and he was alive.

Then the sink skinned over behind him. She saw it happen. It was a slow wet white closing, like a mouth.

Lucan was on his side on the hard salt with Coyle kneeling on him. He was making a sound she had not heard a man make before, a high, airless, astonished wheeze. One of his hands was clutching at his side.

"Ribs," said Coyle. "Two at least. The snap. Hold still, sir, you've no breath to waste. Hold *still*—"

"Good," Orsolya said beside her. "Oh, that's good, chick. That's a lovely release. That's—"

Hester turned her head to look at her, and the world stopped.

* * *

It did not go dark. That was what she would try to tell Teo afterwards and fail. It did not go dark, and she did not faint, and there was no pain. There was just no time.

She was looking at Orsolya's face, at the cold red cheeks under the fur hat and the mouth open on *that's*. And then she was looking at the sky.

It was a different sky. It was the same hard blue, but the sun had moved, she could see it had moved. The shadows had moved. The few there were had swung round a little, as shadows swing on a sundial. She was lying on her back on the salt. Someone had put a coat under her head. Teo was kneeling beside her with her hand in both of his. His face was grey under the freckles and his eyes were wet. Lucan was not on the salt any more. There were voices, a long way off, at the *Gannet*.

She tried to speak and found her mouth was so dry her tongue stuck to her teeth.

"Water," said Teo, and there was water, from somewhere, and she drank all of it, and it was not enough.

"What—"

"You lagged," Orsolya said. She was crouched on Hester's other side, her face very close. "You kept a grown man, chick, first time, with a throw, and then you returned him, and the lag waited till you'd put the weight down. That's how it comes. Four minutes. You've been out four minutes."

Four minutes.

She lay on the salt and looked up at the moved sun. She had been here, all of her, eyes open. They told her afterward that her eyes had been open the whole time. She had been looking at Orsolya, and Orsolya had talked to her and she had not answered. Teo had held her hand and it had been as cold and stiff as a gate-latch. And there was nothing. There was no memory of it. There was not even the memory of an absence. Orsolya had said *that's*, and then the sun had moved.

She had stood beside Tib's casing a hundred and thirty-two times and told him the news. *Sere's in. Daisy's had another one.* She had never once in eleven years thought about what it was like for him, inside. Whether it was like this. Whether it was like anything at all. Whether, if they returned him tomorrow, he would think he had blinked.

She started to shake.

"Here." Orsolya was wrapping her in something, a blanket, Coyle's coat. "Here, chick. It's the cold. It's only the cold. It takes everyone like this the first time. You'll be right in an hour. Anselm, rub her hands. Not like that. Like you mean it."

Teo rubbed her hands like he meant it.

"Is he—"

"Ferrers? He'll live. Two ribs, maybe three, and a bruise where his pride was." Orsolya's mouth twitched. "He's on the *Gannet*, telling Coyle how to splint himself. He'll live."

Hester shut her eyes. That was a mistake. The dark behind her lids felt too much like the four minutes she did not have, and she opened them again at once.

"Four minutes," she said.

"Aye." Orsolya's hand rested on her hair a moment, cold and light. "You'll get them back, after a fashion. You'll not remember them. But you'll get used to there being a gap. Everyone below has a few." She looked out across the salt, toward the bloom, steaming quietly in the sun. "I've lost about a month, all told, in thirty years. A minute here, an hour there. You stop counting."

* * *

They sailed home at a walking pace, under a single reefed sail, because Coyle said the deck was full of invalids and he would not have them shaken. Lucan lay on a pallet by the mainmast with his chest strapped and Ballast's chin on his ankle. He did not look at her. Hester sat on a hatch-cover wrapped in two blankets, drinking from a flask that Teo kept refilling, and did not look at him. Neither of them had any idea, she thought, what the correct thing was to say. She did not suppose there was a correct thing. She had kept the son of the Lord Exchequer in salt over a hole in the world and then dropped him on the White like a sack of meal, and broken him.

Halfway home, when the Halcyon was a needle on the horizon again, Lucan spoke without opening his eyes.

"Vail."

"What."

"You threw it. The brine. You didn't pour it, you threw it, like—" He breathed in carefully, around the ribs. "Like a scullery maid. With a bucket."

"Laundress," Hester said. "My mother was a laundress. Slops go across the yard."

He was silent a moment.

"Remarkable," he said, and went back to sleep.

* * *

The aspirants scattered when they came off the ramp, the way people scatter from a funeral, quickly and quietly. Orsolya took Lucan to the Physic ward herself, on a litter, with Coyle and two porters. Before she went she put her hand on Hester's shoulder, the cold hand, and said, "Hot bath. Hot food. Drink till you're sick of it. You did well, chick. You did very well." And then she was gone down the Stair, and Teo was steering Hester up through the Rookery passages to their cell. He talked the whole way about nothing: the dog, the Pull, whether Harl upside down had been an omen, the probable theological status of a lurcher. He did not once let go of her elbow.

There was someone waiting at the door of R-41.

He was leaning on the passage wall in a black-corded gown that had been good once, with a stick in one hand and his white hair standing up from his head in a fan, as though he had been out in the Sere without a hat. He was old, older than she had thought him in the lecture benches, and smaller. He looked her up and down, the blankets, the salt-crust on her coat, the lips she knew were still blue, without any expression at all.

"Reader Sorle," Teo said, and stopped walking.

Sorle did not look at Teo. He looked at Hester.

"You kept a man," he said. "On the open White. With a *throw*."

She was too tired to be anything but honest. "Yes."

"Badly."

"Yes."

"Lumpy casing, splash-fused to the rim. A release like a cart going over a cliff. Two of the Lord Exchequer's son's ribs, which I gather the Lord Exchequer will want to discuss with somebody." He tilted his head. "And four minutes of lag, Coyle says. On a first keeping of a grown man. Most people lag ten. Some don't come back at all." He looked at her a moment longer, with his mouth turned down at the corners in an expression she could not read. "Interesting."

Then he pushed himself off the wall with his stick.

"My study," he said. "Second Fathom, the black door at the end of the Horology gallery. The day after tomorrow, at the fourth bell. Don't be late. I've no patience and less time." He began to limp away down the passage, and stopped, and said over his shoulder, without turning: "And bring your tongue, Miss Vail. Corvin always said it was the best thing about you."

He went. Hester stood in the passage and listened to his stick tapping away toward the Stair.

"Did he just—" Teo began.

"I don't know," said Hester, and went into the cell and lay down on her bed in her boots and her coat and her two blankets. She lay with her eyes open for a long time, looking at the ceiling, which was salt, and faintly pink in the last of the window light. She did not want to close them.
