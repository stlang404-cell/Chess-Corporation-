# Chapter 12

## Sorle's Folly

> Reader Sorle's hypothesis was found to be without foundation in observation, unsupported by the arrears calculus as correctly applied, and an embarrassment to the Bench. The Conclave records its censure. The Reader is thanked for his candour.
>
> — *Minutes of the Conclave of Readers, 14 Cinder 871 Y.E.*

The black door at the end of the Horology gallery had no name on it. It had a brass knocker in the shape of a closed eye, and a card pinned beneath the knocker that said in a thin furious hand: *NOT NOW*. Hester knocked anyway, at the fourth bell exactly, because she had stood outside it for ten minutes waiting for the bell. The door opened before her hand had left the knocker.

"You're punctual," said Benedek Sorle. "I find that suspicious in a person. Come in. Don't touch anything. Don't sit there, that's Lise's chair. Sit there."

*There* was a three-legged stool beside a desk so heaped with paper that the desk itself was a rumour. Hester sat on it.

The study was long and narrow and very cold. It was one of the old Readers' rooms on the outer gallery of the Second Fathom, with a slit of salt-glass for a window, high in the wall. It let in a grey light that came from a light-shaft somewhere above and had been reflected too many times to have any warmth left in it. Every wall was shelves, and every shelf was books, and where there were no books there were slates. Dozens of slates, the size of tea-trays, stacked and leaning and propped. Every one was covered edge to edge in chalk: columns of numbers, curves, the strange compressed notation of the arrears calculus that Sorle wrote on the Black Bench board in lectures and that half the aspirants copied without understanding. Some of the slates had been rubbed half out and written over. Some had dates on them. One, propped against the coal-scuttle, said at the top *870*, and the chalk on it had gone yellow.

There was a fire, small and bad. There was a kettle on it.

On the desk, among the paper, under a glass bell the size of a teacup, there was a moth.

It was kept. She knew it before she looked properly; she could feel it from the stool, a small weight at the back of her eyes, as if somebody had laid a single grain of salt on each lid. It was a grey moth, an ordinary house-moth with soft dusty wings, held in a casing so fine and clear she could only see the casing's edges where it caught the grey light. Its wings were open. It had been kept mid-flight, a quarter-inch above the surface of the desk, so that the casing touched the wood only at one point, a sort of glass stalk. The moth hung above it, flying, going nowhere.

On the wall beside the window there was a portrait. It was a small oil, not well painted. A woman of forty or so with a broad face and her hair pinned up untidily, in a Physic's red cord, laughing at whoever was painting her.

"Lise," said Sorle, following her eyes. "My wife. Dead twenty-three years. The Settle. She was a Physic on the Brinery wards. Kept people for a living. I'm told I'm very bad company." He lowered himself into the chair that was not Lise's, which creaked. He had his stick across his knees. In the grey light his face was all lines and nose, and his eyes, deep under the white brows, were black and bright and unkind. "Tea?"

"Please."

He did not move to make it. He looked at her.

"Tell me about Ferrers," he said.

* * *

She told him about Ferrers. It took a long time, because he would not let her tell it.

"You threw it," he said. "Not poured. Threw."

"Yes."

"From how far?"

"Three paces. Four."

"So the brine was in the air for—what? A second? Less. And you went into the hold *before* it landed, or after?"

"I don't know. Before. As I threw."

"*Before.*" He leaned forward. "Before. So you were holding while the brine was in flight. You lent your stillness to a stream of brine in the air, which by any orthodox account should have been impossible, because the brine wasn't touching anything you were trying to keep. Except you, at one end, and Ferrers, at the other, and the air between. And by the time it reached him it was already setting. You were keeping the throw." He sat back. "Crisp will have a fit. Go on."

She went on. He stopped her again at the casing.

"Describe the grain."

"Rough. Lumpy where it splashed. Clear in the thick places, white where it was thin."

"Which way did it grow?"

She looked at him.

"Which way," Sorle said patiently, as to an idiot, "did the casing grow? From him outward, or from outside in?"

She thought. She saw it again, the glitter of the brine coming down in a sheet across his raised arms, the setting.

"From outside," she said. "In. It started where the brine landed and grew in toward him. The outside set first."

"Yes," said Sorle. "Yes, it did. Because you poured it. Or threw it, which is the same thing with more swagger. Every poured casing grows from the outside in, Miss Vail. It's the first thing Crisp should have told you, and the last thing anybody remembers, because nobody pours casings any more except on the Brinery wards, where they brush it on." He reached for the kettle, finally, with a hand that was not quite steady, and began to make tea. "Whereas when a worker keeps himself — and it can be done, it's been done, it's a stupid thing to do and Corvin did it every other month to test his own reading — the casing grows from the skin outward. It has to. The worker's lending his stillness from inside. It starts where he starts. It grows out from him like rime from a twig."

He poured. The tea came out black. He put a pinch of salt in hers without asking and handed it to her.

"Which way," he said, "did Corvin Aske's casing grow?"

Hester held the cup in both hands. It was very hot. She did not drink.

She saw it. She had stood in the corridor outside Cell 3-17 in the press of gowns at first bell on the seventeenth of Russet, looking at a thin man held standing in the middle of his own workroom with one arm reaching. She had looked at the casing with her assayer's eye, because there was nothing else she could do with it. She had seen the grain, and the way it lay, and she had thought, before the Proctor's voice came down on all of it, *that's wrong*. And then she had put the thought away, because there was a Proctor and a verdict and a hundred people in the corridor, and she was nobody.

"In," she said. "From outside. It was poured."

Sorle nodded slowly, as if she had passed a test he had not expected her to pass.

"Corvin couldn't keep himself in a pickle jar," he said. "Not a whole-body casing. Not like that. He'd sleeve a hand to test his reading, or keep a finger, and come to my door with his hand in a block of salt like a boy with his fist stuck in a vase, wanting me to release it, because his release was worse than yours. And his hold was radial. Fine and radial, like a star. Every casing he ever made had a little starburst in the grain where he started it." He drank his tea. "That casing in 3-17 has no starburst. It was poured, from outside. By somebody else. A child could see it. Gage saw it. Gage isn't a child; he's a soldier, which is worse, because a soldier knows when he's been told to stop looking."

"Then why—"

"Why didn't I say so?" Sorle put the cup down. "I did. I said so to Gage on the morning of the seventeenth, and I said so in writing to the Deputy Chancellor on the eighteenth, and I said so to the Conclave on the twenty-second, where I was thanked for my candour." The word came out of him like something spat. "*Thanked for my candour.* They've a phrase for everything in Conclave, Miss Vail. That one means *sit down, Benedek, nobody believes a word you say*. They've been thanking me for my candour for thirty-two years."

* * *

He told her about the sixteenth of Russet without being asked.

"Corvin came to my door that afternoon," he said. "Third bell. He'd a sheet of figures in his hand and ink on his face, which was usual, and he was frightened, which wasn't. He wanted me to look at some numbers. Pressure figures. Stay returns from the Fifth over five years. He wanted me to run them through the calculus for him, because Corvin's sums were like his release; he got there, but you wouldn't want to watch. I said I was busy. I was busy. I was writing a reply to a Fellow in Harl who'd published something stupid." His mouth turned down. "He said: come to my Cell tonight, then. The tenth bell. Hester Vail's coming at the tenth; come at half-past. I'll show you both, and you can tell me I'm mad in person. I said I'd think about it."

"And you came."

"I came. At the half after the tenth. I don't sleep. I thought I might as well be insulted by Corvin as by the Harl Fellow." He turned the cup in his hand. "The Third was empty. The corridor was dark. His door was shut and there was no light under it. I knocked. Nobody. I thought he'd gone up to bed and forgotten, as he did; he'd forget his own name if it weren't written on his gown. So I went back down to the Second."

"Down?"

"Down the Stair. My rooms are below his." He looked at her. "Why?"

"Because I was there. At the tenth. I waited outside the door half an hour and he didn't come. And while I waited—" She stopped. She had told Teo this, and Teo had shrugged, and she had stopped thinking about it. "While I waited I heard someone on the Stair. Below. Coming up from the Fourth, or the Fifth. Heavy. Something on wheels, dragging, a trolley, bumping on every step. I thought it was a porter with brine casks."

"At the tenth bell? On the Stair?" Sorle's eyes had gone very narrow. "The porters use the lifts after the eighth. The Stair's for feet. Nobody drags a trolley up the Stair. It's a hundred and nine steps from the Fourth to the Third, and they're worn into bowls."

"Then who—"

"Somebody who didn't want to use a lift," Sorle said. "Lifts have lift-men. Lift-men have mouths." He sat back. "And when did you leave?"

"Before the half. A quarter before. I was cold, and I was angry. I went up."

"Up the Stair."

"Yes."

"And the trolley?"

"I didn't hear it again. It had stopped. Somewhere below."

They sat. The fire made a small sound. On the desk the moth hung above its stalk of glass, flying.

"So you went up at a quarter to," Sorle said slowly, "and I came up from the Second at the half, and the corridor was dark, and his door was shut. And at first bell a porter found him in the middle of his workroom in a poured casing of Hush grain with his arm out. And between a quarter before the half and first bell, somebody brought something up the Stair on a trolley, from below, and didn't use a lift." He looked at the slates on the wall as if they had personally failed him. "Well. I don't have a sum for that."

"He wasn't in his Cell," Hester said. It came out of her flatly, the way her grades came out. "When I knocked. He wasn't there. He'd gone below. Something happened below. And then he was brought back up."

Sorle said nothing for a long time.

"Yes," he said at last. "I think that's very likely."

* * *

"What's the Held Moment?"

She asked it because she had to ask something, and because the slate by the coal-scuttle with *870* on it had been looking at her for an hour. Sorle's face did a complicated thing.

"Do you want the version they teach second-years as a cautionary tale," he said, "or the version that's true?"

"The one you believe."

"That's not the same as the one that's true. Nobody in this building knows the one that's true. That's my whole complaint." He levered himself up out of the chair with the stick and went to the coal-scuttle and picked up the old slate and blew the dust off it and looked at it, as a man looks at a photograph of himself young. "All right. Here it is. Grain keeps. Why?"

"Nobody knows."

"Nobody knows. Correct. The mineral school says stillness is a property of the crystal formed under pressure at the Ebb, like hardness, or colour. That's not an answer, that's a description with a hat on." He put the slate on the desk in front of her, beside the moth. "Look at this."

It was a curve. A long curve, chalked in a firm young hand, rising slowly and then more steeply, labelled at intervals with depths in fathoms and with numbers she did not understand.

"Stillness by depth," Sorle said. "Every grade, every boring, every reference jar from the Shelf to the deepest the Fathomers had reached in 870. I spent four years getting the numbers. Grain gets stiller as you go down. Everybody knew that. But nobody had ever plotted it. Look at the shape."

She looked.

"It's not a straight line," she said.

"No. If stillness were a property of crystal formed under pressure, it ought to go up with depth more or less in a line: more pressure, more stillness. It doesn't. It goes up like *that*." He drew his finger along the curve, up into the steep part. "It goes up like the arrears on a keeping. Exactly like. Mass times duration times liveliness. The same curve you get if you plot the stillness of a casing against the size and life of what it's keeping." He tapped the slate. "Grain from the Hollow Sea has the stillness curve of a *keeping*, Miss Vail. Not a mineral. A keeping. The deeper you go, the more it behaves like the casing round something enormous, and alive."

Hester sat on the stool and looked at the curve.

"So I stood up in Conclave," Sorle said, "in Cinder of 871, thirty-two years old and stupid with it, and I said: the sea didn't go. The sea's still there. It's held. The salt is the casing. The Ebb wasn't the sea leaving; it was the sea being kept, all at once, in one instant, by God knows what. And every dram we dig and dissolve, we're spending a bit of that held moment, like lamp-oil."

He put the slate back against the scuttle.

"They laughed," he said. "Not all of them. Some of them were very polite. And then a young Fathomer stood up — twenty-six, Lowside, sharp as a delver's pick, three years in the Choir and a gift for sums like nothing I'd ever seen — and took me apart. In public. In an hour." There was something in his voice that was not bitterness, or not only. "She showed that my numbers from below the Fourth were bad. They were; the borings were contaminated, I'd been careless. She showed that my arrears terms were wrong at the top of the curve. They were; I'd used Halloway's constants and Halloway was an ass. She showed that if the Hollow Sea were a keeping, the arrears on nine hundred years of a sea would be so large that the crust should be warm to the touch from here to Quell, and it isn't. And she was right about all of it." He sat back down in the chair. "She was right about my maths. She was wrong about the world. And I couldn't prove that, and I still can't, and that's the whole of my career. Orsolya Thane."

Hester's cup was cold in her hands.

"The Warden," she said.

"She wasn't Warden then. She was a chorister with a slate. She made her name that day. I lost mine." Sorle looked at the moth. "We haven't exchanged forty words since. She runs the deep like a ship's captain and she's very good at it, and she thinks I'm a dangerous old fool, and she may be right. She's right about most things. That's what makes her unbearable."

Hester thought about the bloom on the White, and the warm crust under her glove, and the hush and pause and hush in it. She thought about Orsolya crouched beside it with her hand flat on the salt, saying *the sound's the sound*.

"The bloom-sites are warm," she said.

Sorle looked up.

"On the White. The quickenings. When they come up. They're warm. I had my hand on one two days ago. And there's a sound in them." She stopped. "And in the Choir. When we stay. You hear it through the line. Everybody does. They call it the Roar. But it's not a roar." She did not know how to say it. She had not said it to anyone, not even Teo, not properly. "It has a shape. It goes up and down. Like breathing. Like—"

"Like waves," Sorle said quietly.

She had never seen waves. Nobody on the Rim had seen waves. She had seen the pictures in the tavern signs and the drawings in the ballad-books, curling white lines like a row of fingers.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never heard waves."

"No," said Sorle. "No. None of us have."

* * *

He would not sponsor her. She had not asked; she had come to his door a fortnight ago and asked, and he had said *I don't take charity cases from the dead*, and she had not forgiven him for it. She did not ask again. He told her anyway, at the door, as she was going, as if she had.

"I'll not take you onto my Bench on a story," he said. "Not on a taste and a trolley and a casing that grew the wrong way. I've been thanked for my candour for thirty-two years, Miss Vail, and I'll not be thanked for yours. If I stand up in Conclave again, I'll stand up with something I can put on a slate." He leaned on his stick in the doorway, small and old and furious. "Bring me evidence. Not tastes. Evidence. A thing I can weigh. A thing Gage can't lose."

"And then you'll sponsor me."

"And then," said Sorle, "I'll think about it." He began to close the door. Then he stopped it with his stick and said through the gap, in a different voice: "You lagged four minutes. On a first keeping. Do you know how many people on the Shelf could have done what you did on the White?"

"No."

"Nor do I. I'd like to find out. That's all." And he shut the door, and the card that said *NOT NOW* swung on its pin.

* * *

The Physic ward of the Halcyon was on the Third Fathom, behind the Glaziers' kilns, in a long low room that smelled of camphor and warm brine. It had a row of iron beds along one wall and a row of shallow baths along the other, sunk in the floor. They were tiled in white and steaming faintly, for the slow returns. Two of the baths had kept people in them, lying under a hand's depth of warm brine, their casings softening by degrees. A Physic in a red cord sat between them on a stool, reading a book and touching each casing every few minutes with two fingers, like a woman testing bread.

Lucan Ferrers was in the end bed by the wall, propped on pillows, with his chest strapped and a book open face-down on the blanket. He was looking at the ceiling. When he saw her he did not look away, which she had half expected. He watched her come down the length of the ward with no expression at all.

"Vail," he said.

"Ferrers."

"Have you come to see the damage?"

"I've come because Teo said if I didn't he'd bake me nothing till Midwinter." She stood at the end of the bed with her hands in her gown. "How many?"

"Three." He breathed in carefully. "Two cracked, one broken. The Physic says I'm to lie still for a fortnight and think about my sins. I've told her I haven't enough to fill a fortnight. She wasn't amused." He looked at her. "My father's written to the Chancellor."

"About me?"

"About the *Gannet*. About the Warden letting first-years walk a bloom-site in Fallow. About the dog, possibly; I didn't read the whole letter. My father writes letters the way other men break furniture." He moved a hand on the blanket. "Your name is not in it. I asked him to leave it out."

She had not expected that. She did not know what to do with it.

"Why?"

He was quiet for a while. The Physic between the baths turned a page.

"I've never owed anybody anything," Lucan said. He said it to the ceiling. "Do you understand? Not once. Not in my life. Everything I've ever had was either mine already or bought. Even my tutors were paid in advance. And then I went through the floor of the world in front of twenty people, and the last thing I saw was you, with a brass flask in your hand, and your face." He stopped. "You had a face like somebody catching a pot off a shelf. Annoyed. As if I'd fallen on purpose to inconvenience you."

"You had," said Hester. "You stepped forward. Coyle said walk in the dog's track. You stepped forward to see the pool better. You always step forward to see better."

His mouth moved. It was nearly a smile.

"Yes," he said. "That's fair." He looked at her at last. "Thank you. That's what I'm trying to say. I'm bad at it. I've never had to practise. Thank you."

"You're welcome," Hester said, and found that she was bad at it too, and that her face was hot. She looked at the baths. "It was a lumpy casing. Sorle says."

"Sorle." Lucan's eyebrows went up. "You've been to see Sorle?"

"He asked."

"He never asks anyone anything. He tells them they're wrong." He paused. "What did he want?"

She looked at him. She looked at the Physic, who was absorbed in her book. She looked down the length of the ward toward the door. Then she sat down on the edge of the bed, carefully, so as not to jog him. She told him, very low, about the casing that grew from the outside in, and the trolley on the Stair at the tenth bell, and Sorle at the half.

Lucan listened without interrupting. When she had finished he lay with his eyes on the ceiling for a long time.

"Cistern maintenance," he said at last. "Forty firkins of Deep brine. Charged to the Halcyon. And a drain in every row of that ward running east."

"Yes."

"And Sorle wants evidence."

"Something he can weigh. Something Gage can't lose."

Lucan turned his head on the pillow and looked at her, and his face was not smooth at all.

"Then we'll have to go and get some," he said. "Won't we."

"*You*'re lying still for a fortnight."

"I'll lie still for a week. I'm a fast healer. It's the only thing about me my father admires." He shut his eyes. "Go and find out how to get under that building, Vail. I'll find out how to pay for it."

She sat a moment longer on the edge of his bed, in the warm camphor air, with the steam coming off the baths and the Physic turning her pages. Then she got up and went out, up the Third Fathom corridor toward the Stair. She passed the shut door of Cell 3-17, which had a Proctory seal across it now, red wax on black ribbon. She did not stop. She had seen the map in the Brinestacks. She knew where the Great Cistern lay. She knew which street was over it, and whose cellar would have the door.

She went up the Stair to find Teo, already counting, in her head, what a lantern would cost, and a rope, and a saw that would cut brass.
