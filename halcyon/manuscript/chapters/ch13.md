# Chapter 13

## The Cistern

> The founders' cistern beneath Coldwater and the Eastern Wards is bricked, and shall not be reopened, its brine being unwholesome and its passages unsafe.
>
> — *Minute of the Ambry Water Board, 400 Y.E.*

Lucan Ferrers brought a saw.

He brought it up the Rookery stair at the ninth bell on the thirteenth of Fallow, wrapped in a good wool blanket, and laid it on Hester's bed as if he were laying out a body. It was a short bow-saw for metal, with a rosewood frame.

"Where did you get this?"

"From the estate carpenter at Ferrers House. He was asleep." Lucan sat down on Teo's bed without being asked, which he had begun doing, and held himself upright, which he had not stopped doing since the sink. The ribs were mending; she could see it in his breathing, shallow at the top and careful at the bottom. "I left him a note. And a sol. I think he'll consider himself well paid for a saw that cuts brass like cheese."

"You think we'll need to cut brass."

"I think you think so, or you wouldn't have asked me for plans." He took a roll of paper out of his coat. "The Water Board survey of 398, two years before they bricked it. My father keeps the originals of everything that might one day be worth money. I traced them last night. Don't look at the lettering."

Teo spread the roll on the boards between the beds and weighted the corners with his boots and a plate of saltcake, and they bent over it, all three, in the light of the one lamp.

Hester had seen the shape before, in faint red on a mouse-eaten sheet in the Lattice gallery: the long ribbed oval under the foot of Coldwater Street, the line running out of it straight as a rule. This was the same thing drawn by a man who had walked it. *Great Cistern: capacity 40,000 tuns*, in heavy ink, ribbed across with vaulting. A short passage ran west from it, up under the street, to a square marked *Excise Store — cellar — inspection grate*. And from the cistern's eastern end the double line ran out under the sea-wall and the dotted shore and on across two feet of blank paper that was the White. The draughtsman had written along it every few inches, in a small precise hand: *Main. Main. Main.* Where the paper stopped he had written: *to the Collegium. Not surveyed beyond.*

"It isn't a pipe," Lucan said, pointing to an inset: a horseshoe of brick with a man drawn in it for scale, his hat brushing the crown. "It's a tunnel. You could drive a pony down it."

Hester put her finger on the square marked *cellar*. She had walked over that spot on the first of every month for eleven years.

"Agathe said yes?" said Teo.

"Agathe said yes."

She had written on the ninth, and the answer had come with the carrier on the eleventh, in Agathe's round copybook hand. *Mr. Pell is to go to his sister's in Harl on the fourteenth for three nights, it being her lying-in. The cellar door is mine to keep, as the oil is kept there. I do not ask what you want with the cellar, my dear, and I do not want to know, except that if it is to do with the weeping I shall pray for you, and if it is not I shall pray for you anyway. Come by the yard. Eleventh bell. A.L.*

"She's seventy," Lucan said. "And a Tider. And you're asking her to let three Halcyon aspirants into a bricked civic works in the middle of the night. If it's found out, she'll lose her place."

"I know."

"Does she?"

"Yes," said Hester, "she does," and Lucan looked at her a moment longer and nodded, as though she had passed some assay of his own.

They planned it on the floor, over the plan. Three kept-light lamps, which needed no air and would not go out if the tunnel was foul. Rope. A crowbar. The saw. Water, because Hester did not intend to come out of that hole without doing grain-work. A tin box lined with oilcloth for anything they took. Teo's chalk.

"And if somebody's down there?" Teo asked.

"Then we come back up," said Hester.

"And if they don't let us?"

Lucan touched his ribs lightly. "Then I'll tell them who my father is," he said. "It's nearly the only thing I'm good for."

* * *

The fourteenth of Fallow was a dark night with no moon and a hard thin wind out of the north. They took the last Causeway dray into the city at the tenth bell, on the tailboard among sacks of Halcyon laundry, and by the eleventh bell they were in the yard behind the Cold House among the oil-drums and empty brine casks, and Agathe was standing in the cellar door with a candle, in her grey shawl, looking exactly as she looked at the second bell of the morning.

"You'll be the Ferrers boy," she said to Lucan.

"I am, madam."

"You've your mother's chin. She came here once, to give money. She stood in the ward and wept like a child." Agathe looked at Teo. "And you're the one from the Abbey. They'll have taught you to be quiet." She stood aside. "Mind the step. There's eleven of them and the sixth is gone."

The cellar was low and long and smelled of lamp-oil and old brick. Oil barrels stood in racks along one wall, the brinewright's stores along the other. At the far end, where the cellar ran under the charity ward, iron gratings the size of a door were set flush with the brick.

Agathe stopped by the last of them and lowered her candle.

"I come down here for the oil every morning," she said. "Thirty-one years. And in the spring, about the time the first of them started weeping, this grating was different. Somebody had had it up." Round its edge the mortar had been raked out and renewed, pale and clean against the old black iron. "I asked Mr. Pell. He said it was Halcyon maintenance on the old cistern taps, under the Charter, and no business of mine." Her mouth did not move. "I've kept the oil thirty-one years. I know what's my business."

Lucan went down on one knee, carefully, favouring the ribs, and put his fingers to the mortar.

"This has been opened more than once," he said. "They've raked it out and reset it, and raked it out again. There's three colours."

"Then they come and go," said Teo.

"One of them was a Reader," said Agathe.

Hester looked up.

"Yours, my dear. Reader Aske." Agathe's candle did not waver. "He came in Russet to count the weeping, and I walked him round. Then he came back on the twelfth, after dark, by the yard, as you have, and asked me for the cellar key. Mine, not Mr. Pell's; there's only the two. He said it'd go easier if Mr. Pell didn't hear of it. I gave it him." She looked at the grating. "He was down two hours. He came up white to the lips with his boots wet, and gave me back my key, and said, *Mother, I'll tell you when I'm sure.* Five days after, the Board had a letter from the Halcyon saying he was dead."

Hester had known that Aske had counted the casings. She had not known he had been under them.

"You never said."

"You never asked me about the cellar till the ninth. And he asked me not to tell anybody, and I'd no notion who *anybody* was." Agathe's voice stayed gentle. "I've thought since that I should have. Tide forgive me, I didn't know what to be afraid of."

"Then we're going where he went," said Hester. She took the crowbar off her shoulder.

The new mortar came out in crumbs, and the grating, lifted lately, wanted to lift. When they had it up and leaning against the wall, a breath came out of the hole beneath it. It was too slow for a draught. The air simply moved, upward, past their faces.

It was cold. It was sweet. Hester knew it before it reached her mouth: the cellar-of-apples sweetness of the charity ward on the six worn steps. And under the sweetness, far down at the back of the tongue, the thing she had come for.

Quick.

Agathe stood with her candle and looked at Hester's face.

"Go on, then," she said. "I'll not leave the hole." And then, which Hester had not expected: "Tide carry you."

* * *

An iron ladder went down twelve feet into a sloping passage, just high enough to walk in if you stooped, that ran east perhaps thirty yards. Then the walls fell away on either side, and the lamps that had been filling the passage with amber were lighting nothing at all.

Lucan opened his shutter wide, and the light went out and up and up and found, at last, a ceiling.

The Great Cistern was a hall. It had been built to hold water, and had held it for a hundred years before the Ebb, and had held nothing for nine hundred since. Rows of brick piers as thick as houses marched down its length, each rising into a vault, so that the roof was a field of shallow domes, like the underside of a quilt. At about twice a tall man's height a pale line ran round every pier and every wall, exactly level. It was where the water had stood.

Down the centre of the dry brick floor, from the west wall to an east wall they could not see, ran a gutter of dressed stone a foot wide and a foot deep. The channel was not dry.

It was full of brine. It lay in the stone black and motionless in the lamplight, with a skin on it like the skin on milk, and the sweetness came up off it so thick she could taste it with her mouth shut.

"That," said Teo quietly, "is live."

"Yes."

"It shouldn't be. Live brine dies in minutes." He crouched, not touching. "Somebody's keeping it alive."

"Nobody's keeping it," Hester said. "It's being fed."

She did not say what with. She knew what she thought, and she would taste it before she said it. You did not grade by hoping.

They followed the channel east.

The brick swallowed every sound. Somewhere far off something dripped, with a long interval between each drop, and after a while Hester found she was counting the intervals as she counted coins.

Every twenty yards a smaller gutter came into the channel from the north side, from under the street. Each arm ran back to the foot of a pier, and above each pier, set into the vault, was a round iron grating. Hester stopped under the third and held up her lamp. From the grating, eight or nine feet over her head, hung a rod.

It was brass, perhaps an inch thick, and it was new: bright, unscratched, with none of the green that brass took in damp. It came down out of the grating and ran down the pier in iron clips, then bent and ran along the floor, sleeved for its whole length in a trough of hammered tin. The trough was full of the black brine, and the rod lay in it like an eel in a river. It joined the arm. The arm joined the main.

Once she had seen one she could see them all, glinting under every grating along the north side of the hall. She walked down the row with her lamp and counted.

Fourteen.

"Hester," said Lucan.

She had stopped under the seventh, at the iron clip that held the rod to the pier. On its plate was a small clean punch-stamp: a square grid, three by three, like a window, or a crystal of grain seen end-on, and under it the number *23*.

"That's a Lattice mark," said Lucan behind her. His voice was strange. "The Lattice Yards. Third Fathom. They stamp everything they make. My father has a gate of theirs on the Exchequer Yard, and it's on the hinges."

"Halcyon work," said Teo.

"Halcyon work," said Lucan.

Hester took off her right glove.

She had been an assayer since she was nineteen, and what an assayer does with a thing she does not understand is put it on her tongue. She knelt and put the tip of her smallest finger into the black brine. It was so cold it burned. She put the finger in her mouth.

The weight came down on her like a roof.

She had tasted the Deep at a hundred and forty, and the *Constant Wren*'s cargo, and the First Taste on the Long Table that made a hall of adults weep. None of them was this. This did not press. It *settled*. It came down behind her eyes and lay there and went on not moving. Its stillness was so old and so complete that for a moment she could not tell if she was breathing.

It was stiller than grain. It was stiller than anything that came out of the ground, because the ground had not put it there. Ten, twenty, thirty years of a life held still had put it there, the stillness soaking into the salt around a body, day after day, until the salt was saturated. *Old keepings are the stillest things in the world.* Sorle had said it in the Black Bench with his chalk, and she had written it down without understanding it.

And under the stillness, faint, as there had been on the bead on Tib's casing: the bright wet startle of the brine around it, moving. Being drawn.

She took her finger out of her mouth.

"This is them," she said.

She was on her knees on the brick, and her voice was the flat level voice she had used to read a grade aloud to the excisemen, and she held on to it. "This is what's weeping off the casings. It's going down the drains into these rods, and the rods are taking it to the channel, and the channel's taking it east." She swallowed. The weight was still behind her eyes. "This is Tib."

* * *

Teo knelt beside her. He put out his hand, and she nodded, and he dipped one finger in the black brine and put it, not in his mouth, but against the hollow under his ear where the bone was thin, as Reader Fane taught her Bench to listen. He shut his eyes.

It took him longer than it took her. His hold had always been bad. She watched his breath try to slow and fail and try again. Then it caught. The colour went out of his lips. His face emptied, as faces did in the hold, all its quick warmth draining away until it was only a face.

He was gone long enough that Lucan shifted and looked at her, and she shook her head.

When Teo came out he came out slowly, like a man waking in a cold room. He sat back on his heels. He was crying, and she did not think he knew it. The tears left white tracks on his cheeks.

"What did you hear?"

"The Roar. Like always. But it's far off. Thin. It's not the sea in this. It's not old enough." He wiped his face with his sleeve and looked at the white on the wool. "Under it. Under the Roar." He stopped.

"Teo."

"Breathing," he said. "People breathing. Lots of them. All at once and none of them together. Like a ward at night. Like the infirmary at the Abbey when the fever came through and I sat up with the brothers." He looked at her. "And some of it's small. Quick and small and light. Hester, some of it's children."

The brine lay in its sleeve, black and perfectly still, and fed, and was fed upon.

Lucan said, in the voice he used in Vey's Brinescript class to say something rude politely: "Then I think we had better take one with us."

He unwrapped the saw.

* * *

Lucan chose the length of the seventh rod between the last clip and the bend, where it stood clear of its sleeve. Hester held the lamp. Teo held the rod above the cut with both gloved hands, wincing at the cold through the leather.

"If this is full," said Lucan, "it'll spill."

"Then it spills."

"It's *people*, you said."

"It's what's been taken off them. It's no more them than the water you wring out of a shirt is the shirt. What's in that rod is going east whatever we do. Cut it."

He looked at her a moment, then put the blade to the brass and drew it.

The saw went into the brass with a high thin singing that the brick swallowed at once, so that the noise was in their ears and nowhere else, as if the three of them were inside a bell no one outside could hear. Halfway through, the rod began to sweat. Beads stood out on it above and below the cut, in lines. They ran away from the cut along the rod, east, toward the bend, toward the channel.

"Still runs to quick," Teo whispered.

"It's going where it's going," said Hester. "Keep cutting."

Lucan's breathing had gone short. He stopped with his hand pressed flat to his side, and Hester took the saw from him and finished it herself. The last of the brass parted with a little *tink*. The downstream end gave a single slow bubble, and brine welled out of it, black and cold, and she caught it in the oilcloth of the tin box. A spoonful. The rod was hollow, she saw: a brass tube bored fine, with brine running down its heart.

They cut again a foot lower, quicker now that they knew how. When it parted she had in her hands a foot of brass tube as thick as two fingers, so cold she felt it through her gloves, with a stamped band round its middle: the three-by-three lattice and the number *23*.

She was wrapping it in the oilcloth when the light came round the pier.

* * *

It was a tin lantern with a horn window, held high by a man breathing hard through his nose. In his other hand he had a carter's oak cudgel. He was heavy, pale and balding, in a coat buttoned wrong. Hester had seen him perhaps five times in eleven years, always leaving.

"Mr. Pell," she said.

"Who's there?" His face in the lantern-light was grey and wet. "You've no right—this is a closed works—this is *Halcyon property*—"

"You're at your sister's," Hester said. "In Harl."

"I came back." He had seen the tin box in her hands, and the saw, and the cut ends of the rod with the black brine welling slowly out of the stub. His face changed. "Oh, God. What've you *done*? They'll know. They'll know straight off. They always know. Put it *back*—"

He came at her. He held the cudgel as a man holds a stick he is afraid of, but he was heavy and frightened, and frightened men do not stop. Hester stepped back and her heel went into the channel. The brine took her boot to the ankle, so cold she cried out. Teo grabbed at Pell's coat, and Pell flung him off, and Teo went down hard on the brick with his lamp skittering away, its light wheeling across the vaults.

"*Stop.*"

Lucan had not raised his voice. He had straightened, all of him, the ribs and the shoulders and the long Ferrers neck, and turned his lamp full on his own face so that Pell could see it.

"I am Lucan Ferrers," he said. "My father is the Lord Exchequer of this city. My father signs the charter of your Charitable Board. My father signs the warrant that pays your salary, Mr. Pell. If you lay that stick on anyone in this hall, I shall see that he signs another one, and you will not care for it." He paused. "Put it down."

Pell's hand opened and the cudgel fell out of it onto the brick. Then he sat down on the floor as if somebody had cut his strings, and put his face in his hands.

"Ferrers," he said through his fingers. "Of course. Of course it's Ferrers."

Hester got her foot out of the channel. It was numb to the shin. She hobbled to him.

"Who are *they*, Mr. Pell?"

"The Halcyon. It's maintenance, it's Halcyon maintenance on the old cistern taps under the Charter, it's all *permitted*—"

"Who told you that?"

"The gentleman." His shoulders were shaking. "From the Warden's office. He came in the spring. He had papers. Seals. He said it was a Charter matter and the Board needn't be troubled with it. Would I see the cellar was open when his men came, and that nobody went down. And there'd be ten sols a month for my trouble." He took his hands from his face. "Ten sols, Miss Vail. Do you know what the *Board* pays me to run three hundred and four casings on charity money and Pell this and Pell that and not one of them—"

"You knew they were weeping."

"I didn't *know*."

"I left you a note. On the first of Russet. Directional, east, quick. I wrote it down for you."

"I didn't know what it meant!"

"You knew enough," Hester said, "to come back from Harl in the middle of the night because you were afraid somebody might be down here."

He looked at her then, with pale eyes like a fish on a slab, and said nothing.

"Which Warden?" said Teo, from the floor, nursing his elbow.

"What?"

"You said the Warden's office. There are four Wardens. Which?"

"I don't *know*. He said the Warden's office. He had a paper with a seal. He was a clerk. He wore a coat over his gown and he never gave his name and I never asked it." Pell laughed, a terrible small laugh. "I never asked. That was the whole of what the ten sols was for."

Somewhere behind them, at the western end of the hall, a bell rang. A handbell, shaken hard. Faintly, down the passage, Agathe's voice was calling.

"Somebody's watch," said Lucan. "Time to go."

They left Pell sitting on the floor of the Great Cistern beside his cudgel with his lantern guttering. Hester's frozen boot squelched on every stride. Behind them the cut stub of the seventh rod went on welling, slow and black, into its sleeve.

* * *

Agathe had heard boots in the yard. Three men, she said. She had rung the cellar fire-bell and sent them round the front to a sleeper in distress, and by the time they got there the three aspirants were over the yard wall and running down the alley toward the sea-wall. Hester did not stop to find out whose men they were.

The first dray back over the Causeway went at dawn. They spent what was left of the night in the lee of the old sea-wall at the foot of Coldwater Street, and when the sky over the White began to go from black to grey they climbed the worn stair and sat on the top among the iron mooring rings, legs hanging over the drop. Hester's right foot was a dead thing in a wet boot. Teo had his elbow in his hand. Lucan sat with his arms folded tight across his ribs and his face turned east.

Out on the White the Fallow dawn came up as a line, then a band, then the whole flat edge of the world going rose. Two miles out, on the only rise in all that flatness, the dome of the Halcyon caught the first light.

Hester looked down at the foot of the wall. Under all that salt the double line ran. *Main. Main. Main.* On the Lattice gallery sheet it had had an ending: *Line enters at 5th Fm.* She had copied that out three weeks ago as a fact about the year 400. She had not thought of it as a fact about tonight.

"It's not smuggling," she said. "I thought somebody was taking grain out. Out of the Halcyon, to sell. The *Wren*. I thought that was what it was." She opened the box and looked at the foot of brass in its oilcloth, the little lattice and the 23. "It's not coming *out*. It's going *in*. They're not taking the grain out to Ambry. They're taking Ambry in to the grain."

"The stillness," said Teo.

"Out of the casings. Down the drains. Into the rods. East." She shut the box. "Somebody inside the Halcyon is drawing the keep off three hundred sleepers in a charity ward and piping it two miles under the White into the Fifth Fathom, and paying Pell ten sols a month to keep the cellar shut. And Aske went down there on the twelfth of Russet and saw it, and four days later he was in a casing."

"The Warden's office," said Lucan.

"There are four," said Teo.

On the dome the Lantern's mirrors came round and flashed. Hester sat on the cold wall with the box in her lap and her brother's stillness in a foot of brass, and looked at it. She thought of Corvin Aske in his casing in the Quiet Rooms, one hand out, his mouth open on a word he had never finished. *Ask O.*

"Then we find out which," she said.
