# Chapter 13

## The Cistern

> The founders' cistern beneath Coldwater and the Eastern Wards is bricked, and shall not be reopened, its brine being unwholesome and its passages unsafe.
>
> — *Minute of the Ambry Water Board, 400 Y.E.*

Lucan Ferrers brought a saw.

He brought it up the Rookery stair at the ninth bell on the thirteenth of Fallow, wrapped in a good wool blanket. He laid it on Hester's bed as if he were laying out a body. It was a short bow-saw with a fine-toothed blade for cutting metal. When Hester unwrapped it she saw that the frame was rosewood, with a brass maker's plate let into the grip.

"Where did you get this?"

"From the estate carpenter at Ferrers House. He was asleep." Lucan sat down on Teo's bed without being asked, which was a thing he had begun doing, and held himself very upright. That was a thing he had not stopped doing since the sink. The ribs were mending. Hester could see them mending in the way he breathed, shallow at the top and careful at the bottom, like a man carrying a full tray. "I left him a note. And a crown. I think he'll consider himself well paid for a saw that cuts brass like cheese."

"You think we'll need to cut brass."

"I think you think so, or you wouldn't have asked me for plans." He took a roll of paper out of his coat. "And these. Copies. The Water Board survey of 398, two years before they bricked it. My father keeps the originals in his library, because my father keeps the originals of everything that might one day be worth money. I traced them by candle last night. Don't look at the lettering; I was tired."

Teo was sitting cross-legged on the floor between the beds with a plate of saltcake balanced on his knee. He took the roll and spread it out on the boards, weighting the corners with his boots, a cup and the plate. They bent over it, all three of them, in the light of the one lamp.

It was a long, narrow drawing, the paper overlapped and pasted where Lucan had joined sheets. At its western end were the streets of Lowside, faint, in dotted line: Coldwater, Tanner's Row, Chandler's Lane, the curve of the sea-wall. Under Coldwater Street, in heavy ink, lay a great oblong labelled *Great Cistern: capacity 40,000 tuns*. It was ribbed across with the lines of vaulting. Smaller chambers clustered round it like cells round a queen. And from its eastern end a single double line ran out under the sea-wall, out under the dotted shore, and on, straight as a rule, across two feet of blank paper that was the White. The draughtsman had written along it, every few inches, in a small precise hand: *Main. Main. Main.* At the far eastern end, where the paper stopped, he had written: *to the Collegium. Not surveyed beyond.*

"Not surveyed beyond," Teo said. "Of course not. Nobody wanted to walk two miles in a pipe."

"It isn't a pipe," Lucan said. "Look at the section." He pointed to a little inset drawing in the corner: a horseshoe of brick, with a man drawn in it for scale, his hat brushing the crown. "It's a tunnel. You could drive a pony down it."

Hester put her finger on the oblong under Coldwater Street. She did not need to count the dotted streets to know where her finger was. She had walked over that spot on the first of every month for eleven years.

"The Cold House," she said, "is sitting on top of it."

"It was a customs warehouse once," said Teo. "Before the brinery. Built when there was a sea. Of course they'd put it over the town's water." He looked up at her. "Agathe said yes?"

"Agathe said yes."

She had written on the ninth, and the answer had come back with the carrier on the eleventh. Agathe's hand was round and careful, like a child's copybook, which was how they had taught writing at Tidewater Abbey sixty years ago. *Mr. Pell is to go to his sister's in Harl on the fourteenth for three nights, it being her lying-in. The cellar door is mine to keep, as the oil is kept there. I shall be up. I do not ask what you want with the cellar, my dear, and I do not want to know, except that if it is to do with the weeping I shall pray for you, and if it is not I shall pray for you anyway. Come by the yard. Eleventh bell. A.L.*

"She's seventy," Lucan said. "And a Tider. And you're asking her to let three Halcyon aspirants into a bricked civic works at midnight. If it's found out, she'll lose her place."

"I know."

"Does she?"

"Yes," said Hester, "she does," and Lucan looked at her a moment longer and then nodded, as though she had passed some assay of his own.

They planned it on the floor, over the plan. Lamps: three, kept-light, which did not need air and would not go out if the tunnel was foul. Lucan had brought them too, Exchequer issue, with the Ferrers mark on the shutters. Rope. A crowbar. The saw. Three water-bottles, because grain-work made you thirsty and Hester did not intend to come out of that hole without doing grain-work. A tin box lined with oilcloth for anything they took. Teo's chalk, to mark the turns.

"And if somebody's down there?" Teo asked.

"Then we come back up," said Hester.

"And if they don't let us?"

Lucan touched his ribs, lightly, as if to check they were still where he had left them. "Then I'll tell them who my father is," he said. "It's very nearly the only thing I'm good for."

* * *

The fourteenth of Fallow was a dark night with no moon and a hard thin wind out of the north. The Sere had blown itself out a week ago and left Lowside's shutters scoured. They took the last Causeway dray into the city at the tenth bell, sitting on the tailboard among sacks of Halcyon laundry with their gowns rolled into their bags. By the eleventh bell they were in the yard behind the Cold House, among the oil-drums and the empty brine casks. Agathe was standing in the cellar door with a candle, wrapped in her grey shawl, looking exactly as she looked at the second bell of the morning.

"You'll be the Ferrers boy," she said to Lucan.

"I am, madam."

"You've your mother's chin. She came here once, to give money. She stood in the ward and wept like a child." Agathe looked at Teo. "And you're the one from the Abbey."

"I was," said Teo. "Once."

"Once is enough. They'll have taught you to be quiet." She stood aside. "Mind the step. There's eleven of them and the sixth is gone."

The cellar was low and long and smelled of lamp-oil and old brick. Barrels of oil stood along one wall in racks. Along the other stood the brinewright's stores: sacks of Deep in wax-sealed bags, a recasting trough, buckets, a long brass dipping-ladle. At the far end, where the cellar ran under the charity ward, the brick floor was laid with iron gratings, the size of a door, set flush with the floor at intervals. Drains, Hester thought. Every cellar in Lowside had drains. You needed them when the Weeps backed up in a wet spring.

Agathe stopped by the last of them and lowered her candle.

"I come down here for the oil," she said, "every morning. I've come down here for the oil every morning for thirty-one years. And in the spring, about the time the Corrie twins started weeping, this grating was different. Somebody had had it up." She pointed with the candle. The iron was old, black, rough with rust. But round its edge the mortar that held it to the brick had been raked out and renewed, and the new mortar was pale and clean against the old. "I asked Mr. Pell. He said it was Halcyon maintenance. He said the Halcyon looks after the old cistern taps, under the Charter, and it was no business of mine." Her mouth did not move. "I've kept the oil thirty-one years. I know what's my business."

Lucan was looking at the grating. He went down on one knee, carefully, favouring the ribs, and put his fingers to the new mortar.

"This has been opened more than once," he said. "Look. They've raked it out and reset it, and raked it out again. There's three colours of mortar."

"Then they come and go," said Teo.

"Then they come and go," said Hester, "and we're going." She took the crowbar off her shoulder.

The mortar was soft, as new mortar is. It came out in crumbs. The grating was heavy, but it had been lifted recently and it wanted to lift. When they had it up and leaning against the wall, a breath came up out of the hole beneath it. It was not a draught, exactly; it was too slow for that. The air simply moved, upward, past their faces, the way air moves out of a cellar door on a hot day.

It was cold. It was sweet. Hester knew it before it reached her mouth. It had the cold sweetness of the charity ward on the six worn steps, the cellar-of-apples sweetness. Under the sweetness, far down at the back of the tongue, it had the thing she had come for.

Quick.

Agathe stood with her candle and looked at Hester's face.

"Go on, then," she said. "I'll be here. I'll not leave the hole." And then, which Hester had not expected: "Tide carry you."

* * *

There was an iron ladder fixed to the brick, old, its rungs flaking. It went down twelve feet into a short sloping passage, round-arched, just high enough to walk in if you stooped. The passage ran east for perhaps thirty yards. Then the walls fell away on either side and the floor went on, and the lamps that had been filling the passage with amber light were suddenly lighting nothing at all.

They stopped. Lucan opened his lamp's shutter wide, and the light went out from them and up and up and found, at last, a ceiling.

The Great Cistern was a hall.

It was a hall built to hold water, and it had held it for a hundred years before the Ebb, and it had held nothing for nine hundred since. Hester thought it had perhaps forgotten what it was for. It was perhaps eighty yards long and forty wide. Rows of square brick piers marched away down its length, each as thick as a house, each rising into a vault, so that the roof above them was a field of shallow domes, like the underside of a quilt. The brick was black at the bottom and dun above. At a height about twice a tall man's, a pale line ran round every pier and every wall, exactly level. Hester thought at first that it was paint. Then she understood that it was where the water had stood.

The floor was dry. It was dry brick, swept by nothing, gritted with fallen mortar and dust. It sloped very gently toward the centre. Down the centre of it, from the west wall where they stood to the east wall they could not see, ran a channel. It was a gutter of dressed stone a foot wide and a foot deep, the sort that was cut to drain a reservoir when it needed cleaning. The channel was not dry.

It was full of brine.

It lay in the stone very still, black in the lamplight, with a skin on it like the skin on milk. It did not move. Hester stood at its edge and looked down into it, and the sweetness came up off it so thick she could taste it without opening her mouth.

"That," said Teo, very quietly, "is live."

"Yes."

"It shouldn't be. Live brine dies in minutes. You leave a basin of it on a bench and by the time you've found your spoon it's gone flat." He crouched, not touching. "Somebody's keeping it alive."

"Nobody's keeping it," Hester said. "It's being fed."

She did not say what with. She did not know yet. She knew what she thought, and she did not let herself think it, because thinking it before she had tasted it was the kind of thing she had spent twenty-one years on a stool teaching Customs House clerks not to do. You did not grade by hoping.

They followed the channel east.

Their feet made no echo, which was the strange thing. A hall that size should have rung like a bell. The brick swallowed sound. Teo's chalk-marks squeaked on the piers, and then the squeak was gone. Somewhere far off, water, or something like water, dripped, with a long interval between each drop. After a while Hester found she was counting the intervals the way she counted coins.

The channel had arms. Every twenty yards or so a smaller gutter came into it from the north side, from the direction of the street above. Each arm ran back to a round iron grating in the floor, like the grating in the cellar, only these were set into the vault above. Hester stopped under the third one and held up her lamp. The grating was in the ceiling, eight or nine feet over her head. From it, hanging down, was a rod.

It was brass. It was perhaps an inch thick and it was new, bright, unscratched, with none of the green that brass took on in damp. It came down out of the grating overhead and ran down the face of the pier in iron clips, and then bent and ran along the floor. It was sleeved for its whole length in a sort of trough of hammered tin, and the trough was full of the black brine, and the rod lay in the brine like an eel in a river. It joined the arm of the channel. The arm joined the main.

There were others. Once she had seen one, she could see them all, glinting under every grating along the north side of the hall: a row of bright brass rods coming down out of the dark, each in its sleeve of brine, each laid along the floor to the channel. She counted them. She walked down the row with her lamp and counted.

Fourteen.

"Hester," said Lucan.

She had stopped under the seventh. She did not answer him. She was looking at the clip that held the rod to the pier, an ordinary iron clip, forged, with a flat plate where it was bolted to the brick. On the plate there was a stamp, small, very clean, the sort of stamp you make with a punch and a hammer: a square grid of lines, three by three, like a window, or like a crystal of grain seen end-on, and under it the number *23*.

"That's a Lattice mark," said Lucan, behind her. His voice was strange. "That's the mark of the Lattice Yards. Third Fathom. They stamp everything they make. My father has a gate made by them on the Exchequer Yard, and it's got the same—it's on the hinges."

"Halcyon work," said Teo.

"Halcyon work," said Lucan.

Hester took off her right glove.

She did not think about it. She had told herself, on the ladder, that she would think about it. But she had been an assayer since she was nineteen, and what an assayer does with a thing she does not understand is put it on her tongue. She knelt by the tin sleeve where the seventh rod lay in its brine. She put the tip of her smallest finger into the black liquid. It was so cold that it burned. She lifted the finger and put it in her mouth.

The weight came down on her like a roof.

She had tasted the Deep at a hundred and forty. She had tasted the cargo of the *Constant Wren*, and the warm white grain from the Fifth Fathom stays, and the First Taste on the Long Table that made a hall of adults weep. None of them was this. This was not heavy. Heavy was a word for things that pressed. This did not press. It *settled*. It came down behind her eyes and lay there and did not move, and went on not moving. Its stillness was so old and so complete that for a moment she could not tell if she was breathing. It had the patience of something that had lain in the dark for decades, waiting, and had forgotten it was waiting, and had become only the shape of the wait.

She knew it. She knew it the way you know a voice in the next room before you know the words.

It was the stillest thing she had ever tasted. It was stiller than grain. It was stiller than anything that came out of the ground, because the ground had not put it there. Ten, twenty, thirty years of a life held still had put it there, the stillness soaking into the salt around a body, day after day, until the salt was saturated with it. Old keepings were the stillest things in the world. Sorle had said it in the Black Bench with his chalk, and she had written it down without understanding it.

And under the stillness, as there had been on the bead on Tib's casing, at the very back, faint: the bright wet startle of the brine around it, moving. Being drawn.

She took her finger out of her mouth.

"This is them," she said.

Neither of the others spoke.

"This is them." She was on her knees on the brick, and her voice was flat and level, the voice she had used to read a grade aloud to the excisemen, and she held on to it. "This is what's coming off the casings. This is what's weeping off them. It's going down the drains into these rods, and the rods are taking it to the channel, and the channel's taking it east." She swallowed. The weight was still behind her eyes. "This is Tib."

* * *

Teo knelt down beside her. He did not ask. He put out his hand, and she nodded, and he dipped one finger in the black brine as she had done and put it, not in his mouth, but against the hollow under his ear, where the bone was thin. That was how Reader Fane taught her Bench to listen. He shut his eyes.

It took him longer than it took her. His hold had always been bad. He fidgeted, even kneeling; his shoulders went up and down; she watched his breath try to slow and fail and try again. Then it caught. She saw the colour go out of his lips. His face emptied, as faces did in the hold, all its quick warmth draining away until it was only a face.

He was still for a long time. Long enough that Lucan shifted and looked at her, and she shook her head.

When Teo came out, he came out slowly, the way a man wakes who has been sleeping in a cold room. He sat back on his heels. He was crying. She did not think he knew it. The tears ran down into his collar and left white tracks on his cheeks.

"What did you hear?"

"The Roar," he said. "Like always. But it's far off. Thin. It's not—it's not the sea in this. It's not old enough." He wiped his face with his sleeve and looked at the white on the wool. "Under it. Under the Roar." He stopped.

"Teo."

"Breathing," he said. "People breathing. Lots of them. All at once and none of them together. Like a ward at night. Like the infirmary at the Abbey when the fever came through, when I sat up with the brothers." He looked at her. "And some of it's small. Some of it's quick and small and light. Hester, some of it's children."

The brine lay in its sleeve, black and perfectly still, and fed, and was fed upon.

Lucan said, in his driest voice, the voice he used in Vey's Brinescript class to say something very rude very politely: "Then I think we had better take one with us."

He unwrapped the saw.

* * *

The seventh rod came down out of the vault on its iron clips to within a foot of the floor, then bent at a right angle and ran along it. Lucan chose the length between the last clip and the bend, where the rod stood clear of the pier and clear of its tin sleeve. Hester held the lamp. Teo held the rod above the cut with both hands in his gloves, wincing at the cold through the leather.

"If this is full," said Lucan, "it'll spill."

"Then it spills."

"It's *people*, you said."

"It's what's been taken off them." She had thought about it on her knees. "It's no more them than the water you wring out of a shirt is the shirt. What's in that rod is going east whatever we do. Cut it."

He looked at her a moment, and then he put the blade to the brass and began to draw it.

The saw was very good. The estate carpenter at Ferrers House would not have been ashamed of it. It went into the brass with a high thin singing that the brick swallowed at once. The noise was in their ears and nowhere else, as if the three of them were inside a bell that no one outside could hear. Brass filings came off the blade in bright curls and fell into the sleeve and lay on the black brine without sinking. Halfway through, the rod began to sweat. Beads stood out on it above and below the cut. They were in lines. They ran away from the cut along the rod, east, toward the bend, toward the channel.

"Still runs to quick," Teo whispered.

"It's going where it's going," said Hester. "Keep cutting."

Lucan's breathing had gone short. He stopped once with his hand pressed flat to his side, and Hester took the saw from him without a word and finished it herself. The last of the brass parted with a little *tink*. Teo, holding the upper length, felt it go and gasped. The cut end on the downstream side gave a single slow bubble, and brine welled out of it, black and cold. Hester caught the welling in the tin box, in the oilcloth. It was not much. A spoonful. The rod was hollow, she saw; a brass tube, bored very fine, with brine running down its heart.

They cut again a foot lower. The second cut was quicker, because they knew how now. When it parted, Hester had in her hands a foot of brass tube as thick as two fingers. It was so cold she could feel it through her gloves, and in the middle of its length there was a stamped band: the three-by-three lattice and the number *23*.

She was wrapping it in the oilcloth when the light came round the pier.

* * *

It was a lantern, an ordinary tin lantern with a horn window. It was held high, by a man who was breathing hard and fast through his nose. In his other hand he had a cudgel: a short length of oak with a knob at the end, the kind of thing carters kept under the seat of a dray. He was a heavy, pale, balding man in a coat buttoned wrong. Hester had seen him perhaps five times in eleven years, always at a distance, always leaving.

"Mr. Pell," she said.

"Who's that? Who's there?" He swung the lantern toward her voice. His face in its light was grey and wet. "This is—you've no right—this is a closed works—this is *Halcyon property*—"

"You're at your sister's," Hester said. "In Harl."

"I came back." He had seen the tin box in her hands. He had seen the saw, and the cut ends of the rod, and the black brine welling slowly out of the downstream stub into its sleeve. His face changed. "Oh, God. Oh, God, what have you done? What've you *done*? They'll know. They'll know straight off. They always know. Put that—put it *back*—"

He came at her. He did not come well; he was not a man who had hit anyone in his life, and he held the cudgel as a man holds a stick he is afraid of. But he was heavy and frightened, and frightened men are dangerous because they do not stop. Hester stepped back and her heel went into the channel. The brine took her boot to the ankle, so cold she cried out. Teo was up and grabbing at Pell's coat, and Pell flung him off, and Teo went down hard on the brick with the lamp skittering away from him, its light wheeling across the vaults.

"*Stop.*"

Lucan had not raised his voice. He did not need to. He had straightened, all of him, carefully, the ribs and the shoulders and the long Ferrers neck. His lamp was in his hand and he had turned its light full on his own face, so that Pell could see it.

"I am Lucan Ferrers," he said. "My father is the Lord Exchequer of this city. My father signs the charter of your Charitable Board. My father signs the warrant that pays your salary, Mr. Pell. If you lay that stick on anyone in this hall, I shall see that he signs another one, and you will not care for it." He paused. "Put it down."

Pell put it down. He did not drop it; he stood there, and his hand opened, and the cudgel fell out of it onto the brick. Then he sat down on the floor, quite suddenly, as if somebody had cut his strings. He put his face in his hands.

"Ferrers," he said through his fingers. "Ferrers. Of course. Of course it's Ferrers."

Hester got her foot out of the channel. It was numb to the shin. She hobbled to him.

"Who are *they*, Mr. Pell?"

"The Halcyon." He did not look up. "The maintenance. It's maintenance, it's Halcyon maintenance on the old cistern taps under the Charter, it's all above—it's all *permitted*—"

"Who told you that?"

"The gentleman." His shoulders were shaking. "From the Warden's office. He came in the spring. He had papers. Seals. He said the Halcyon had need of the old line, there was a—a matter of safety, a Charter matter, and the Board needn't be troubled with it. And would I see that the cellar was open when his men came, and that nobody went down. And there'd be ten sols a month for my trouble." He took his hands away from his face. "Ten sols. Ten sols, Miss Vail. Do you know what the Board pays me? Do you know what the *Board* pays me to run three hundred and four casings on charity money and Pell this and Pell that and not one of them—"

"You knew," Hester said. "You knew they were weeping."

"I didn't *know*."

"I left you a note. On the first of Russet. Directional, east, quick. I wrote it down for you."

"I didn't know what it meant!"

"You knew enough," Hester said, "to come back from Harl in the middle of the night because you were afraid somebody might be down here."

He looked at her then. He had pale eyes, like a fish on a slab. He said nothing.

"Which Warden?" said Teo, from the floor, where he was nursing his elbow.

"What?"

"You said the Warden's office. There are four. Upper Fathoms, Lower Fathoms, the Stacks, the Light. Which?"

"I don't know. I don't *know*. He said the Warden's office. He had a paper with a seal. He was a clerk. He wore a coat over his gown and he never gave his name and I never asked it." Pell laughed, a terrible small laugh. "I never asked. That was the whole of what the ten sols was for."

Somewhere behind them, in the dark at the western end of the hall, a bell rang. It was not a church bell. It was a handbell, being shaken hard. Faintly, down the passage, Agathe's voice was calling something.

"The watch," said Lucan. "Or somebody's. Time to go."

They left Pell sitting on the floor of the Great Cistern beside his cudgel with his lantern guttering. As they ran, Hester's frozen boot squelched on every stride. Behind them the cut stub of the seventh rod went on welling, slow and black, into its sleeve.

* * *

Agathe had heard boots in the yard. Three men, she said. She had rung the cellar bell, which was meant for fires, and told them through the door that there was a sleeper in distress in the fourth row and would they come round the front like Christians. By the time they had come round the front, the three aspirants were out of the cellar and over the yard wall and running down the alley toward the sea-wall. The grating lay against the wall where they had leaned it. Hester did not know whose men they were. She did not stop to find out.

They came up onto the sea-wall at the end of Coldwater Street as the sky over the White was going from black to grey. They sat there, on the top of the wall, among the old iron mooring rings, with their legs hanging over the drop. The tin box was in Hester's lap. Her right foot was a dead thing in a wet boot. Teo had his elbow in his hand. Lucan sat very straight with his arms folded tight across his ribs and his face turned east.

Out on the White the dawn came up the way it always did in Fallow: first a line, then a band, then the whole flat edge of the world going rose. The salt took the colour and gave it back doubled. The brine-pans along the margin lay in their stripes of pink and green. Two miles out, on the only rise in all that flatness, the dome of the Halcyon caught the first light. On top of it the Lantern's mirrors came round and flashed, once, white and hard.

Hester looked at the Lantern. Then she looked down, at the foot of the sea-wall. Under all that salt, under the dotted line of the old shore on the Water Board survey, the double line ran. *Main. Main. Main.* Straight as a rule, east, across two miles of blank paper.

*To the Collegium. Not surveyed beyond.*

"It's not smuggling," she said.

Nobody answered.

"I thought—I thought somebody was taking grain out. Out of the Halcyon. To sell. The *Wren*. I thought that was what it was." She opened the box and looked at the foot of brass in its oilcloth, with the little stamped lattice and the 23. "It's not coming *out*. It's going *in*. They're not taking the grain out to Ambry. They're taking Ambry in to the grain."

"The stillness," said Teo.

"Out of the casings. Down the drains. Into the rods. East." She shut the box. "Into the Halcyon. Somebody inside the Halcyon is drawing the keep off three hundred sleepers in a charity ward and piping it two miles under the White, and paying Pell ten sols a month to keep the cellar shut."

"The Warden's office," said Lucan.

"There are four," said Teo.

The Lantern flashed again. Hester sat on the cold wall with the box in her lap, and her brother's stillness in a foot of brass, and looked at it. She thought of the long slow blink the whole city lived under and no longer saw. She thought of Corvin Aske, in his casing in the Quiet Rooms, one hand out, his mouth open on a word. *Ask O.*

"Then we find out which," she said.
