# Chapter 14

## What Is Owed

> Every keeping is a lie we tell time, and time collects.
>
> — *B. Sorle, Lectures on Arrears (unpublished; circulated in fair copy among the Black Bench)*

"Don't," said Sorle.

He did not look up. He was at his desk with his back to the door and a moth-eaten black shawl round his shoulders, writing small and fast and crossing out as much as he set down. The kept moth sat in its lump of clear grain at his elbow with its wings half open.

"Don't what?"

"Don't take it to Gage." He blotted a line, examined it, and crossed it out. "You are standing in my door at the second bell with a box under your arm and a face like a summons, Miss Vail, smelling of wet brick and Lowside. I deduce that you have been somewhere you were not supposed to be and brought something back. I further deduce, from the way you are holding the box, that you mean to carry it straight down to the Proctory and put it on the Chief Proctor's desk. Don't."

"It's evidence."

"It is a length of brass."

"With a Lattice Yards mark on it. Out of the founders' cistern under the Cold House, drawing the keep off a charity ward and carrying it two miles east, to here." She put the box on the one corner of his desk that was not under paper and opened it. "You told me to bring you evidence. Not tastes. Something you could put on a slate. That's it."

Sorle turned round in his chair.

He looked at the brass for a long time without touching it. Then he looked at Hester's right boot, still dark to the shin with salt-tide, and then at her face.

"Sit," he said.

She told him all of it, from the grating to the sea-wall, in order. When she came to Pell, and *the Warden's office*, and *ten sols*, he shut his eyes. When she had finished he kept them shut a while, and then said, "And now you wish to take it to Halloran Gage."

"He's the Chief Proctor. Somebody in this building is drawing stillness out of living people without their consent or their families'. That's—" She had looked it up in the Brinestacks on her way down, with her boot squelching. "That's Article Thirty. Improper working upon a kept person. It's his job."

"It is his job," Sorle agreed. "So is closing files." The chair complained as he leaned back. "Let me tell you what will happen. Gage will listen to you with every appearance of gravity, because he is a grave man and it costs him nothing. He will take the rod, and give you a receipt, and promise an inquiry. And then the rod will go wherever things go in this Halcyon when they are inconvenient to someone who outranks the Chief Proctor. And you will have nothing. Not even a length of brass."

"You don't know that."

"I have been a Reader of this Collegium for thirty-four years and censured by it for thirty-two. I know it as you know Shelf from Middling."

"Then what would you have me do? Sit on it?"

"Yes. Keep it. Show it to no one. Find out which Warden, and why. And when you can prove it, go over Gage's head and over the head of whoever is over Gage's. Go to the Assize, if you must." He paused. "If you take it down the Stair today, you take it down alone. I'll not come with you."

She looked at the rod, at the lattice and the 23. She thought of Pell on the floor of the Cistern: *that was the whole of what the ten sols was for.* She thought of Tib in the fourth row, weeping east.

"I've sat on things before," she said. "Eleven years. It's not done him any good."

She closed the box. Sorle did not try again. He turned back to his desk and said, to the paper, "Shut the door on your way out. The moth dislikes a draught."

* * *

The Proctory lay on the First Fathom behind the Hall of Assay, down a short corridor hung with framed copies of the Charter. It had a front room with a rail and a sergeant behind the rail, and an inner office with a desk of Hask oak and a map of the Fathoms stuck with pins.

Chief Proctor Halloran Gage listened to her from behind the desk. He was a broad, grey, close-cropped man of fifty-odd who sat as if the chair had been issued to him and would have to be returned in good condition. He did not interrupt. He wrote in a leather book in a clerk's hand.

When she had finished he picked up the brass, turned it over, and held it to the window so the light went down its bore. He studied the stamped lattice for some time.

"Twenty-three," he said.

"I don't know what the number means."

"Lot number. The Yards stamp one on everything they cast. Twenty-three would be—" He stopped himself. "That's for the inquiry." He set the rod down. "You understand, Miss Vail, that you entered a closed civic works at night without a warrant."

"Yes, sir."

"And interfered with Halcyon property."

"With something that has a Halcyon mark on it, sir, in a place the Halcyon has no warrant to be either, so far as I know. I looked. Article Thirty."

He considered her. His eyes were small and pale and steady, and she could read nothing in them, which she supposed was the point of them.

"I know Article Thirty," he said. "I'll take this, and your statement, signed. I'll open an inquiry. It'll go to the Master of the Lattice Yards for the lot, and to the Water Board for the works, and—" He paused. "And to whatever office is concerned. You'll hear from me."

"When, sir?"

"When I've something to tell you." He wrote her a receipt on a printed slip. *Received from H. Vail, aspirant, one length brass tube, stamped, approx. 12 in., in connection with a matter under Article 30. H. Gage, Ch. Proctor.* As she turned to go, he said, in a slightly different voice: "Miss Vail. Do you go to the Cold House on the first of the month?"

She stopped. "Yes."

"Then go on the first of the month," said Gage. "And not otherwise. Not at night. Good morning."

* * *

She heard nothing on the fifteenth, or the sixteenth. On the morning of the seventeenth a sergeant in a buff coat was waiting at the door of the Benches after Crisp's class. Miss Vail was to step down to the Proctory.

Gage was standing at the window of the inner office with his hands behind his back. The chair behind the Hask oak desk was not empty.

The man in it was tall, silvered at the temples, handsome in an expensive way, his gown open over a dove-grey coat, with the ochre cord of the Brinescript Bench at his shoulder and over it the narrow silver chain that only one man in the Halcyon wore. Deputy Chancellor Ilario Vey. She had seen him at the head of the Readers' table at Matriculation. She had never spoken to him. Her signed statement lay on the blotter in front of him.

"Miss Vail," he said, as if they were old acquaintances. "Do sit."

She sat. Gage did not turn round.

"I've read this twice," said Vey, touching the statement with one finger. "It's very good. Dates, times, distances. Fourteen rods; you counted. A lot number. Article Thirty, cited correctly, which is more than half my Bench could manage. Corvin always said you had an eye." He smiled. "He was a diligent man too. The Conclave thanked him for it."

"The brass, sir."

"Is a Halcyon matter, and is with the office that concerns it." He said it pleasantly, as if he were telling her where the Bursary was. "The Chief Proctor has been good enough to pass it on. You'll keep your receipt, of course. Receipts are important. I'm told you were in customs."

She looked at Gage's back. It did not move.

"There are thirteen more," she said. "Under the Cold House. I can show you tonight."

"I'm sure you could. I'd rather you didn't." Vey turned a page of her statement that did not need turning. "You walked here from Lowside with a dead man's privilege on your shoulder. I admire that; I do. But a privilege is not a Bench. An aspirant without a sponsor holds her place at the Chancellor's pleasure, and the Chancellor"—a small, regretful movement of the hand—"tires. These things come to my desk now. Midwinter isn't far off." He squared the statement against the blotter's edge. "And your brother is kept on a charity form, I think. The Board is very careful with its charity forms. So am I."

It was said so kindly that it took her a moment to hear it.

"Is that all, sir?"

"That's all. Diligence is a fine quality in an aspirant." He stood, so she stood. "So is discretion. The best of them learn both."

He went out past her, unhurried. The sergeant behind the rail stood up straight, and Vey's steps went away down the corridor of framed Charters.

Gage turned from the window. For the first time something moved in his small pale eyes, and was put away again.

"You've your receipt, Miss Vail," he said. "Keep it. Good morning."

* * *

Sorle did not turn round. "Well?"

"Gone," said Hester.

He laughed, once. It was not a pleasant sound, and it was not, she thought, aimed at her.

"Sit down, Miss Vail."

She sat. Afternoon light came down the shaft in the corner of the study, a long dusty bar of it from the Lantern's mirrors. It lay across the chalked slates against the bookcases and across the portrait of the broad-faced woman with Physic red at her collar.

She told him: Gage at the window with his back to her; Vey in Gage's chair; *diligence*; *the Chancellor's pleasure*; the charity form.

"Vey," said Sorle, as if tasting the name and not caring for the vintage. "Who thanked Corvin for his diligence in Conclave and moved on to the price of lamp oil. Who has the Chancellor's ear, because the Chancellor no longer has the strength to hold it herself. Who is, I need hardly add, the head of your friend Lucan's Bench." He tapped his pen on the blotter. "It need not be Vey. Vey is the sort of man through whom things pass. That rod may have passed through him on its way to somewhere else. But it passed."

"You told me so."

"I did. It gives me no pleasure. I've been right about the Halcyon for thirty-two years and it has never once given me pleasure." He swivelled to face her properly, and the shawl slipped off one bony shoulder, and he let it. "What will you do now?"

"I don't know."

"An honest answer. Rare."

"I've a receipt," said Hester. "And a Cistern with thirteen more rods in it that somebody will have had out by tonight, if they've any sense. And a man in a cellar who'll swear he never saw me. And a brother whose casing is thinner every week." She heard her voice go and brought it back. "And no Bench. The Registrar's written again. Midwinter, or I'm sent down. Crisp's full. Bassano's full. Orsolya can't take me till her third-years assay out, and she's been kind, but kind isn't a Bench. The rest of them look at me as if I'd come to read the meter."

Sorle looked at her for a long while.

Then he worked loose the top right drawer of his desk, which stuck, and took something out of it, and held it a moment before he put it on the blotter between them.

It was a Bench cord, looped and knotted in the Halcyon fashion. It was black, and not new. The black had gone brown at the knot, where a thumb had worked it.

"I have two aspirants," said Sorle. "Both third-years. Both will assay out this Rime, with distinction, God help the Fellowship. After that I had intended to have none. I've taken no one new in nine years; the Registrar has stopped sending me lists." He looked at the moth, not the cord. "It is not a good Bench to be on. You will be known as Sorle's, which is not a recommendation in this building. It will close doors Aske's white cord would have opened, and Aske's cord, you will forgive me, is no longer opening anything. Some of your examiners will mark you down for the colour on your shoulder before you have lifted a spoon. Crisp won't, because Crisp is honest. Vey will."

"Why are you doing it?"

The bar of light had moved onto the portrait, across the broad face and the red collar.

"Because Corvin asked me to," he said at last. "Not in words. Corvin never asked anyone for anything in words; he was too busy talking. But he spent his privilege on you, which in twenty years he had not spent on anyone. And on the sixteenth he asked me up to his Cell, and I found a dark corridor and a shut door and went back to bed in a temper. You know that part." His mouth twisted. "I owe him a half-hour. I can't pay it to him. You'll do."

He pushed the cord an inch toward her.

"And because I have nothing left to lose," he said, "and you have even less. That's a Bench."

She did not pick it up at once. She thought of the white cord on her shoulder, which Aske had looped there in the Gatehouse on Matriculation Day, fumbling, humming. Her eyes had gone hot. She did not let them do anything about it.

She took off the white cord, clumsily, folded it and put it in her pocket, where she would keep it. Then she looped the black one at her shoulder and pulled the knot tight.

"Ta," she said.

"Don't thank me. You'll hate it within the week." Sorle pulled a slate toward him and picked up the chalk. "Thursday. The fourth bell. Bring paper; I don't lend it. We'll begin with the arrears calculus, since you clearly can't be trusted to understand anything without first putting it in your mouth."

* * *

The arrears calculus was, Hester discovered over the next three days, a great deal of arithmetic dressed up as philosophy. She was good at arithmetic. She was not good at philosophy, and said so. Sorle said that put her ahead of most of the Conclave, who were good at neither but could tell the difference at dinner.

He wrote fast, in a hand that sloped backward, and wiped out what he had written with the side of his fist.

"Arrears," he said, "is pressure. Not a metaphor. A keeping holds a thing at one moment. The world goes on to the next, and the thing does not. Between where the thing is and where the world is, there is a difference, and the difference *wants*. It wants to close." He drew two lines, one flat, one rising away from it. "That wanting is arrears. It grows with time. It grows with the mass of what is kept." He drew the line steeper. "And it grows with how *lively* the thing was. A stone hardly minds being kept. A man minds a great deal. A man running minds more than a man asleep. A sea—" He stopped, and wiped the slate with his fist. "Never mind a sea. Not today."

"Why not today?"

"Because today you are learning arithmetic. Every casing pays its arrears out of its own stillness: the grain's, and what soaks into the grain from what it holds. And so. *Still runs to quick.* You've seen it twice now, once in my class and once on your knees in a hole in Lowside. Join a full old casing by live brine to something with a great deal of arrears and little stillness to pay them with. What happens?"

"The stillness runs out of the casing," said Hester. "Into the thing that's owing."

"It pays the other thing's debt." He drew an arrow. "And if the other thing is large enough, and old enough, and owes enough, it will drink a charity ward dry and still be thirsty."

She looked at the arrow.

"A quickening," she said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." The study was always cold, but she was colder. Two miles east, and a Halcyon at the end of it, and five fathoms down, pockets that were warm and wet and owed a great deal, and a Choir that sang slow to pay them. She had stood in that line and poured herself into it with sixty others and felt it drink.

She looked at the thought, and then she put it down, as you put down a sample you have not yet weighed.

"There are a great many rooms in the Halcyon," said Sorle, watching her. "You have a pipe that goes east and a building at the end of it. That is all you have."

"I know."

"Then go on with the arithmetic." His eyes were sharper than his voice. "If a stay is a pouring-in of stillness to pay a quickening's arrears, what does the quickening do?"

"Quiets."

"For how long?"

She thought of the brass stay-lines in the Fifth, and of a pocket that had been held down on the Tuesday and was warm again by the Saturday.

"Not long," she said. "And then it's back. Worse."

"Why worse?"

"Because you've not paid anything. You've just added to it. You've kept the keeping. It still owes everything it owed, and now it's had longer to owe it."

"Stays saturate," said Sorle softly. "Pour enough still into a quickening and it quiets, and grows hungrier. The Fathomers know it. Orsolya Thane knows it better than anyone alive; she's been pouring for thirty years. They've no other tool, and they've never wanted one. A stay is a lid, Miss Vail. That is all a stay is."

Hester did not move.

*You lid a spitting pan, she'll blow the lid through the roof.* Her father in the steam, drawing the fire out a shovelful at a time.

"What if you let it out?" she said. "What if you didn't pour stillness in. What if you let the owing out. A bit at a time. So it's paid."

"Then the moment *proceeds*." Sorle's voice was quiet. "A kept thing let out goes on from where it stopped. That's the Third Law; a child knows it. A kept apple goes on being an apple. A kept man goes on bleeding; a kept corpse blooms grey in a month or so, whatever the grade, and goes on being a corpse. And a quickening—" He put down the chalk. "A quickening is not an apple. We do not know what a quickening is. We know it is warm and wet and sounds like surf. If you let one go on, you would find out what it stopped *in the middle of*. In the Fifth Fathom of a building with three thousand people in it."

"Not in the Fifth. Somewhere else. Somewhere it couldn't—"

"No." He wiped the slate, all of it, the lines and the arrow and the rising curve. "That is not a thought for a first-year with a black cord three days old. You know what one thought about the sea has cost me. I am still paying for it." He turned back and wrote, fast, a line of arithmetic that had nothing to do with anything. "Arrears of a moth. Kept forty years. Mass a quarter-grain. Work it."

She worked it. She got it right. He grunted.

But when she looked up his chalk had stopped. He was looking at the portrait on the wall, at the broad-faced woman with the Physic red at her collar, with the expression of a man listening for something in another room.

"*A bit at a time,*" he said, under his breath. "*So it's paid.*" And then, without turning: "Arrears of a man, kept three months. Mass ten stone. Hush grain. Work it."

She looked at him.

"Work it, Miss Vail."

She worked it. It was a very large number. She did not say whose it was. Neither did he.

* * *

Agathe's letter came with the carrier on the twentieth of Fallow, in the round copybook hand.

*My dear. Mr. Pell has been quiet since he came back from his sister's and has had the cellar grating mortared over in the old way, thick, by a man from the Water Board. He has said nothing to me and I have said nothing to him. On the morning after, I went down for the oil and the drain was dry, and the weeping on our people stopped for two days. On the third day it began again. I do not know what that means. I expect you do.*

*There is a thinness at the foot of your lad's casing that I can see my candle through if I hold it low. Tide carry you.*

*A.L.*

Hester read it twice at the window of R-41, with the White going grey behind the glass. She put it in the tin box under her bed, where the rod had been and was not, and sat down on the bed with the black cord at her shoulder and looked at her hands.

Teo came in at the sixth bell with saltcake wrapped in a cloth and found her still sitting there. He did not ask. He put the saltcake on the sill and sat on his own bed and waited. After a while she held out the letter, and he read it, and gave it back.

"Two days," he said.

"Two days."

"One rod cut, out of fourteen, and it stopped for two days, until they'd mended it." He turned the cloth over in his hands without opening it. "So it can stop. That's what it means, Hester. It can be stopped."

"For two days."

"Two days," said Teo, "is not nothing."

The Lantern came round and flashed, and the light went across the White and the window and the two of them, and was gone. Hester sat with her hands on the letter and did the arithmetic of a thinning casing in her head. She did it again. It came out the same both times.
