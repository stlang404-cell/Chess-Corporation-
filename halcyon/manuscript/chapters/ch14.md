# Chapter 14

## What Is Owed

> Every keeping is a lie we tell time, and time collects.
>
> — *B. Sorle, Lectures on Arrears (unpublished; circulated in fair copy among the Black Bench)*

"Don't," said Sorle.

He did not look up. He was at his desk with his back to the door and his shoulders hunched under a moth-eaten black shawl, writing. He wrote small and fast, crossing out as much as he set down. A kept moth sat in a lump of clear grain at his elbow with its wings half open, as it had sat there, he once said, since before the Delving Wars were taught in schools.

"Don't what?"

"Don't take it to Gage." He blotted a line, examined it, and crossed it out. "You are standing in my door with a box under your arm and a face like a summons, Miss Vail, at the second bell, smelling of wet brick and Lowside. I deduce that you have been somewhere you were not supposed to be and brought something back. I further deduce, from the way you are holding the box, that you mean to carry it straight down the Stair to the Proctory and put it on the Chief Proctor's desk. Don't."

"It's evidence."

"It is a length of brass."

"With a Lattice Yards mark on it. Out of the founders' cistern under the Cold House. Drawing the keep off fourteen charity sleepers and carrying it two miles east, to here." She came into the room and put the box on the one corner of his desk that was not under paper and opened it. "You told me to bring you evidence. Not tastes. Something you could put on a slate. That's it."

Sorle turned round in his chair.

He looked at the brass for a long time. He did not touch it. He looked at the cut ends, and the stamped band, and the fine bore with the black brine in it, and at Hester's right boot. It was still dark to the shin with salt-tide, and she had not been able to get it off. Then he looked at her face.

"Sit," he said.

She told him. All of it, from the grating to the sea-wall, in order, as she would have read a schedule to the excisemen. He did not interrupt. When she came to Pell, and *the Warden's office*, and *ten sols*, he shut his eyes. When she had finished he sat for a while with them shut, and then opened them and said, "And now you wish to take it to Halloran Gage."

"He's the Chief Proctor. It's a Charter matter. Somebody in this building is drawing stillness out of living people without their consent or their families'. That's—" She had looked it up in the Brinestacks, on the walk back from the sea-wall, at the fourth bell, with her boot squelching. "That's an offence under Article Thirty. Improper working upon a kept person. It's his job."

"It is his job," Sorle agreed. "So is closing files." He leaned back. The chair complained. "Miss Vail. You did me the courtesy of bringing me what I asked for. Let me do you the courtesy of telling you what will happen. You will take that rod to the Proctory. Gage will listen to you with every appearance of gravity, because he is a grave man and it costs him nothing. He will take the rod from you, and give you a receipt, and promise an inquiry. And then the rod will go wherever things go in this Halcyon when they are inconvenient to someone who outranks the Chief Proctor. And you will have nothing. Not even a length of brass."

"You don't know that."

"I have been a Reader of this Collegium for thirty-four years," said Sorle, "and censured by it for thirty-two. I know it the way you know Shelf from Middling."

"Then what would you have me do? Sit on it?"

"Yes," said Sorle. "Sit on it. Keep it. Show it to no one. Find out which Warden. Find out why. And when you know, and can prove it, go over Gage's head and over the head of whoever is over Gage's head. Go to the Assize, if you must, with Aske's witness and your own." He paused. "If you take it down the Stair today, you take it down alone. I'll not come with you."

She looked at the rod, at the lattice and the 23. She thought of Pell on the floor of the Cistern, saying *I never asked; that was the whole of what the ten sols was for.* She thought of Tib in the fourth row, under the third window, weeping east.

"I've sat on things before," she said. "Eleven years. It's not done him any good."

She closed the box. Sorle watched her do it. He did not try again. He turned back to his desk and picked up his pen and said, to the paper, "Shut the door on your way out. The moth dislikes a draught."

* * *

The Proctory lay on the First Fathom, behind the Hall of Assay, down a short corridor of plain salt hung with framed copies of the Charter. It had a front room with a rail and a sergeant behind the rail. It had a row of holding-cells beyond, with doors of iron lattice, empty this morning but for one sleeping Fellow who had been brought in drunk from the Thirsty Society. And it had an inner office with a desk of Hask oak and a map of the Halcyon's Fathoms on the wall. The map was marked with pins.

Chief Proctor Halloran Gage sat behind the desk and listened to her. He was a broad, grey, close-cropped man of fifty-odd, with a soldier's back and a soldier's way of sitting, as if the chair were something issued to him that he would be expected to return in good condition. He did not interrupt either. He wrote, in a clerk's hand, in a leather book.

When she had finished he set down his pen and looked at the brass on his blotter. He did touch it. He picked it up, turned it over, and held it to the window so the light went down its bore, as a man looks down a gun-barrel. He looked at the stamped lattice for some time.

"Twenty-three," he said.

"I don't know what the number means."

"Lot number. The Yards stamp a lot number on everything they cast. Twenty-three would be—" He stopped himself. "It doesn't matter what it would be. That's for the inquiry." He set the rod down. "You understand, Miss Vail, that you entered a closed civic works at night without a warrant."

"Yes, sir."

"With two other aspirants, whom you have not named."

"Yes, sir."

"And interfered with Halcyon property."

"With something that has a Halcyon mark on it, sir, in a place the Halcyon has no warrant to be either, so far as I know. I looked. Article Thirty."

He considered her. He had small, pale, very steady eyes, and she could not read anything in them at all, which she supposed was the point of them.

"I know Article Thirty," he said. "I'll take this. I'll take your statement, and I'll want it signed. I'll open an inquiry. It'll go to the Warden of the Lattice Yards for the lot, and to the Water Board for the works, and—" He paused. "And to whatever office is concerned. You'll hear from me."

"When, sir?"

"When I've something to tell you." He wrote her a receipt on a printed slip and signed it and blotted it. *Received from H. Vail, aspirant, one length brass tube, stamped, approx. 12 in., in connection with a matter under Article 30. H. Gage, Ch. Proctor.* He handed it across. Then, as she was turning to go, he said, in a slightly different voice: "Miss Vail."

"Sir?"

"Do you go to the Cold House on the first of the month?"

She stopped.

"Yes."

"Then go on the first of the month," said Gage. "And not otherwise. Not at night. Good morning."

* * *

She heard nothing on the fifteenth. She heard nothing on the sixteenth. On the morning of the seventeenth, at the end of Crisp's Keeping class, a sergeant of Proctors in a buff coat was waiting at the door of the Benches. He said the Chief Proctor would be obliged if Miss Vail would step down.

Gage was standing this time, at the window of the inner office, with his hands behind his back. The leather book lay closed on the desk. The blotter was empty.

"I regret," he said, before she was through the door, "to tell you that the item you deposited on the fifteenth has been mislaid."

She did not sit down.

"Mislaid."

"It was entered in the evidence store on the evening of the fifteenth. It was not in the evidence store on the morning of the sixteenth. The store was locked. There's no sign of entry. I have questioned the sergeant on duty, who is a sound man and whom I believe." Every sentence came out separately, squared off, like bricks. "There will be a note of it in the Proctory book."

"And the inquiry?"

"Without the item, Miss Vail, there is very little to inquire into. A trespass in a closed civic works. A Water Board matter." He turned from the window. "I've written to the Water Board."

"There's a cistern under the Cold House," Hester said, "with fourteen brass rods in it stamped by the Lattice Yards. I can take you there. Tonight. I can show you the other thirteen."

"I've no warrant for the Cold House."

"Get one."

"From whom?" said Gage, and for the first time something moved in his face, and was put away again. "Miss Vail. There are matters of Halcyon infrastructure above both our heads. I've been made aware that this is one of them. I'm advising you, as plainly as I'm able, to leave it there."

"Made aware by whom?"

He did not answer. He looked at her with his pale steady eyes, and she understood that he was not going to, and that he wanted her to understand that he was not going to. It was the closest thing to an answer he could give.

"You've your receipt," he said. "Keep it. Good morning."

She went out through the front room, past the rail and the sergeant. In the corridor of framed Charters a man was coming toward her from the direction of the Assay Hall, unhurried, with his gown worn open over a dove-grey coat. He was tall and silvered at the temples and handsome in an expensive way. He had the ochre cord of the Brinescript Bench at his shoulder, and over it the narrow silver chain that only one man in the Halcyon wore. He carried a slim folder of papers under his arm. He was smiling at nothing in particular, as men smile who are used to being looked at.

Deputy Chancellor Ilario Vey. She had seen him at the Matriculation, at the head of the Readers' table. She had never spoken to him.

He passed her in the corridor and inclined his head, pleasantly.

"Miss Vail," he said.

He did not slow. He went on into the Proctory, and the sergeant behind the rail stood up very straight, and the door of the inner office opened before Vey reached it and closed behind him.

Hester stood in the corridor among the framed Charters. *Miss Vail.* He had known her name. Of course he had. She was Aske's privilege, the customs woman, the one who had been at the dead man's door. Everyone knew her name. There was no reason at all why the Deputy Chancellor should not know it.

She noticed, and set it down, as she would have set down a short weight.

* * *

Sorle was at his desk. He did not turn round.

"Well?"

"Mislaid," said Hester.

He laughed, once. It was not a pleasant sound, and it was not, she thought, aimed at her.

"Sit down, Miss Vail."

She sat. The chair was the same chair, the shawl the same shawl, the moth the same moth. Afternoon light came down the light-shaft in the corner of the study, the Lantern's mirrors sending a long dusty bar of it across the room. It lay across the slates leaned against the bookcases, chalked with Sorle's equations, and across the portrait of the dead woman on the wall. She had a long face and dark eyes and Physic red at her collar. Hester had looked at it on the eighth of Fallow without asking, and did not ask now.

"Tell me," said Sorle.

She told him. Gage at the window; the evidence store; *above both our heads*; Vey in the corridor; *Miss Vail*.

"Vey," said Sorle. He drew the name out as if he were tasting it and did not care for the vintage. "Our Deputy Chancellor. Who thanked Corvin for his diligence in Conclave and moved on to the price of lamp oil. Who has the Chancellor's ear, because the Chancellor no longer has the strength to hold it herself. Who is, I need hardly add, the head of your friend Lucan's Bench." He tapped his pen against the blotter. "It need not be Vey. Vey is the sort of man through whom things pass. That rod may have passed through him on its way to somewhere else. But it passed."

"You told me so."

"I did."

"Go on, then. Say it."

"I've no wish to say it." He put the pen down. "It gives me no pleasure, Miss Vail, to be right about the Halcyon. I've been right about the Halcyon for thirty-two years and it has given me no pleasure once." He swivelled his chair to face her properly for the first time, and the shawl slipped off one bony shoulder, and he let it. "What will you do now?"

"I don't know."

"An honest answer. Rare."

"I've a receipt," said Hester. "And a Cistern with thirteen more rods in it that somebody will have had out of there by tonight, if they've any sense. And a man in a cellar who'll swear he never saw me. And a brother whose casing is thinner every week." She heard her own voice go and brought it back. "And no Bench. You know that. The Registrar's written again. Midwinter, or I'm sent down. Crisp's full. Bassano's full. Orsolya can't take me till her third-years assay out, and she's been kind, but kind isn't a Bench. And the rest of them look at me as if I'd come to read the meter."

Sorle looked at her for a long while.

Then he opened the drawer of his desk, the top drawer on the right, which stuck. He had to work it loose. He took something out of it and held it in his hand a moment, looking at it, before he put it on the desk between them.

It was a cord. A Bench cord, the kind every aspirant wore at the shoulder of the grey gown, looped and knotted in the Halcyon fashion. This one was black. It was not new. The black had gone a little brown at the knot, where a thumb had worked it.

"I have two aspirants," said Sorle. "Both third-years. Both will assay out this Rime, with distinction, God help the Fellowship. After that I had intended to have none. I've taken no one new in nine years. The Registrar has stopped sending me lists." He did not look at the cord. He looked at the moth. "It is not a good Bench to be on, Miss Vail. You will be known as Sorle's. That is not a recommendation in this building. It will close doors that Aske's white cord would have opened, and Aske's cord, you will forgive me, is no longer opening anything. Your examiners at the Assay will mark you down for the colour on your shoulder before you have lifted a spoon. Crisp won't, because Crisp is honest. Vey will."

"Why?"

"Why will Vey?"

"Why are you doing it?"

Sorle was quiet. The bar of light from the shaft had moved; it lay now across the dead woman's portrait, across her long face and her red collar.

"Because Corvin asked me to," he said at last. "Not in words. Corvin never asked anyone for anything in words, he was too busy talking. But he brought you here. He spent his privilege on you, which in twenty years he had not spent on anyone. And on the night he was—on the sixteenth—he had asked me to come to his Cell at the half after ten. He had something to show me, he said, and someone he wanted me to meet. I assume that was you." He looked at her. "I was late. I am always late. I came up the Stair at a quarter to eleven and his corridor was empty and his door was locked. I thought he had forgotten me, which he often did, and I went to bed in a temper." His mouth twisted. "So. I owe him a half-hour. I can't pay it to him. You'll do."

He pushed the cord an inch across the desk toward her.

"And because I have nothing left to lose," he said, "and you have even less. That's a Bench."

Hester did not pick it up at once. She sat and looked at it: an old black cord on a blotter, worked brown at the knot. She thought of the white one on her shoulder, which Aske had given her in the Gatehouse on Matriculation Day, fumbling with the loop, humming. She thought of what Sorle had said, that it was no longer opening anything. She found that her eyes had gone hot, and she did not let them do anything about it.

She took off the white cord. Her fingers were clumsy. She folded it and put it in her pocket, where she would keep it. Then she picked up the black one and looped it at her shoulder and pulled the knot tight.

"Ta," she said.

"Don't thank me. You'll hate it within the week." Sorle turned back to his desk and pulled a slate toward him and picked up the chalk. "Thursday. The fourth bell. Here. Bring paper; I don't lend it. We'll begin with the arrears calculus, since you clearly can't be trusted to understand anything without first putting it in your mouth."

* * *

They began with the arrears calculus.

It was, Hester discovered over the next three days, a great deal of arithmetic dressed up as philosophy. She was good at arithmetic. She was not good at philosophy, and she said so. Sorle said that this put her ahead of most of the Conclave, who were good at neither but could tell the difference between them at dinner.

He wrote on the slate. He wrote fast, in a hand that sloped backward. He talked while he wrote, and broke off to wipe out what he had written with the side of his fist, so that by the second afternoon his sleeve was white to the elbow.

"Arrears," he said, "is pressure. Not a metaphor. Pressure. A keeping holds a thing at one moment. The world goes on to the next moment, and the next, and the thing does not. Between where the thing is and where the world is, there is a difference. The difference *wants*. It wants to close." He drew two lines on the slate, one flat, one rising away from it. "That wanting is arrears. It grows with time." He tapped the rising line. "It grows with the mass of what is kept." He drew the line steeper. "And it grows with how *lively* the thing was. A stone hardly minds being kept. A man minds very much. A man running minds more than a man asleep. A sea—" He stopped. He wiped the slate with his fist. "Never mind a sea. Not today."

"Why not today?"

"Because today you are learning arithmetic." He wrote. "Every casing pays its arrears. It pays them out of its own stillness. Out of the grain's stillness, and the stillness that soaks into the grain from what it holds. Old casings grow stiller. Why? Because they are full. Saturated. They have had years to drink. And so—Miss Vail. *Still runs to quick.* You've seen it. You've seen it twice now, once in my class and once on your knees in a hole in Lowside. Join a full old casing by live brine to something with a great deal of arrears and very little stillness to pay them with. What happens?"

"The stillness runs out of the casing," said Hester. "Into the thing that's owing."

"Into the thing that's owing. It pays the other thing's debt." He drew an arrow. "And if the other thing is very large, and very old, and owes a very great deal, it will drink a charity ward dry and still be thirsty."

She looked at the arrow.

"A quickening," she said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." She was cold. The study was always cold, but she was colder. Two miles east. A Halcyon at the end of it. And in the Halcyon, five fathoms down, pockets that were warm and wet and owed a very great deal, and a Choir that sang slow to pay it. She had stood in that line. She had poured herself into it with sixty others and felt it drink.

She looked at the thought, and then she put it down, the way you put down a sample you have not yet weighed.

"There are a great many rooms in the Halcyon," said Sorle, watching her. "You have a pipe that goes east and a building at the end of it. That is all you have."

"I know."

"Then go on with the arithmetic," he said, and his eyes were sharper than his voice. "If a stay is a pouring-in of stillness to pay a quickening's arrears, what does the quickening do?"

"Quiets."

"For how long?"

She thought. She thought of the warm wet salt in the Fifth, and the brass stay-lines, and the sixty of them singing slow to lock their breath. She thought of a pocket that had been held down on the Tuesday and was warm again by the Saturday.

"Not long," she said. "Not long, and then it's back. Worse."

"Worse. Why worse?"

"Because—" She looked at the rising line on the slate. "Because you've not paid anything. You've just added to it. You've kept the keeping. It still owes everything it owed, and now it's had longer to owe it."

"Stays saturate," said Sorle softly. "Pour enough still into a quickening and it quiets, and grows hungrier. The Fathomers know it. Orsolya Thane knows it better than anyone alive; she's been pouring for thirty years. They've no other tool. They've never wanted one. A stay is a lid, Miss Vail. That is all a stay is."

Hester sat very still.

*You lid a spitting pan, she'll blow the lid through the roof.* Her father in the steam, drawing the fire out a shovelful at a time. *Lid's for when you're in a hurry.*

"What if you let it out?" she said.

Sorle looked at her.

"What if you didn't lid it," Hester said. "What if you didn't pour stillness in. What if you let the—the owing out. A bit at a time. So it's paid."

"Then the moment *proceeds*." Sorle's voice was very quiet. "A kept thing that is let out goes on from where it stopped. That's the Third Law; a child knows it. A kept apple let out goes on being an apple. A kept man goes on bleeding. And a quickening—" He stopped. He put down the chalk. "A quickening is not an apple, Miss Vail. We do not know what a quickening is. We know it is warm and wet and sounds like surf. If you let one go on from where it stopped, you would find out what it stopped *in the middle of*. In the Fifth Fathom of a building with three thousand people in it."

"Not in the Fifth Fathom. Somewhere else. Somewhere it couldn't—"

"No." He said it flatly, and wiped the slate with his fist, all of it, the lines and the arrow and the rising curve. "No. That is not a thought for a first-year with a black cord three days old. It's not a thought for anyone. Do you know how I came by my nickname? I had a thought about the sea. One thought. I said it aloud in Conclave, and Orsolya Thane took it apart with sums in front of two hundred Readers. She was quite right about the sums. I have been paying for that thought for thirty-two years." He turned back to the slate and wrote, fast, a new line of arithmetic, nothing to do with anything. "Arrears of a moth. Kept forty years. Mass a quarter-grain. Work it."

She worked it. She got it right. He grunted.

But when she looked up from her paper his chalk had stopped, and he was not looking at the slate. He was looking at the portrait on the wall, at the long-faced woman with the Physic red at her collar, and his face had the expression of a man listening for something in another room.

"*A bit at a time,*" he said, under his breath. "*So it's paid.*" And then, without turning: "Arrears of a man, kept three months. Mass ten stone. Hush grain. Work it."

She looked at him.

"Work it, Miss Vail."

She worked it. It was a very large number. She did not say whose it was. Neither did he.

* * *

Agathe's letter came with the carrier on the twentieth of Fallow, in the round copybook hand.

*My dear. Mr. Pell has been very quiet since his sister's lying-in and has had the cellar grating mortared over in the old way, very thick, by a man from the Water Board. He has said nothing to me and I have said nothing to him. On the morning after, I went down for the oil and the brine in the drain was gone and the drain was dry, and the weeping on our people stopped for two days. On the third day it began again. I do not know what that means. I expect you do.*

*Your lad is worse. Not much, but worse. The lines on him are longer and there is a thinness at the foot of the casing that I can see the light through, if I hold my candle low. The Linnow girl in the second row is worse than he is. Her mother comes every day now, though she is seventy-nine and walks with two sticks, and sits on the step by the plinth and does not say anything. The brinewright has been and says recasting will not hold on her. I have asked him to look at your lad and he says the same.*

*I pray for you. I pray for them. I do not know any longer what to pray for, except that you find what it is, and quickly, and that the Tide carry you when you do.*

*A.L.*

Hester read it twice, standing at the window of R-41 with the White going grey behind the glass. Then she folded it along its creases. She put it in the tin box under her bed, where the rod had been and was not. She sat down on the bed in her grey gown with the black cord at her shoulder and looked at her hands.

Teo came in at the sixth bell with saltcake wrapped in a cloth and found her still sitting there.

He did not ask. He put the saltcake on the sill and sat down on his own bed opposite and waited. After a while she held out the letter, and he read it, and gave it back.

"Two days," he said.

"Two days."

"They'd one line cut. One rod, out of fourteen. And it stopped for two days, and then they'd mended it." He turned the cloth of saltcake over in his hands without opening it. "So it can stop. That's what it means, Hester. It can be stopped."

"For two days."

"For two days," Teo said, "is not nothing."

Outside, the Lantern came round and flashed, and the light went across the White and across the window and across the two of them sitting there. Then it was gone, and the room was grey again. Hester sat with the letter in her lap and her hands on the letter and did the arithmetic of a thinning casing in her head. She did it again. It came out the same both times.
