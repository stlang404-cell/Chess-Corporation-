# Chapter 15

## Ferrers

> The Purchaser shall make no enquiry as to the provenance of the said grain beyond the Vendor's warrant, and shall hold the Vendor harmless in any matter arising from its resale.
>
> — *Contract of sale between the Office of the W.L.F. and Messrs. Ferrers & Lisk, clause 7*

Lucan went home on the twenty-second of Fallow and came back the same night with a face like a shut door.

Hester was on the Choir Steps when he found her. It was the hour after the Lantern Hour. Most of the Halcyon was still at the Long Table, and the outdoor amphitheatre on the dome's eastern flank was empty, its tiers of salt gone blue in the dusk. In summer the Choir rehearsed here, and Lowside heard them across the White on still evenings. In Fallow nobody came. Hester came because it was the one place on the Shelf where nobody would sit down beside her and want to talk about the Assay. She was sitting on the third tier with her knees drawn up and her gown wrapped round them, looking east. There was nothing to the east but white going grey, and, a quarter of a mile off, the low dark hump of the Well-Cap on its chains, catching the last of the light.

She heard him come down the tiers behind her. He walked carefully still; the ribs were three weeks mended and he still went down steps as if each one might have an opinion. He sat on the tier above hers, a little to the side, not close.

"I've seen my father," he said.

She waited.

"He was at dinner. We had a turbot, kept, from Hask. My father is fond of fish. It reminds him that he can afford to be." Lucan's voice was perfectly even, the voice he used in Vey's Brinescript class. "After dinner I went to his library to fetch a book. I fetched the book. I also opened the drawer in his desk where he keeps the Ferrers and Lisk contracts for the current year, because I was eleven when I found out where he kept the key, and he has never moved it. He thinks it is a very clever hiding place. It is inside a hollowed copy of *The Duties of a Magistrate*, which no one in our family has ever opened for any other reason."

"Lucan."

"I'm coming to it." He took a folded paper from inside his coat. "I didn't take the original. I copied it at his desk, by his lamp, with his pen, in about a quarter of an hour, and put everything back. Then I went to find him. He was in the long room with his port, and I asked him about it." He held the paper out to her without looking at her. "Read it first."

She unfolded it. It was a long sheet in Lucan's tidy, slanting, schoolroom hand. The hand was faster and less tidy than usual toward the bottom.

*Articles of agreement made this 3rd of Thaw, 903 Y.E., between the Office of the W.L.F., the Halcyon Collegium of the Grain (hereinafter the Vendor), and Messrs. Ferrers & Lisk, Exchequer Yard, Ambry (hereinafter the Purchaser).*

*1. The Vendor shall supply, at such intervals as are convenient to the Vendor, quantities of residual stay grain, being grain removed in the course of maintenance from the lower workings, in sealed cases, at the Purchaser's landing at the Dry Harbour.*

*2. The Purchaser shall convey the said grain by its own vessels to such markets beyond the Rim as it sees fit, and shall not offer it for sale within the Free City or within one hundred miles thereof.*

There was more. There were prices, per case; they were very high. There was a clause about relabelling at the Purchaser's discretion for the convenience of foreign customs. There was clause seven, about provenance. And at the bottom, where the signatures were, Lucan had copied them as he found them, imitating the hands as far as he was able:

*For the Vendor: E. Dunmore, clerk, per W.L.F.*

*For the Purchaser: A. Ferrers.*

Hester read it twice. Then she read the signatures a third time.

"*Not within one hundred miles,*" she said. "And the *Wren* came into the dry harbour with forty crates of it marked Shelf for curing."

"Yes. I asked him about that." Lucan was looking at the Well-Cap. "I said, Father, this grain is not Shelf. It is not Middling, or Deep. It's from the stays. Below the Fifth, very likely; quickened; the Customs schedule doesn't even have a word for it. It is a felony under the Charter to sell it at all. And you have been sending it to Quell to have its papers changed and bringing it back into Ambry to sell to the curing-houses at Shelf prices with Deep profits, because the Hask market has gone soft this year. Which," he said, "I had worked out on the Causeway dray, and was rather proud of."

"What did he say?"

"He asked me if I wanted more port." Lucan's mouth moved. "Then he said that the Halcyon sold him the grain, and the Halcyon's warrant was on the contract, and it was the Halcyon's business where the Halcyon got it. He said that if the Halcyon had a Charter it might look to its own Charter. He said—" Lucan stopped. When he went on, his sentences had got shorter. "He said the Halcyon's dirty laundry keeps our lamps lit. He said I was to stay out of it. He said that if I had any notion of carrying tales to the Proctory I should remember who paid my fees, and who had got me onto the Deputy Chancellor's Bench, and why. He was quite calm. He's never calm with me. He was calm."

The salt of the Choir Steps was cold through Hester's gown. Out on the White the last light went off the Well-Cap and it was only a shape.

"Why did Vey take you?" she asked.

"Because my father asked him to. At dinner. Over a turbot." He laughed, very slightly. "I thought it was because I was good."

"You are good."

"I am adequate. I am also the son of the Lord Exchequer." He turned his head and looked at her for the first time since he had sat down. In the dusk his face looked younger and more tired than she had seen it. "That is how it works, Vail. You'll have noticed. Everything in this building is somebody's favour to somebody. My father does the Deputy Chancellor a kindness and the Deputy Chancellor does my father one, and somewhere at the bottom of it is a ward full of people in the Cold House. And the man at the top of it eats turbot and tells his son to stay out of it." He looked at the paper in her hands. "He signed it. My father. *A. Ferrers.* I watched him sign things my whole childhood. I'd know that A anywhere."

She did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would not have been a kindness, and she did not think he wanted one, and she did not know how to give it. After a while she folded the paper and held it out to him. He shook his head.

"Keep it," he said. "I've no drawer that isn't his."

* * *

She did not need to look it up. She looked it up anyway, because that was what she did.

The Halcyon Calendar was printed every Thaw by the Collegium press and stood in a row of fat grey volumes in the Brinestacks reference alcove, one for each year back to 612. It listed every office of the Collegium, every Reader and his Bench, every Fellow, every aspirant. And at the front, under *Abbreviations Used in Collegium Correspondence*, it listed the offices by their initials. She had looked at that page on her second day, looking for nothing, the way she looked at every list she was handed. She had not remembered it. She had remembered there was such a page.

She found it by lamplight in the alcove at the eleventh bell, with the Brinestacks empty around her. Somewhere on the far side of the gallery Callow sat at his desk, so still he might have been one of the cases.

*W.U.F. — Warden of the Upper Fathoms.*

*W.L.F. — Warden of the Lower Fathoms.*

*W.S. — Warden of the Stacks.*

*W.L. — Warden of the Light.*

She sat with the book open in front of her for some time.

*Warden of the Lower Fathoms.* Orsolya.

Then she did what she had spent twenty-one years teaching Customs House clerks not to do. She did it carefully, step by step, the way a clerk adds a column he wants to come out a certain way.

The contract was signed *E. Dunmore, clerk, per W.L.F.* Not by the Warden. *Per.* On behalf of the office. And the office of the Warden of the Lower Fathoms was not one woman. It was a gallery in the Fifth with three clerks in it, and a store, and the Choir rolls, and the pension book, and correspondence with the Delvers' Guild and the Lattice Yards and the Water Board. It was the sort of office that put its initials on a hundred letters a week, most of which the Warden would never see. Hester knew how offices worked. She had worked in one for twenty-one years. Comptroller Mallory had put his name to a great many things he had never read, and some he had read and wished he had not.

And there had been a man in grey wool at the dry harbour, by the dray, with a lantern and a paper. A clerk. Pell had said it too: *a clerk, he wore a coat over his gown and he never gave his name.*

*E.D.* Edwin Dunmore. The bland man with the inky cuffs who sat at the outer desk of the Warden's Gallery and wrote down the Choir's hours and handed out the stipend on Fridays. He never forgot a name and never remembered a face. Hester had collected three sols from him every Friday since the twentieth of Russet and could not have told you the colour of his eyes.

Dunmore. Who signed for the office. Who had the seals. And who, if he was selling quickened grain to Ferrers and Lisk behind the Warden's back, would want the stays held down whatever it cost. Who might very well have found an old line in the old plans and opened it. Who answered, perhaps, not to the Warden at all but to somebody who could make a length of brass disappear out of a locked Proctory store. To a man in a dove-grey coat who knew her name.

It added up. It added up quite well, if you were careful which figures you carried.

She sat in the alcove and looked at it. She was aware, at the back of her mind, the place where she kept the soft spot in the floor, that she was carrying some figures and not others. She did not look there. Orsolya had put a cold hand on her shoulder at Aske's door. Orsolya had wrapped her in a blanket in the Fifth, after her first stay, with the same hands, and said *well done, chick* in a voice that could have come from Coldwater Street. Orsolya had buried eleven choristers in five years and gone to every funeral.

Orsolya was Lowside.

Hester shut the Calendar. She put it back on its shelf, in its place, between 902 and 904.

* * *

"And the Warden?" said Lucan.

They were in the Reading Well off the north gallery of the Brinestacks the next afternoon. It was a small round room with a brine basin set in the floor and a bench round the wall. Teo was sitting on the bench with Lucan's copy of the contract on his knee. Hester had laid it out for them. Dunmore; the office; *per*; the clerk at the dray; the clerk in Pell's cellar; the vanished rod; Vey in the corridor.

"What about the Warden?" Hester said.

"It's her office. Her initials." Lucan was leaning against the wall with his arms folded. "Her stays, if the line goes to the stays, and Sorle seems to think—"

"Sorle doesn't know where the line goes. Neither do I. And it isn't her signature."

"No. It's her clerk's."

"Clerks sign for their offices every day. Mallory's sub-clerk signed for the Customs House forty times a morning. It didn't mean Mallory knew what he'd signed." She heard her voice harden and could not stop it. "She's not a thief, Ferrers. She's not a—she's held the Fifth for thirty years. She's buried eleven of her own. You've not stood in that line. You don't know what it costs her."

"I know what it costs the Cold House," said Lucan.

The basin in the floor had a skin of brine on it, dead, flat. Teo looked down at it and did not look up.

"I'm not saying she did it," Lucan said, more gently. "I'm saying you've not asked."

"I'll ask."

"Will you?"

"I said I'll ask." She stood up. She was taller than him, as she was taller than most people, and she found she was using it, and stopped. "She's offered to help me with my release. Private lessons. Wednesday. I'll ask her then about the residue contracts. I'll ask her about Dunmore."

"And if she says what you want her to say?"

"Then she'll have said it."

Lucan looked at her a long moment. Then he said, without any heat at all, "You know, you are doing what I did with my father. For about twenty-three years."

She had no answer to that. She went out. Behind her she heard Teo say, low, "Leave her be," and Lucan say something she did not catch, and then the door of the Reading Well swung shut on its weight and she was in the gallery among the stacks. The brinescripts in their cases glimmered faintly in the lamplight, ten thousand salt tablets remembering being touched.

* * *

The Warden's Gallery lay at the bottom of the Stair. It was the last landing before the iron gates of the Fifth, a long low room cut into the salt with the grain left raw on the walls. Dunmore's desk stood at one end and the Warden's at the other, and between them were the Choir rolls on their racks, a stove, a kettle, a horsehair settee and a rack of grey wool blankets. There was always a kettle on. There was always someone on the settee, coming up out of a stay with blue lips, being given tea.

On Wednesday at the fifth bell there was no one but Orsolya, because Orsolya had sent them all away.

"Sit down before you fall down, chick." She poured. "You look like a week of wet Fridays. That Sorle working you?"

"Arithmetic."

"Ha. He would." Orsolya put the cup into Hester's hands and closed her own hands round it, round Hester's, briefly. Her fingers were cold as stone. They always were. Thirty years of holding had done that; it had whitened her hair at fifty and slowed her heart to something Hester could feel, if she stood close, like a clock in another room. "Black cord. I saw. Well. There's worse than Benedek, though I'd not say it to his face, and he'd not thank me if I did. He hates me, you know. I took him apart once in Conclave. I was twenty-six and pleased with myself and he was wrong. He was wrong in a way that would have got people killed." She sat down on the settee beside Hester with her own cup. "Still. He's not a fool. If he's taken you, he's seen something."

"He says I hold like a miser."

"Crisp said that. I heard it. Everyone heard it." Orsolya laughed, a short Lowside bark. "She's not wrong. You hold like a Coldwater landlady with the rent. Nothing comes out once it's in. Well, there's no shame in it. There's places it's what's wanted. In the line, it's what's wanted. But you'll not pass the Assay if you can't let your own moth go, and Maudelaine'll not give you so much as a *wanting* if you can't. So." She set down her cup. "Let's see you."

They worked for an hour. Orsolya had a basin of live brine on a stand by the stove, and a dropper, and she kept drops for Hester and made Hester return them, one after another. A drop falling: kept, a bead of clear salt hanging in the air above the basin. Then Hester put her hand to it, wet, and went into the hold, and tried to come out of it the other way. The release. The giving-up.

She could not do it. She could feel the drop in its casing, still, held, owing; she could feel exactly what it wanted. She could not open her hand. Each time she tried, something in her closed tighter round it, the way your fist closes round a coin when someone jostles you in a crowd.

"No. No. You're pushing." Orsolya's cold fingers were on her wrist. "You're trying to push it out. You can't push a release, chick. It's not a door. Don't push it. Open your hand."

"I am opening it."

"You're thinking about opening it. That's not the same." Orsolya sat back. She looked at Hester a moment with her head on one side. "Who taught you to hold? No one, I know. Who taught you to keep?"

"No one."

"Somebody did. Everybody learns it off somebody. You learned it off somebody you watched, who never let anything go." She did not wait for an answer. "Well. Never mind. It comes, or it doesn't. I'll tell you what my da used to say. He was a delver. Coldbank, the old pit east of the Weeps. Haskish man, big as a door; he married a Lowside girl and never went home. He used to say, the salt's not yours, Orsi. You only borrow it. Hold it like a borrowed thing." She looked at the stove. "The Coldbank roof came in when I was eleven. Eighty men. He was in the third gallery. They kept him, the ones that got there first; the Halcyon had a Physic on the site and she kept him with Middling out of a bucket, standing up, the way they found him. Under the beam." She drank her tea. "He was in the Cold House nineteen years. Then the casing started weeping, and the brinewright said it wouldn't take another coat. I signed for him at thirty. That's how I know your Coldwater Street, chick. I've stood where you stand."

Hester had not known that. She sat with the cup in her hands and did not know what to say.

"Well," said Orsolya, briskly. "Enough of that. Watch me. I'll show you, and maybe it'll go in through your eyes, since it won't go in through your hands."

She got up and took a candle from the shelf, an ordinary tallow candle in a tin holder, and lit it at the stove. She set it on the stand beside the brine basin. The flame stood up straight in the still air of the Gallery, yellow, a little smoky at the tip. Orsolya dipped her left hand in the basin to the wrist and held it, dripping, beside the flame.

"Kept flame," she said. "You'll not have seen one. They're not allowed in lamps; somebody always forgets and returns one in a hayloft. But it's good for teaching. A flame's all motion. It's nothing *but* motion. If you can keep a flame and let it go clean, you can keep anything." She went still. Hester watched her go: the slowing breath, the colour leaving her lips, the stillness spreading down through her shoulders into her arm like frost down a pane. Then, very lightly, Orsolya flicked her wet fingers at the candle.

The brine took the flame in mid-air.

It did not put it out. That was the thing that made Hester's breath catch. The flame did not go out. It stopped. It hung there on its wick inside a skin of clear salt the size of a hen's egg, yellow and smoky-tipped and perfectly motionless. It was a little fold of fire, every edge of it sharp, as if somebody had cut it out of a painting of a candle and stood it up on the stand. It gave no heat. It gave light. The light did not flicker. Nothing in the Gallery had ever been so still.

And in the salt around it, as the casing set and cleared, a pattern came up into the grain.

It was very fine. Hester would not have seen it if she had not been looking for nothing, the way she looked at everything. It was like the grain in a piece of wood, or the whorl in a thumb: a set of faint lines in the crystal, all curving the same way. It was a spiral. It began at the base of the casing where it met the wick and wound up and round the flame and closed at the top. It turned to the left, against the sun.

"There," said Orsolya. She came out of the hold, blinking, and looked at the casing with a craftsman's satisfaction. "See that? Every worker leaves a hand in the grain, chick. Like your writing. Yours is all squares. I've seen it. Like a ledger. Maudelaine's is flat, like pages in a book; Corvin's was all spokes, God rest him, like a cartwheel." She tilted her head at the spiral. "Mine's a corkscrew. Always has been. My mam said I came out twisting."

Hester looked at the spiral.

Something in her went toward it the way a tongue goes toward a broken tooth. There was a place in her mind where she had put something down, at a cold door on the Third Fathom, on the seventeenth of Russet. It was something she had seen and noted and not understood, and set aside to come back to. Then there had been Gage, and the porters, and Orsolya's hand on her shoulder, and she had not come back to it. The thing in her went toward that place. It got as far as the edge.

She did not let it go further. She did not decide not to. She simply found that she was looking at the flame, and not at the grain. She was thinking how beautiful it was, a fold of fire stood up in glass, and how Tib would have liked it. And when Orsolya said, "Now. Watch the release," she watched the release.

Orsolya put her wet fingertips to the casing and went still again, and this time Hester saw it, or saw something. The stillness did not go out of Orsolya so much as *loosen*, the way a knot loosens when you stop pulling on both ends. Her hand did not move. It opened from inside. The salt clouded, softened, ran, and the flame was suddenly flickering, warm, ordinary, on its wick in a little puddle of brine. It gave off a single sharp tick of heat that Hester felt on her face, as if somebody had opened an oven door for an instant and shut it.

"Arrears," said Orsolya. "Half a minute's worth of candle. Not much. See how I did it? I didn't push. I just stopped holding on."

"Yes," said Hester.

"Try it with the drop."

She tried it with the drop. She could not do it. But she was nearer, she thought; for an instant she felt her hold go soft at the edge, like the skin of a pan, before it closed again.

"Better," said Orsolya. "There. That's the first time I've seen you *want* to."

* * *

Afterward, with the kettle on again, she asked.

She asked the way she would have asked Mallory about a manifest: plainly, looking at her cup. She said that she had been in the Brinestacks and had seen the office's initials on some papers. She did not say which papers. She said she understood the Warden's office sold off the grain it took out of the stays, and she wondered how that worked, under the Charter. She asked whether it was quickened grain; whether it was Hush. She said she had worked in Customs and could not help wondering.

Orsolya did not stiffen. She did not look away. She leaned back on the horsehair settee and stretched her cold hands out toward the stove and said, comfortably, "Oh, that's Edwin's. The residue contracts. Edwin handles all that; I'd not know a bill of sale from a hymn sheet." She nodded down the room at Dunmore's empty desk, with its neat stacks and its inkstand. "What we take off the stays has to go somewhere. You can't put it back in the ground, and you'd not want it in the Halcyon; it spits. So it goes for sale abroad. Hask buys it for the mines. They want quick grain for blasting, of all things, it's got a kick to it. It's all above board. Conclave-approved, years since. The money goes back to the Choir. Pensions. Widows. God knows they need it."

"It's Hush grain, though," said Hester. "Some of it. The schedule says—"

"The Customs schedule." Orsolya smiled at her, fond and a little rueful, the smile of a Lowside woman for a Lowside girl who has been reading too much. "Chick, the Customs schedule was written by a man in Ambry who's never been below the First Fathom. Stay residue's not Hush grain. Not the way the Charter means it. It's spent. Used up. It's had its stillness poured out of it into a stay, like your twice-used casing grain. You know. You've tasted it. There's nothing in it but a kick." She drank. "Edwin keeps it all in order. He's a dull dog, but he's never lost a sol."

Hester sat with her cup. She felt something in her chest unclench. It did not unclench all the way; it unclenched the way a hand does when you stop holding a coin so tight, but keep holding it.

"Ta," she said.

"Don't *ta* me. Drink your tea. You've a shift at the ninth."

* * *

The ninth-bell shift went down at the ninth bell, forty of them in grey wool, singing slow on the Stair. Hester was in the second rank, between Ama Tessaly and a broad young delver's son from Coldbank called Fenwick, who sang flat and held like a wall. They went through the iron gates of the Fifth and down the long galleries, where the brass stay-lines ran along the walls in their sheaths. They went to the eastern stay, which had been warm on the Monday.

It was warm again. The salt underfoot was damp and soft, and there was a smell in the gallery of something green and cold that Hester had no name for. Behind the walls, very faint, a sound like breath drawn in through teeth and let out, and drawn in.

They took their places along the line. They laid their wet hands on the brass. They sang the first slow bars of the work-song to lock their breathing, *hold the line, hold the line*. Then they went down into the hold together, forty of them, and poured.

And it was easy.

It had been easy on the Monday, and the Friday before, and every shift since she had joined. She had not had another shift to compare it to, only Ama's face and Fenwick's. Fenwick said, after, that he'd never known a season like it, he'd been on the line three years and some autumns the stays had nearly had him off his feet. And Ama said nothing, but sat afterward on the horsehair settee in the Gallery with her eyes shut and her hands open on her knees. Ama had been on the line forty years.

Tonight Hester felt it. She had not let herself feel it before. In the hold, in the line, with her stillness running out of her through her wet palms into the brass and away down the line toward the warm wall, she felt that the brass was not empty. There was something in it already. Before her, beside her, under her. Something cold and very old and very still was already in the line when the Choir's stillness reached it. It took the weight of the pour as a full bucket takes the weight of the next cup, without needing much. It came from the other direction. From behind her. From the east trunk, where the main stay-line came in through the gallery wall from somewhere far off.

She did not taste it. She was in the hold and her mouth was shut and her hands were on the brass, and you could not taste through your hands. She only felt it, the way you feel through a floor that there is somebody in the room below.

She came out of the hold at the end of the shift, cold and slow, with the lag sitting on her like wet wool. Ama Tessaly put a blanket round her shoulders. Up in the Gallery, Dunmore was at his desk, writing down the hours in his neat hand, not looking up. At the other end of the room Orsolya was on the settee with her eyes closed and a cup of tea going cold in her lap. The kept candle-flame was gone from the stand, returned, and only a ring of dried salt showed where it had been.

Hester stood a moment in the doorway with the blanket round her and looked at the ring of salt. Then she went up the Stair, past the chalked names of the Settled, all the way to the top, slowly, because her legs were slow. She did not think about anything at all, very carefully, the whole way.
