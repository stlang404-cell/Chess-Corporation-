# Chapter 16

## Annealing

> Glass remembers how it cooled.
>
> — *Proverb of the Quell glassmakers*

The boots were the first thing.

They were Quell boots, which meant they were good. They were black calf, cut high, laced through brass eyes, with a welted sole that would take the Rookery's salt-glazed floors for ten winters without splitting. Hester knew what boots like that cost because she had once seized forty pairs of them at the dry harbour. A Hask trader had tried to bring them in as "personal effects," and Daisy had valued them at a sol and a half the pair, wholesale. Saskia Orme came down to the Long Table in them on the second of Cinder. She sat across from Hester and ate her porridge as if her feet had always been inside good leather.

Hester noticed. She noticed most things that had a price, and she did not, at first, do anything about it. People bought boots. Winter was coming down off the Varrow and the Rookery was cold enough to crack a water jug. It was none of her business.

The second thing was the hours. Saskia's cell was three doors along from R-41, and its door had a hinge that shrieked. Twice that week Hester, lying awake with the Fifth Fathom's cold still in her bones from a Choir shift, heard it shriek after the twelfth bell. The second time she got up and looked out. Saskia was coming along the passage in her outdoor coat with her collar up, carrying her boots in her hand so as not to wake anyone. There was salt-mud on the coat's hem to the knee. That was not the mud of the Pans or the Causeway, which was grey. It was the white paste of the harbour ramps, where the landships came up and the harbour-men raked the salt smooth every morning and the runners churned it every night.

The third thing was the money.

Hester did not go looking for it. She was in the Bursary on the First Fathom on the third, paying her Choir fine for a missed shift. She had lagged at the Stay the day before and come round on the gallery floor ten minutes after her relief should have taken over, and the Choir docked you for that whether it was your fault or not. The bursary clerk was a Harlish boy with a cold. While he wrote her receipt she read, upside down, the open page of the ledger at his elbow, because she had read ledgers upside down for twenty-one years and could not stop. *Orme, S. Lumen. Fees Thaw and Turning, paid in full. Additional: remitted to Glassmarket Infant School, Ambry, for P. Orme, the year in advance, 6 sols.*

A Thaw aspirant on a Lumen bursary paid her fees by the term, late, with a note. Hester had seen Saskia do it in Russet, standing at this same window with her jaw set. Now she had paid two terms at once, and her daughter's school for a year, and she had new boots.

Hester took her receipt and went up the Stair, and did the sum in her head the whole way, and did not like the answer.

* * *

She found her in the kilns.

The Lumen kilns were on the Third Fathom, at the end of a long corridor that grew warmer every yard. They were the only warm place in the Halcyon below the Crown, and the glaziers knew it, and guarded them like a guild. By day they were full: aspirants feeding the furnaces, Bassano in his rings shouting about colour, the great sun-presses on the Crown sending light down brass tubes to be pressed into grain. By night only the annealing ovens were kept going. The glaziers called them lehrs. They were long brick tunnels, warm as a bread oven, where the lantern-jars made that day crept on iron trays from the hot end to the cool end over twelve hours. Somebody had to sit with them, turning the dampers and walking the trays forward a hand's breadth at a time. Saskia took that duty three nights a week for a fleck an hour. She had told Hester so in Russet, when they had still been needling each other, as evidence that some people had to work.

She was there now, sitting on an upturned crate at the cool end of the long lehr, in her shirtsleeves, with her new boots crossed at the ankle. A ledger of tray-times lay on her knee. The light in the kiln room was the dull red of the lehr's mouth and the clear gold of three kept-light jars on a shelf, and in it Saskia's face was all angles.

"Vail," she said, without looking up. "If you've come to borrow heat, it's a fleck a quarter-hour. Bassano's rule."

"I've come about your boots."

Saskia did look up then.

"They're very nice," she said. "Thank you. Quellish. My cousin makes them."

"Your cousin charges a sol and a half, wholesale. I've valued them." Hester stood just inside the door. She had not decided how to do this. She had come down the corridor with the sum in her head and nothing else, and now she was here, and the heat was on her face. "And you paid two terms' fees on the first. And a year at the Glassmarket school for Pip. Six sols. That's thirty sols, near enough, since Russet. You're on a Lumen bursary and a fleck an hour for watching ovens."

Saskia set the ledger down on the crate beside her carefully, as if it were a tray of glass.

"You read my bursary page."

"It was open."

"You *read my bursary page*." Her voice was quite level. "Do you do this to everyone? Or only the ones from across the White? Is it a customs thing? Do you go through people's pockets for contraband?"

"Only when there's contraband in them."

It was the wrong thing to say, and she knew it as she said it. Saskia stood up.

"Get out of my kiln," she said.

"Who pays you, Orme?"

"Get *out*."

"You come in at the twelfth bell with harbour paste on your coat to the knee," Hester said. "Ramp-salt. From the landship quays. I know it; I walked on it for twenty-one years. Three nights a week. Not the nights you sit with the lehr. The other three." She heard her own voice going flat and hard, the voice she had used on Mallory, the customs voice. She could not seem to find another. "There's a man in grey wool who oversaw forty crates of felony grain being carried out of the Ambry bond-shed in the middle of the night. Somebody brought that grain to the harbour in the first place. Somebody carried it out of here. Was it you?"

The lehr ticked. Somewhere along its length a tray of jars, cooling, made a sound like a bell struck with a fingernail.

Saskia looked at her for a long time. Then she sat down again on the crate, quite slowly, and put her elbows on her knees and her face in her hands. For an instant Hester thought she was crying. She was not. When she took her hands away her eyes were dry and her mouth was tight with something that was more like disgust than shame.

"Two sols a run," she said.

* * *

It came out in pieces, the way stones come out of a field: not in order, but in the order they were hit.

She had been in the Halcyon since Thaw. She had come over from Quell with Pip and her mother and forty sols. That was what was left of Jory's landship after the Exchequer took its cut and the insurers found a clause. Forty sols, in a city that charged rent like a toll-gate. Bassano's bursary paid her fees and nothing else. She had sold Jory's sextant in the spring—"before you say it, Vail, yes, I know, a landship pilot's sextant, his father's, I sold it to a Hask trader for nine sols and I'd do it again"—and by midsummer she was choosing between her mother's coal and Pip's shoes.

"And then a man came to the kilns," she said. "One night at midsummer. A clerk. Bland, ink on his cuffs. Pleasant. He'd heard I was a hard worker who needed evening hours. He said the Warden's office needed a courier who could keep her mouth shut and her hands steady. Residue grain, he said. Stay salt. It comes up from the Fifth in sealed boxes and goes down to the harbour, to a Ferrers freighter, under contract. Conclave-approved. All above board. They wanted somebody from outside the Choir because Choir people talk, and somebody poor because poor people turn up."

"Dunmore," Hester said.

"I never asked his name. It was on a docket once. E. Dunmore." Saskia's mouth twisted. "He gave me a pass for the Fifth-Fathom lift and a barrow. I'd meet the lift at the tenth bell at the Third Fathom landing. He'd bring the boxes up from below. Four, five, sometimes ten. I'd barrow them out through the Gatehouse on the porters' road and down the Causeway to the harbour. A freighter's mate would sign for them. Two sols. Every time. In silver, in my hand, at the harbour."

"You didn't ask what was in them."

"I knew what was in them. Grain. It said so on the docket."

"You didn't ask why it was going out at night."

"No."

"You didn't ask why a Conclave-approved contract needed a woman with a barrow and not the porters' drays."

"*No*, Vail. I didn't ask." Saskia's voice came up for the first time. "I didn't ask, because if I'd asked, he'd have found someone who didn't. I've a daughter. She's six. Her feet grow. Have you ever had to look at a child's feet, Vail, and do sums? No. You've a brother who doesn't grow at all. You're lucky."

The word hung in the hot air.

"The boxes were warm," Hester said.

Saskia was silent.

"You'd have felt it through the wood. Hush grain is warm, if it's quickened. You've a sense, Orme, you see colour at the edges of things. You'd have seen it on them. Bleeding round the corners like a lamp through a sheet." She took a step into the kiln room. "I tasted that grain. At the Customs House. It came in on the *Constant Wren* labelled Shelf for curing and certified in Quell, which was a lie. It was quickened Hush, which is a felony to sell, and it tastes exactly like the brine that's beading on my brother's casing in the Cold House. And somebody walked it down the Causeway in a barrow at the tenth bell for two sols a run."

"The *Wren*." Saskia had gone pale under the red light. "That was in high summer. They went east. Some of them went east. I didn't know—"

"They went to Quell and came back with a new label. The same grain. I've a twist of it in my pocket."

"I didn't *know*—"

"You didn't ask."

"And you did?" Saskia was on her feet again. "You *did*? Look at your own shoulder, Vail. What's that?"

Hester did not look. She knew what it was. A Choir badge: the little iron wave on a grey ribbon, pinned under the black cord of Sorle's Bench.

"Three sols a week," Saskia said. "Paid out of the Warden's office. The *same office*. Signed by the same bland little man with ink on his cuffs, I'll lay money. Where do you think the Choir stipends come from? The Conclave? The Conclave wouldn't pay for a candle-end if it could keep it. You take his money every Seventhday, Vail, you take it to pay your brother's fee, and you go down into the Fifth and do whatever it is they do there, and you've never once asked where the silver comes from. Have you? Have you *asked*?"

Hester said nothing.

"No," said Saskia. "Because you needed it. Because it was for Tib." She laughed, short and ugly. "You took that gown on a dead man's privilege. You took a Choir place from a Warden you'd known a fortnight. You'd have taken it from the Chancellor's own hand if it paid the Cold House. You're Lowside, so you think you're clean. Lowside isn't clean, Vail. It's just poor. Poor's not the same as clean. Ask anyone in Quell."

"I didn't carry felony grain to the harbour in a barrow."

"No. You just let other people's dead pay your rent."

That one went in.

Hester felt it go in, under the ribs, the way a cold thing goes in. And because it went in she did what she had done all her life when something went in. She hit back with the nearest heavy thing.

"Jory would be proud," she said.

She saw it land. She saw Saskia's face go still, not with the grain-stillness but with the other kind, the kind that comes before a person decides whether to strike you. Saskia did not strike her. She looked at Hester for the length of three ticks of the lehr. Then she sat down on her crate and picked up the ledger of tray-times and opened it on her knee.

"Get out of my kiln," she said, quite quietly. "Please."

Hester went.

* * *

She walked. It was what she did. She went up the Stair to the First Fathom and along the Benches, dark at that hour, and up again to the Crown. She went out through the Gatehouse onto the dome and walked round the Lantern's foot in the cold with her hands in her armpits. It was a hard clear night. The White lay under the stars like a sheet laid out for a body, and far off to the west the lamps of Ambry were a smudge of yellow at the edge of the world. The tiles were ticking. They always ticked in Cinder, when the day's warmth went out of them all at once. A thousand small sounds across the plain, like a room full of people tapping their nails on tables, waiting.

She had not decided whether she was right.

That was the trouble. She knew she was right about the grain. Saskia had carried felony salt for money, and some of it was the salt that tasted like Tib's beads, and that made her part of whatever was happening, whether she had asked or not. But Saskia was right too. The silver in Hester's own purse came up the same Stair from the same office. She had never asked. She had told herself it was because there was nothing to ask, that the Choir was the Choir and the Warden was the Warden. Orsolya had sat her down in that warm office in Russet and given her tea with Hask honey in it and said *I need holds like yours, chick*. Hester had been so grateful, so Lowside-grateful, to be wanted by someone who talked like her mother's sister, that she had not looked at the money at all. She had only counted it.

And she had said the thing about Jory. Jory, who had gone into a sink on the eastern White with a full crew and a cargo of Quell glass. Saskia had told the story on Lantern Night, in the Glassmarket, very quickly, with Pip on her hip, as if it were the price of something.

The tiles ticked. Hester stood at the dome's edge and looked toward the Well-Cap, a quarter of a mile east: a low dark hump on the white, chained, never opened. She thought about her father drawing the fire from under a spitting pan a shovelful at a time. *Lid's for when you're in a hurry.*

She went back in.

* * *

Teo was baking.

He was not supposed to be. Nobody was supposed to bake in the Rookery. Cooking fires were forbidden on the dome because of the draughts, and the Proctors made a point of checking. Teo had solved this in his second week by buying, from a Lumen third-year with gambling debts, a cracked kept-light jar that would not hold a full charge. Released slowly into a covered tin, a jar of midsummer noon gave off enough heat to bake one small saltcake, very badly, in about two hours. The Rookery cell R-41 consequently smelled most nights of burnt sugar and hot salt. Teo sat cross-legged on his bed in his grey Tider shawl, watching the tin as other men watch a sick child.

"You've been fighting," he said, without looking round.

"How do you know?"

"You've got your customs face on. And your knuckles are white. And Saskia Orme came past an hour ago and didn't sneer at our door, which she always does, so either she's dead or she's been in a fight and lost, and she doesn't lose." He lifted the lid of the tin a crack and peered in and let it down again. "Not yet. Ten minutes. Sit."

She sat on her own bed. After a while she told him. She told it badly, in the wrong order, the boots and the bursary and the barrow and the *Wren*, and then what Saskia had said, and then, at the end, what she had said herself. Teo listened to the whole thing without interrupting, which was a skill he had learned in nine years of Tider confession and which she had come to rely on more than she liked.

When she had finished he was quiet a moment.

"Well," he said. "That was a horrible thing to say."

"I know."

"No. I mean truly horrible. It was well made. You've a gift for it. I could feel it from here." He lifted the tin lid again. "And she's right about the stipend. And you're right about the grain. And she's right that you're Lowside and think you're clean. And you're right that she should have asked. I don't think there's a single person in this whole conversation who was wrong about anything, Hester, which is generally how the worst conversations go." He let the lid down. "In the Order we'd have said you were both in a state of grace and both damned and should go and do penance together. Scrubbing the refectory, usually. It works better than you'd think."

"I'm not going to scrub anything with her."

"No," said Teo. "But you might eat something."

There was a knock at the door.

It was not a Proctor's knock, which was three and loud. It was one knock, hard, as if whoever made it had thought about it for some time and decided it would be only one. Teo looked at Hester. Hester looked at the door.

Saskia was standing in the passage with a bottle of Quell clear in one hand and her new boots in the other. She had been crying, or had not been and had decided to look as if she might start. She held the bottle up.

"I've come about your manners," she said.

"I've no manners."

"No. I noticed." Saskia came in without being asked and sat on the end of Teo's bed, since there was nowhere else, and put the boots on the floor and the bottle on the window ledge. "That's my cousin's clear. It's illegal on this side of the White. You can seize it if you like. It's for drinking."

Teo produced, from somewhere in the shawl, three tin cups.

They drank. Quell clear tasted like having your mouth set on fire by a person who loved you. Nobody apologised. Hester had not thought anyone would. She did not know how she would have done it if she had tried, and Saskia, she suspected, knew even less. They sat on the two narrow beds in the cold cell with the White outside the salt-glass window and the burning-sugar smell of Teo's tin. After the second cup Saskia said, to the window and not to Hester, "Jory would have asked."

"I shouldn't have said it."

"No. But he would have. He asked about everything. He asked the insurers why their clause was in Ambry black and the rest of the contract was in Quell blue. They didn't pay out anyway, but he'd have asked." She turned the cup in her fingers. "I'm not Jory. I'm the one who does sums."

"So am I," Hester said.

"I know. That's why I hate you." Saskia drank. "Some days."

The saltcake came out of the tin black on the bottom and raw in the middle and sweet as a sin, and they ate it with their fingers off the lid. Teo said a Tider grace over it that had been composed, he said, for the breaking of a fast after the death of an abbot, and was suitable for most occasions involving sugar and recrimination.

* * *

"Tell me about the lift," Hester said, later.

Saskia told her. The tenth bell. The Third Fathom landing, where the brine-lift from the Fifth came up behind an iron grille that was locked at night. Dunmore, in his grey gown with an iron-grey cord, coming up in the cage with the boxes stacked around his feet like a man in a pantry. He was always alone. He never let her go down.

"How did he open the grille?"

"Keys. He's got a ring. He keeps it in his desk in the Warden's antechamber, the left-hand drawer. He took it out in front of me once, the first time, when he was showing me the route." Saskia frowned, remembering. "A big ring, brass tag on it. *W.L.F. Duplicates.* There must be twenty keys. Grilles, stores, the Choir lockers. And one big ugly old one, iron, black, longer than my hand, with a bird on the bow. A little bird sitting on a line." She drew it in the air. "I asked what that was for, as a joke. He said it was for the Hush, and nobody went there, and I should mind my barrow."

Hester and Teo looked at each other.

"The Hush key," said Teo. "On a duplicate ring. In a clerk's desk."

"Bureaucracy," said Saskia. "It's the same in Quell. The keys to the bishop's crypt are in the sacristan's tea-caddy."

"Did he ever speak about the Warden?" Hester said. "Thane. Did she ever come to the lift?"

"Never. I never saw her once." Saskia hesitated. "He said once, when I dropped a box on the landing, *Careful. The Warden says the Choir eats on this.* I thought it was a manner of speaking. Like, it pays the bills."

Hester heard it, and turned it over, and set it aside, as you set aside a sample you have not got the reagent for yet.

"He'd use her name," she said. "Of course he would. He's her clerk. He'd use her name on anything. That's what clerks do. Mallory used the Exchequer's."

Teo said nothing. He was looking at her with the expression he had sometimes in Fane's lection classes, when he was listening to the Roar under a tablet and had heard something nobody else in the room could hear and was deciding whether to say so.

"There's one more thing," Hester said, because it had to be asked, and she had decided to ask it. "Aske's note. The one Teo read. It ends *Ask O.* Did Aske ever speak to you? About the boxes? About anything?"

Saskia stared at her. Then, for the first time that night, she laughed properly, a bark of real amusement.

"*Orme?* You thought O was *Orme*?" She shook her head. "Vail. Corvin Aske didn't know I existed. I said good morning to him once on the Stair at midsummer and he walked into the wall. He was reading a sample. *Ask O.*" She laughed again, less certainly. "I'm flattered. Truly. Nobody's suspected me of anything interesting since I was nineteen."

"Then who is it?"

Nobody answered. The tiles were ticking out on the White.

* * *

So Hester told her. All of it, because there seemed to be no sense in telling half.

She told her about Tib, and the Cold House, and the beads running east. She told her about the *Wren* and Aske's twelve samples and his card. She told her about the casing in Cell 3-17 and the sheet in the blotter. She told her about the Cistern: the brass rods with Lattice-mason marks in the founders' brick, sleeved in brine so still it made her eyes ache, running east under Coldwater Street toward the Halcyon. She told her about the rod she had taken to Gage, and how Gage had lost it. She told her about Lucan's contract, *W.L.F.*, signed by *E. Dunmore, clerk*. She told her that the charity sleepers in the Cold House were coming back and dying in the last moments of their sicknesses, and that her brother's casing was among them, weeping toward the east.

She did not tell her what Tib had said on the night they kept him. There were limits.

Saskia listened. She did not interrupt either, though she made faces, and once she said "*Lattice marks?* On the rods? Then they were made here," and Hester said yes. When it was finished she sat for some time looking into her empty cup.

"Who else knows?" she said.

"Teo. Sorle. Lucan Ferrers."

"*Ferrers?*" Saskia looked as if she had bitten a lemon. "The Exchequer's son? You've told the Lord Exchequer's son, and he hasn't had you thrown in the Proctory?"

"He found the contract. In his father's house. He's—" Hester stopped, finding she did not know how to describe Lucan Ferrers to someone who had only ever seen him from across the Long Table. "He's not what he looks like."

"Nobody is," said Saskia. "That's the whole trouble with people." She set the cup down on the window ledge. "What do you want from me?"

"I don't know yet."

"Well, decide. I've a lehr to walk at the first bell." But she did not get up. She sat on the end of Teo's bed in her stockinged feet, with her good boots beside her on the floor, and looked out of the salt-glass at the dark White. "I don't do causes, Vail. I told you that in Russet. I do rent."

"I know."

"So understand me. I'm not doing this because I'm sorry." She said it to the window, very evenly. "I am sorry. I'm sorry I carried that grain, and I'm sorrier I didn't ask, and I'll be sorry about it for longer than you'll be angry, I expect. But that's not why. I'm doing it because there are three hundred people kept in that ward, and some of them have daughters. And because if somebody in the Warden's office is bleeding them to pay my two sols a run, then my daughter's school was bought with them. I'll not have that. I'll not have her feet in their shoes." She turned from the window. "So. At cost. Whatever this is, I'll do it at cost."

"At cost," Hester said.

They did not shake hands. Teo, who seemed to think that something ought to be done, poured the last of the Quell clear into the three tin cups and handed them round. They drank it without a toast, because nobody could think of one.

* * *

Lucan came the next night, because Teo sent for him.

He stood in the door of R-41 in his Upper Ward wool with the ochre cord of Vey's Bench on his shoulder. He took in the cell, the two beds, the burnt tin, Saskia Orme sitting cross-legged on Hester's blanket with a ledger, and Hester at the window. His face did the thing it did, the polished blank that Hester had learned meant he was thinking fast and did not want anyone to see.

"Orme," he said.

"Ferrers."

"I was told there was a meeting."

"There's cake," said Teo. "Of a kind."

Lucan looked at the cake. He looked at Saskia. He looked at Hester, and she saw him understand, from her face, that Saskia knew everything, and that it had been Hester's choice to tell her. He did not argue it. He folded himself onto the floor by the door with his back to the wall and his long legs out in front of him, ribs still careful from the sink. He accepted a piece of black-bottomed saltcake from Teo with the gravity of a man accepting a treaty.

"Well," he said. "Here we are."

"Here we are," said Saskia. "The Lord Exchequer's heir, a defrocked Tider, a widow with a barrow, and a customs officer with no job. Somebody should write a ballad."

"Somebody will," said Teo. "They always do. It's usually terrible."

Hester stood at the window with her back to them and listened to the three of them start to talk. It was cautious at first, and then less cautious. Lucan asked Saskia about the docket hand and Saskia asked Lucan about the contract seal. Teo produced Aske's sheet and read it out again in his careful brine-script voice: *Cold House, fourteen weeping. A draw? Still runs to quick. Cistern?? Old lines. Ask O.* She had her ledger open on the sill. In the ledger, under the heading she had written in Russet, *Suspects*, she had crossed out *Orme* that afternoon with a single neat line. Below it, in the space where the next name should go, she had written a letter and nothing else.

*O.*

She looked at it in the glow from Teo's cracked jar. Then she closed the ledger, and turned round, and sat down on the floor with the others. She took the last piece of cake before Lucan could, because he was nearer and slower and had cracked ribs. Out on the White the tiles ticked and ticked in the cold, a thousand small sounds, as if something large were tapping its fingers, waiting for someone to ask it the right question.
