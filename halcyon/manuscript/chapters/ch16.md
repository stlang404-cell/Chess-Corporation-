# Chapter 16

## Annealing

> Glass remembers how it cooled.
>
> — *Proverb of the Quell glassmakers*

The boots were the first thing.

They were Quell boots, which meant they were good: black calf, cut high, laced through brass eyes, with a welted sole that would take the Rookery's salt-glazed floors for ten winters without splitting. Hester knew what boots like that cost because she had once seized forty pairs of them at the dry harbour from a Hask trader who called them "personal effects," and Daisy had valued them at a sol and a half the pair, wholesale. Saskia Orme came down to the Long Table in them on the second of Cinder and ate her porridge as if her feet had always been inside good leather.

Hester noticed; she noticed most things that had a price. People bought boots.

The second thing was the hours. Saskia's cell was three doors along from R-41, and its door had a hinge that shrieked. Twice that week Hester, lying awake with the Fifth Fathom's cold in her bones from a Choir shift, heard it shriek after the twelfth bell. The second time she got up and looked out. Saskia was coming along the passage in her outdoor coat with her collar up, carrying her boots so as not to wake anyone. There was salt-mud on the coat's hem to the knee. Not the grey mud of the Pans or the Causeway: the white paste of the harbour ramps, where the harbour-men raked the salt smooth every morning and the landship runners churned it every night.

The third thing was the money.

Hester did not go looking for it. She was in the Bursary on the First Fathom on the third, paying her Choir fine; she had lagged at the Stay the day before and come round ten minutes after her relief should have taken over, and the Choir docked you for that whether it was your fault or not. While the clerk, a Harlish boy with a cold, wrote her receipt, she read the open ledger at his elbow upside down, because she had read ledgers upside down for twenty-one years and could not stop. *Orme, S. Lumen. Fees Thaw and Turning, paid in full. Additional: remitted to Glassmarket Infant School, Ambry, for P. Orme, the year in advance, 6 sols.*

Saskia paid her fees by the term, late, with a note; Hester had seen her do it in Russet at this same window with her jaw set.

Hester took her receipt and went up the Stair doing the sum, and did not like the answer.

* * *

She found her in the kilns.

The Lumen kilns were on the Third Fathom, at the end of a corridor that grew warmer every yard: the only warm place in the Halcyon below the Crown. By night only the annealing ovens were kept going. The glaziers called them lehrs: long brick tunnels, warm as a bread oven, where the day's lantern-jars crept on iron trays from the hot end to the cool end over twelve hours. Somebody had to sit with them, turning the dampers and walking the trays forward a hand's breadth at a time. Saskia took that duty three nights a week for a fleck an hour. She had told Hester so in Russet, when they were still needling each other, as evidence that some people had to work.

She was sitting on an upturned crate at the cool end of the long lehr, in her shirtsleeves, with her new boots crossed at the ankle and a ledger of tray-times on her knee. In the dull red of the lehr's mouth and the gold of three kept-light jars on a shelf, her face was all angles.

"Vail," she said, without looking up. "If you've come to borrow heat, it's a fleck a quarter-hour. Bassano's rule."

"I've come about your boots."

Saskia did look up then. "They're very nice. Thank you. Quellish. My cousin makes them."

"Your cousin charges a sol and a half, wholesale. I've valued them." Hester stood just inside the door. She had come down the corridor with the sum in her head and nothing else. "And you paid two terms' fees on the first. And six sols for a year at the Glassmarket school. That's thirty sols, near enough, since Russet. You're on a Lumen bursary and a fleck an hour for watching ovens."

Saskia set the ledger down on the crate beside her, carefully, as if it were a tray of glass.

"You read my bursary page."

"It was open."

"You *read my bursary page*." Her voice was level. "Do you do this to everyone? Or only the ones from across the White? Do you go through people's pockets for contraband?"

"Only when there's contraband in them."

It was the wrong thing to say, and she knew it as she said it. Saskia stood up.

"Get out of my kiln."

"Who pays you, Orme?"

"Get *out*."

"You come in at the twelfth bell with harbour paste on your coat to the knee," Hester said. "Ramp-salt, from the landship quays. I walked on it for twenty-one years. Three nights a week, and not the nights you sit with the lehr." She heard her voice go flat and hard, the voice she had used on Mallory, and could not find another. "There's a man in grey wool who oversaw forty crates of felony grain being carried out of the Ambry bond-shed in the middle of the night. Somebody carried that grain out of here in the first place. Was it you?"

The lehr ticked.

Saskia looked at her a long time. Then she sat down again on the crate, slowly, and put her elbows on her knees and her face in her hands. When she took them away her eyes were dry and her mouth was tight with something nearer disgust than shame.

"Two sols a run," she said.

* * *

It came out in pieces, not in order but in the order they were hit.

She had come over from Quell in Thaw with Pip and her mother and forty sols, which was what was left of Jory's landship after the Exchequer took its cut and the insurers found a clause. Forty sols, in a city that charged rent like a toll-gate. Bassano's bursary paid her fees and nothing else. She had sold Jory's sextant in the spring—"before you say it, Vail, yes, his father's, I sold it to a Hask trader for nine sols and I'd do it again"—and by midsummer she was choosing between her mother's coal and Pip's shoes.

"And then a man came to the kilns," she said. "One night at midsummer. A clerk. Bland, ink on his cuffs. Pleasant. He'd heard I was a hard worker who needed evening hours. He said the Warden's office wanted a courier who could keep her mouth shut and her hands steady. Residue grain. Stay salt, up from the Fifth in sealed boxes, down to a Ferrers freighter at the harbour, under contract. Conclave-approved. They wanted somebody from outside the Choir because Choir people talk, and somebody poor because poor people turn up."

"Dunmore," Hester said.

"I never asked his name. It was on a docket once. E. Dunmore." Saskia's mouth twisted. "He gave me a pass for the box-lift and a barrow. It runs from the Fifth to the Third and nowhere else. Three nights a week I'd meet it at the tenth bell at the Third Fathom landing, and he'd bring the boxes up. Four, five, sometimes ten. I'd barrow them out through the Gatehouse on the porters' road and down the Causeway, and a freighter's mate would sign for them. Two sols, every time. In silver, in my hand."

"You didn't ask what was in them."

"I knew what was in them. Grain. It said so on the docket."

"You didn't ask why a Conclave-approved contract needed a woman with a barrow at night and not the porters' drays."

"*No*, Vail. I didn't ask." Saskia's voice came up for the first time. "Because if I'd asked, he'd have found someone who didn't. I've a daughter. She's six. Her feet grow. Have you ever had to look at a child's feet and do sums? No. You've a brother who doesn't grow at all. You're lucky."

The word stayed in the hot air between them.

"The boxes were warm," Hester said. "You'd have felt it through the wood. Quickened grain is warm. And you see colour at the edges of things, Orme; you'd have seen it bleeding round the corners like a lamp through a sheet." "I tasted that grain at the Customs House. It came in on the *Constant Wren* labelled Shelf for curing, which was a lie. It was quickened Hush, which is a felony to sell, and it tastes exactly like the brine beading on my brother's casing. You've seen that casing. You stood by it on Lantern Night. And somebody walked that grain down the Causeway in a barrow for two sols a run."

"The *Wren*." Saskia had gone pale under the red light. "That was high summer. Some of them went east. I didn't know—"

"They went to Quell and came back with a new label."

"I didn't *know*—"

"You didn't ask."

"And you did?" Saskia was on her feet again. "You *did*? Look at your own shoulder, Vail. What's that?"

Hester did not look. A Choir badge: the little iron wave on a grey ribbon, pinned under the black cord of Sorle's Bench.

"Three sols a week," Saskia said. "Out of the Warden's office. The *same office*. Paid over by the same bland little man with ink on his cuffs, I'll lay money. Where do you think the Choir stipends come from? The Conclave wouldn't pay for a candle-end if it could keep it. You take his money every Friday, Vail, to pay your brother's fee, and you go down into the Fifth and do whatever it is they do there, and you've never once asked where the silver comes from. Have you? Have you *asked*?"

Hester said nothing.

"No," said Saskia. "Because you needed it. Because it was for Tib." She laughed, short and ugly. "You took that gown on a dead man's privilege. You took a Choir place from a Warden you'd known a fortnight. You'd have taken it from the Chancellor's own hand if it paid the Cold House. You're Lowside, so you think you're clean. Lowside isn't clean, Vail. It's just poor. Ask anyone in Quell."

"I didn't carry felony grain to the harbour in a barrow."

"No. You just let other people's dead pay your rent."

That one went in, under the ribs. And because it went in, Hester did what she had done all her life when something went in. She hit back with the nearest heavy thing.

"Jory would be proud," she said.

She saw it land. Saskia's face went still, not with the grain-stillness but with the other kind, the kind that comes before a person decides whether to strike you. She did not strike. She looked at Hester for three ticks of the lehr. Then she sat down on her crate and opened the ledger of tray-times on her knee.

"Get out of my kiln," she said, quietly. "Please."

Hester went.

* * *

She walked. It was what she did. She went up the Stair to the Crown and out onto the dome, and round the Lantern's foot in the cold with her hands in her armpits. It was a hard clear night. The White lay under the stars like a sheet laid out for a body, and far to the west the lamps of Ambry were a smudge of yellow at the edge of the world.

She knew she was right about the grain. Saskia had carried felony salt for money, and some of it tasted like Tib's beads. But Saskia was right too. The silver in Hester's own purse came up the same Stair from the same office, and she had never asked. Orsolya had sat her down in that warm office in Russet and given her tea and said *I need holds like yours*, and Hester had been so grateful, so Lowside-grateful, to be wanted by someone who talked like her mother's sister, that she had not looked at the money at all. She had only counted it. She was looking at it now, and at the woman who paid it.

And she had said the thing about Jory, who had gone into a sink on the eastern White with a full crew. Saskia had told that story on Lantern Night in the Glassmarket, quickly, with Pip on her hip, as if it were the price of something.

Hester stood at the dome's edge and looked toward the Well-Cap on its chains, never opened. She thought of her father drawing the fire from under a spitting pan a shovelful at a time. *Lid's for when you're in a hurry.*

She went back in.

* * *

Teo was baking.

Nobody was supposed to bake in the Rookery. Fires were forbidden on the dome, and the Proctors checked. Teo had solved this in his first week with a spirit stove smuggled up the Stair in his laundry, which he lit with the window cracked and a towel along the foot of the door, and which baked one small saltcake, badly, under a covered tin. A cracked kept-light jar on the sill did for a lamp. R-41 consequently smelled most nights of burnt sugar and hot salt. Teo sat cross-legged on his bed in his grey Tider shawl, watching the tin as other men watch a sick child.

"You've been fighting," he said, without looking round.

"How do you know?"

"You've got your customs face on. And Saskia Orme came past an hour ago and didn't sneer at our door, which she always does, so either she's dead or she's been in a fight and lost, and she doesn't lose." He lifted the lid a crack and let it down again. "Ten minutes. Sit."

She sat and told him, badly, in the wrong order: the boots and the bursary and the barrow and the *Wren*, then what Saskia had said, and at the end what she had said herself. Teo listened without interrupting.

"Well," he said. "That was a horrible thing to say."

"I know."

"No, I mean truly horrible. It was well made. You've a gift for it." He lifted the lid again. "And she's right about the stipend. And you're right about the grain. And she's right that you're Lowside and think you're clean, and you're right that she should have asked. I don't think anyone in this conversation was wrong about anything, Hester, which is generally how the worst conversations go." He let the lid down. "In the Order we'd have said you were both in a state of grace and both damned, and sent you to scrub the refectory together. It works better than you'd think."

"I'm not going to scrub anything with her."

"No," said Teo. "But you might eat something."

There was a knock at the door. Not a Proctor's knock, which was three and loud. One knock, hard, as if whoever made it had thought about it for some time and decided it would be only one.

Saskia was standing in the passage with a bottle of Quell clear in one hand and her new boots in the other. She held up the bottle.

"I've come about your manners," she said.

"I've no manners."

"No. I noticed." Saskia came in without being asked, sat on the end of Teo's bed, and put the boots on the floor and the bottle on the window ledge. "That's my cousin's clear. It's illegal on this side of the White. You can seize it if you like. It's for drinking."

Teo produced three tin cups from somewhere in the shawl.

Quell clear tasted like having your mouth set on fire by a person who loved you. Nobody apologised. Hester did not know how she would have done it if she had tried, and Saskia, she suspected, knew even less. They sat on the two narrow beds with the White outside the salt-glass and the burnt-sugar smell of Teo's tin. After the second cup Saskia said, to the window, "Jory would have asked."

"I shouldn't have said it."

"No. But he would have. He asked about everything. He asked the insurers why their clause was in Ambry black and the rest of the contract in Quell blue. They didn't pay out anyway, but he asked." She turned the cup in her fingers. "I'm not Jory. I'm the one who does sums."

"So am I," Hester said.

"I know. That's why I hate you." Saskia drank. "Some days."

The saltcake came out black on the bottom and raw in the middle and sweet as a sin, and they ate it with their fingers off the lid. Teo said a Tider grace over it, composed, he said, for the breaking of a fast after the death of an abbot, and suitable for most occasions involving sugar and recrimination.

* * *

"Tell me about the lift," Hester said, later.

The tenth bell, Saskia said. The Third Fathom landing, where the box-lift from the Fifth came up behind an iron grille that was locked at night. Dunmore in the cage with the boxes stacked round his feet like a man in a pantry. Always alone. He never let her go down.

"How did he open the grille?"

"Keys. He's got a ring. He keeps it in his desk in the Warden's antechamber, the left-hand drawer. He took it out in front of me the first time, showing me the route." Saskia frowned, remembering. "A big ring with a brass tag: *W.L.F. Duplicates.* Twenty keys, near enough. Grilles, stores, the Choir lockers. And one big ugly old one, black iron, longer than my hand, with a bird on the bow. A little bird sitting on a line." She drew it in the air. "I asked what that was for, as a joke. He said it was for the Hush, and nobody went there, and I should mind my barrow."

Hester and Teo looked at each other.

"The Hush key," said Teo. "On a duplicate ring. In a clerk's desk."

"Bureaucracy," said Saskia. "It's the same in Quell. The keys to the bishop's crypt are in the sacristan's tea-caddy."

"Did he ever speak about the Warden?" Hester said. "Did she ever come to the lift?"

"Never. I never saw her once." Saskia hesitated. "He said once, when I dropped a box on the landing, *Careful. The Warden says the Choir eats on this.* I thought it was a manner of speaking."

*The Warden.* Hester thought of a coil of grain turning left round a candle-flame, and of brass in the Fifth that was full before the Choir's pour reached it, and she kept her face exactly where it was.

"He'd use her name," she said. "He's her clerk. Mallory used the Exchequer's." It came out easily. She had said true things in that flat voice for twenty-one years; she had not known it would serve as well for the other kind. "It's Dunmore. He signs for the office. He has the seals and the keys and the lift. Dunmore's the one we watch."

Teo said nothing. He was looking at her with the expression he had sometimes in Fane's lection classes, when he had heard something under a tablet that nobody else in the room could hear and was deciding whether to say so.

"There's one more thing," Hester said, because it had to be asked. "Aske's note. It ends *Ask O.* Did Aske ever speak to you? About the boxes? About anything?"

Saskia stared at her. Then, for the first time that night, she laughed properly.

"*Orme?* You thought O was *Orme*?" She shook her head. "Vail. Corvin Aske didn't know I existed. I said good morning to him once on the Stair and he walked into the wall. He was reading a sample." She laughed again, less certainly. "I'm flattered. Nobody's suspected me of anything interesting since I was nineteen. So who's O.?"

"Somebody Aske meant to ask," said Hester. "Somebody who knows the Fifth."

"That's half the Choir."

"Then it's half the Choir," Hester said, and reached for the bottle, so that she had something to do with her hands.

* * *

So Hester told her the rest. Saskia had stood in the Cold House on Lantern Night and seen the beads. She had not seen what came after.

Hester told her about the casing in Cell 3-17 and the sheet in the blotter; Lucan's torn page, *per W.L.F.*; the Cistern, and the brass rods with Lattice marks sleeved in brine so still it made her eyes ache; the rod that had gone into the Proctory and not come out; Lucan's contract, signed by *E. Dunmore, clerk*, and what the letters stood for; and her brother's casing thin enough at the foot to see a candle through.

She did not tell her what Tib had said on the night they kept him, or what she had seen in the grain round a candle-flame. There were limits.

Saskia did not interrupt, though she made faces, and once she said, "*Lattice* marks? Then they were made here," and Hester said yes. When it was finished she sat looking into her empty cup.

"Who else knows?"

"Teo. Sorle. Lucan Ferrers."

"Ferrers went down a hole in Lowside with you?" Saskia looked as if she had bitten a lemon. "With his ribs? Against his own father's firm?"

"He's—" Hester stopped. She did not know how to describe Lucan Ferrers to someone who had last seen him standing a careful yard apart from them on the sea-wall. "He's not what he looks like."

"Nobody is. That's the whole trouble with people." Saskia set the cup on the ledge. "What do you want from me?"

"I don't know yet."

"Well, decide. I've a lehr to walk at the first bell." But she did not get up. She sat in her stockinged feet with her good boots on the floor beside her and looked out at the dark White. "I don't do causes, Vail. I told you so on Lantern Night. I do rent."

"I know."

"So understand me. I'm not doing this because I'm sorry." She said it to the window, evenly. "I am sorry. I'm sorry I carried that grain, and sorrier I didn't ask, and I'll be sorry for longer than you'll be angry, I expect. But that's not why. There are three hundred people kept in that ward, and some of them have daughters. And if somebody in the Warden's office is bleeding them to pay my two sols a run, then my daughter's school was bought with them. I'll not have that. I'll not have her feet in their shoes." She turned from the window. "So. At cost. Whatever this is, I'll do it at cost."

"At cost," Hester said.

They did not shake hands. Teo poured the last of the Quell clear into the three tin cups, and they drank it without a toast, because nobody could think of one.

* * *

Lucan came the next night, because Teo sent for him.

He stood in the door of R-41 in his Upper Ward wool with the ochre cord of Vey's Bench on his shoulder and took in the cell: the two beds, the burnt tin, Saskia Orme cross-legged on Hester's blanket with a ledger, Hester at the window. His face went to the polished blank that meant he was thinking fast.

"Orme," he said.

"Ferrers."

"I was told there was a meeting."

"There's cake," said Teo. "Of a kind."

Lucan looked at the cake, and at Saskia, and at Hester, and she saw him understand that Saskia knew everything and that it had been Hester's choice. He did not argue it. He folded himself onto the floor by the door with his back to the wall, ribs still careful, and accepted a piece of black-bottomed saltcake from Teo with the gravity of a man accepting a treaty.

"Well," he said. "Here we are."

Hester stood at the window with her back to them and listened to the three of them start to talk, cautiously, then less so. Saskia asked Lucan about the contract seal; Lucan asked Saskia about the docket. Teo read Aske's sheet out again in his careful brine-script voice: *Cold House, fourteen weeping. A draw? Still runs to quick. Cistern?? Old lines. Ask O.* Then Lucan turned and asked what she made of the clerk, and Hester gave them Dunmore: the ring, the drawer, the lift, the docket hand. She said his name three times before the cake was finished. Each time, under it, like the low note under the Choir's work-song, she heard the other.

Her ledger lay open on the sill. Under the heading she had written in Russet, *Declaration of Interest*, she had crossed out *Orme* that afternoon with a single neat line. Below it she had written *E. Dunmore*, plainly, where anyone in the room could read it. The other name she had not written anywhere.

She looked at the page in the glow from Teo's cracked jar. Then she closed the ledger and sat down on the floor with the others, and took the last piece of cake before Lucan could, because he was nearer and slower and had cracked ribs, and somebody had to teach him.
