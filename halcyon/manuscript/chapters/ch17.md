# Chapter 17

## The Silent Supper

> On the ninth of Cinder, at the Lantern Hour, the whole Halcyon shall be still for one minute, and the Settled shall be named.
>
> — *Customs of the Collegium, as recorded by the Warden of the Stacks, 412 Y.E.*

They gave out the chalk at the sixth bell.

It came up from the Bursary in wooden trays, one stick for every soul in the Halcyon who could hold one: Readers and Fellows and aspirants, porters and cooks, the delvers who worked the Halcyon's own pits under the Shelf, and the Proctors, who took theirs without expression and went to their places like men on parade. Hester's stick was new and square and left a powder on her fingers that tasted of nothing at all. Varrow chalk. Dead stone. Teo said it was the one place in the Halcyon where nobody wanted anything kept.

The Stair ran from the Crown to the Fifth Fathom in a single great spiral cut into the living salt, nine hundred years of feet worn into its treads, and its outer wall was written on the whole way down. Names, in columns, each with a year. Salt drew damp out of the air and damp drew the chalk out of the salt, so that by Rime the oldest names near the Crown were ghosts, legible only by a lantern held flat to the wall. On the ninth of Cinder they were all written again.

Every aspirant was given a stretch of wall and the Bursary's list for it, and told to write each name over its ghost exactly. Hester's stretch was on the Second Fathom, between a lattice-arch and the door of a disused stairwell, and it ran from 641 to 655 Y.E. *Odo Marchant, Choir, 641. Wenna Hale, Choir, 641. Brother Cole Ansty, Fathoming, 642.* She went down the column on her knees with her lantern on the step, and found she was writing in her best customs hand, the hand she used for seizure notes, as if somebody were going to check.

*Hesk Rowan, Choir, 649. Tobit Marr, Choir, 649.* There were a great many Choir. In some years the whole column was Choir.

Below her, round the curve, Teo was saying names under his breath as he wrote them, half to himself, as he said grace. Above her someone was crying quietly. She did not look up. That was the custom too.

When she had finished she sat back on her heels and looked at the column of fresh white letters on the pink salt. Then she went on down, because the Choir was to gather on the Fifth Fathom landing at the seventh bell, and she was Choir.

* * *

The Warden of the Lower Fathoms wrote her own names.

Hester came round the last turn of the Stair with Ama Tessaly and two others, and there at the foot, in the cold blue light of the Fathom lamps, was Orsolya Thane, kneeling on the bottom tread with a stick of chalk in her left hand.

These were the newest names. They had their own panel at the bottom of the Stair, a smooth stretch of salt the width of a door where the Fathom's own dead went. There were eleven, from the last five years. The Bursary's list lay folded on the step by Orsolya's knee, unopened. She did not need it. She wrote each name in big plain capitals, slowly, pressing hard, so that the chalk squeaked. Her hands were blue at the knuckles with cold, as they always were.

*IDRIS FELL, CHOIR, 898.*
*MARRA COLE, CHOIR, 899.*
*JONAH SAINT, CHOIR, 899.*

The Choir stood on the stairs above her and waited. Orsolya wrote all eleven. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at them, and put the chalk in her pocket, and stood up stiffly, one hand on the wall.

"Right, my chicks," she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice. "Supper. Up you go. Mind the Stair, it's wet at the turn."

"She does that every year," Ama said softly. "Won't let anyone else. Says it's her hand put them there."

Hester looked at the eleven names in their white capitals as Orsolya went up the Stair ahead of them.

The capitals leaned, every one. A small steady lean to the left, as if drawn by a wrist that turned against itself.

She looked away.

* * *

There were six hundred people at the Long Table, and more standing.

Hester had eaten there every day for two months and had stopped seeing it. Tonight she saw it: three hundred feet of table down the centre of the great hall on the First Fathom, carved from the living salt nine centuries ago and worn by elbows until it shone. There were no dishes yet, only a tallow candle at every place. No grain lights. The Lantern Well, which on every other night of the year poured kept noon onto the table at the Lantern Hour, was dark. On the ninth of Cinder they kept no light. They burned it.

The Chancellor was at the head of the table.

Hester had seen her twice, at a distance. Perpetua Holm was seventy-eight and had been Chancellor for thirty-one years, and she was Settled further than anyone Hester had seen still upright. She sat in a high-backed chair of black Hask wood with a rug over her knees and her hands on its arms, white as the salt, and in the twenty minutes Hester watched her she did not visibly move them. Her eyes moved. Slowly, face by face, down the length of the table, as a lighthouse travels a coast. Beside her Vey sat upright and elegant in black, saying something now and then in her ear.

The Choir sat together at the foot, nearest the Stair-head. Hester was between Ama and a broad silent Delver-born man called Pask. Three places down, Orsolya sat with her hands round a cup of halcyon tea, her face tired and calm. Further up, among the first-years, Hester could see Teo's grey Tider shawl and Saskia's dark head beside it, and, among the Upper Ward aspirants at Vey's end, the back of Lucan's.

"First time?" said Ama.

"Yes."

"Nothing to fear, lamb. It's only a minute." Ama was sixty-six, small and round-shouldered, with the sweet slow voice of the long Choir. She had wrapped Hester in a blanket after her first stay in Russet and had been wrapping her in things ever since: scarves, advice, slabs of Hask honey-cake from a tin in her Choir locker. Her lips were blue, and when she laughed, which was often, it took her a long time to stop. "You hold if you can. Most of the Readers do. But with all of us together, lamb—" She made a bowl of her hands. "You'll feel it. It's like the Choir, only the whole house."

The Chancellor's bell rang.

It was a small handbell of old brass at Holm's elbow, and Vey lifted it, because her hand could not. The hall went silent in a wave from the head of the table down, a ripple of hush running three hundred feet along the salt like wind over grass. When it reached the Choir's end it stopped. Six hundred people sat at the Long Table and did not move.

* * *

Hester did not decide to hold. She found, after some seconds, that she was already doing it, as you find you have fallen asleep in a warm room.

Breath slow. Heart slow. Hands flat on the cold salt. It was the hold Crisp had drilled into her at the Pans, and she went into it as she had a hundred times since Russet, the coins coming down on her eyelids, the weight settling behind her face. Only this time she did not go in alone.

She felt the table first. It was one stone, the dome's own living grain, and six hundred hands lay on it. A good half of them belonged to people with the sense, and most of those were holding, or nearly. She felt them as she felt grain, through her palms, as weight. Here a Reader holding deep and steady, like a lead sinker in a pond. There a nervous first-year holding in little jerks. Three places down, someone holding so cold and so deep it was like a well: Orsolya. Beside her, Ama, soft and sure. And all of it running into the one stone, joining, a slow vast stillness spreading through the salt from six hundred places at once.

The candles stood up straight.

Before the Minute the flames had been leaning and fluttering in the draughts from the Lantern Well. Now, one by one down the whole length of the table, they stood up. They did not go out. They did not flicker. Six hundred small yellow spear-points burned in a line in the dark, so steady they looked painted, and the smoke rose from them in six hundred straight grey threads to the roof.

She could hear her own heart. Between one beat and the next there was time to count to four.

And under the stillness, in the stone, the sound.

It was the Roar, the thing she had first heard in the *Wren* grain and heard every Choir shift since: the long low rushing, the rise coming again before the fall had finished. She had never heard it in the Long Table. The dome's own grain was Shelf-depth, too shallow to hold more than a whisper. But with six hundred of them holding together, the whisper was there under her hands, a breath going in and out of the salt beneath the whole Halcyon. She thought, from a long way off, *it sounds like something asleep*.

Ama's hand found hers on the table and held it, cold and dry.

*Idris Fell*, Hester thought. *Marra Cole. Jonah Saint.* She had never met them. The names came up through the stillness like bubbles through brine. *Odo Marchant. Wenna Hale.* And somewhere behind her eyes, where the coins were, Tib's hand curled on his chest, holding its pencil.

Then the bell rang.

Not the Chancellor's handbell. The other one.

* * *

It was iron, and it came from below.

Every Fathom had a bell in its Stair-head with a rope that ran down through the floors to the Fathom beneath. Hester had learned the rings her first week in the Choir. One and one was a summons. Two and two was fire. Three and three, iron on iron, hard and fast, meant a pocket had broken.

*Clang-clang-clang.* A breath. *Clang-clang-clang.*

The Minute broke like a plate.

Hester came out of the hold too fast and felt the world tilt and right itself. The candles were leaning again, all six hundred, thrown sideways at once by six hundred people moving. Near the head of the table a chair went over backward with a crack like a shot. A first-year girl was saying "What is it, what is it?" to nobody. And at the Choir's end, before Hester had her breath, Orsolya Thane was on her feet.

"Choir," she said.

She did not shout. Her voice went down the table like a hand laid flat on it, and the Choir rose: sixty people in grey wool rising in one movement out of six hundred, like a flight of salt-larks off a pan. Ama's hand pulled out of Hester's, and Ama was up, and Pask, and Hester with them before she had thought about it at all.

"Pask, the stay-lines from the Fourth store, all of them. Ama, the trunk valves. Edwin—" Dunmore was at Orsolya's elbow from nowhere, ink on his cuffs. "Edwin, the Proctors. Clear the Fourth east gallery. Everyone out who isn't Choir." Her eyes found the rest of them. "With me. Run if you can. Don't if you can't. Down."

In the one glance Hester gave back before the Stair took her, the Chancellor sat in her black chair with her white hands on its arms and did not move. Her eyes followed them to the Stair-head, the lighthouse look, travelling the coast. Then the curve of the wall cut it off.

* * *

They went down the Stair in a grey river, past the fresh white names.

The Fourth Fathom held the Quiet Rooms and the Choir hall and, to the east, the long gallery where the Brinestacks kept their overflow: records too bulky or too damaged for the shelves above, much of it salvage from the old fire, in iron cases floor to ceiling. Hester had walked through it twice on Choir business. It had always been cold, dry and silent.

It was not silent now. The sound came up the passage to meet them as they ran, a sound the Halcyon did not make: a rushing, and a slapping, and under it a long hollow boom that came and went, so that the floor gave a small shiver each time it came.

"Tide take it," said Pask, beside her, quietly. He was a Delver's son. He had heard the White crack under him before.

Then they were through the door, and Hester saw water.

It was running across the floor of the east gallery, a sheet of it an inch deep, black in the lamplight, carrying scraps of paper on it like leaves on a stream. Fifty yards down, the gallery's end wall was wet from floor to ceiling, shining like a cliff in rain. Out of a crack at about the height of a man's shoulder, water was coming in a thick clear rope. Every few breaths the rope swelled and spat, and that was the boom: a deep thump from inside the wall, and a gush.

The smell hit her a second after the sight. It was cold, and green, and strong, a smell she had met only once, faintly, in the warm wet salt of her first stay. It was like the Weeps at low water in summer, but cleaner, and brighter, and enormously more. It was the smell of a great many things growing in water.

"Sweet sea," someone whispered.

The water came over Hester's shoes, bitterly cold, the cold of deep winter. She bent and put two fingers in it and put them to her mouth before she had decided to.

Salt, but not grain, not brine that anyone had mixed. It tasted as the *Wren* grain had tasted under her tongue at the Customs House: bright, metallic, startled. And there was something living in it, green and faintly of iodine. It tasted of the cry she had heard in that cargo, if a cry could have a taste. It tasted *quick*.

"Vail!" Orsolya's voice. "Line, chick. *Now.*"

* * *

The stay went up in four minutes. Hester did not see most of it, because she was part of it.

Pask and the others came at a run with the stay-lines, long hoses of oiled leather sleeved in brass, coiled on their shoulders like rope. They ran them from the trunk valves at the gallery door, where Ama knelt with a spanner opening the cocks, out across the flooded floor to the wet wall. The Choir followed them in and formed. Hester had done it a dozen times in drills on the dry Choir floor, never in water with the boom in the walls. It made no difference. The Choir knew its own shape: sixty in a long curved line facing the wall, each with a hand on the shoulder of the one before, each line-leader with a stay-line clamped to his belt and its brass mouth pressed to the weeping salt.

Live brine came rushing down the leather from the trunk. Every chorister put a pinch of Middling under the tongue, as they always did, to tune, and went in.

And Orsolya sang.

It was a note, low and long, held for a full breath, then let go, then held again, and every voice in the line took it and held it with her. The note was a rope you could all hold at once. Your breath went out on it as slowly as hers did, and your heart came down to meet your breath, and sixty holds became one hold, poured down the stay-lines into the wet salt.

*Hold the line,* Orsolya sang. *Hold the line.*

*So the others can sleep*, the Choir answered.

The stillness went out of Hester through her hands and the soles of her feet in the cold water, down through Pask's shoulder under her palm and into the brass and the wall. She felt the wall take it. A quickening was a hungry thing; that was the first thing they taught you in the Choir. The boom came again, weaker, and the rope of water thinned and shuddered.

She was deep now. The coins had become a weight on her whole face, and the cold of the water had stopped being cold and become simply a fact, like the dark. She was listening, as she had learned to, to the line itself: the long brass trunk running from the valve at the door away into the Halcyon's walls, to the stores, to the Fifth, to wherever stays drew from.

Something was coming up it, from behind her. From the east.

She had felt this before. In Fallow, on the night shift after the candle-flame, with her palms on the brass in the Fifth: something cold and old already in the line before the Choir's pour reached it. She had told herself then that you could not taste through your hands, and she had gone up the Stair thinking of nothing.

It came now in a flood. Not the Choir's stillness, which she knew after six weeks as she knew her own breath: sixty human holds, warm at the edges, a little ragged, smelling of wool and fear. This was colder than any hold she had ever felt, and perfectly smooth, the stillness of something that had not moved in years and years. It came up the trunk and into the wall like a tide of cold oil. Under its weight the quickening's boom faltered, and went *tick*, and *tick*.

The stay-line at Pask's belt bucked with the load. Where its brass mouth met the wall it spat, a short hard jet, and brine struck Hester across the cheek and the mouth.

The Middling under her tongue was long gone. What was on her lips was the line itself.

She knew it at once, as she knew the reference jars on her old bench. She had tasted it in Fallow, on her knees in the founders' brick under Coldwater Street with Lucan's lantern shaking, a fingertip of brine from the sleeve round a brass rod. *This is them.* She had smelled it for eleven years in the cold sweet air of the charity ward, standing by casing two hundred and eleven. It was the stillness of the Cold House, the stillest thing in the world, because old keepings were.

It was Tib.

It was coming up the stay-line past her and pouring into the wall, and the wall was drinking it, and the stay was holding.

*Hold the line,* Orsolya sang.

Hester held. She did not know what else to do. Her hand was on Pask's shoulder and her brother was on her lips and going past her through the brass a yard away, as she had once watched the excisemen carry forty crates past her desk. She held, and the Choir held, and the rope of water thinned to a trickle and then to a weep and then to nothing. The black sheet on the floor slowed and began, at its edges, to frost into salt.

* * *

The hold broke in a long ragged ending, the Choir coming out of it one by one like people waking in a cold room. Somebody coughed. Somebody was sick into the water. The lag took Hester for a moment, the world going grey and soundless, and then let her go. She was standing in water iced to the ankle with her hand still on Pask's shoulder, and her teeth were chattering so hard she could hear them.

"Down," Pask was saying. "Down, down. Ama, love. Ama."

Ama was sitting in the water.

She was sitting where she had stood, at Hester's left, in the line. She had folded down so gently that Hester had not felt her go: knees bent, back straight, hands open on her lap. The water had frozen to salt around her skirt and her shoes. Her eyes were half open. Hester bent beside her in the ice, and heard, faintly, the note.

She was still singing. Not aloud; the breath of it. The long low Choir note was going out of her on a breath that did not end, the hold still in her, held past the place where it could be let go.

"Ama," Hester said.

The note went on. Then it was quieter. Then it was a breath. Then it was not anything.

Hester put her fingers to the old woman's throat, as her mother had taught her when she was ten, and felt for the beat. It was there, a long way off. One. She counted to twenty. She counted to forty. It did not come again.

"She held too long," Pask said above her, roughly. "She always held too long. Every stay, a breath past the rest of us, the old fool. She said someone ought to be last out." He knelt in the water on Ama's other side. "Oh, you old fool."

Feet in the slush. A lantern. Hester knelt with two fingers on Ama Tessaly's throat and did not take them away, because taking them away would make it a fact.

Then Orsolya was there.

She came through the Choir, which parted for her, and looked down at Ama sitting in the frozen water with her hands open, and her face did not change at all. She went down on her knees in the ice and took Ama's face between her two cold hands, carefully, as you might take a bowl of something hot.

"Ama," she said. "Oh, my girl. Oh, my girl."

And then she wept.

Hester had seen people cry in the Cold House every first of the month for eleven years. This was not like that. Orsolya knelt in the frozen sea with her friend's face in her hands and wept without a sound, without bending. The tears whitened as they ran. By the time they reached her jaw they were salt, little ridged crusts on her skin, the long Settle showing in her as it showed in all of them. She did not wipe them. Somewhere far behind the wall there was a sound like the sea turning over in its sleep.

"Twelve," Orsolya said at last. To nobody. To Ama. "That's twelve."

She looked up. Her eyes found Hester's across Ama's bowed head, a foot away, wet and grey and tired beyond anything Hester had words for. Hester saw no guilt in them, no fear, no calculation. Only grief, and under it something that might have been love, for the whole cold shivering line of them standing in the dark.

"Help me get her up, chick," Orsolya said. "She'll not want to be sat in the wet."

* * *

They carried her up on a Choir blanket, Pask and Orsolya and Hester and three others, past all the fresh white names. Nobody spoke. At the Fifth Fathom landing they laid her at the foot of the panel where Orsolya had written the eleven, and Orsolya stood looking at it.

Hester did not stay. She went back up the Stair alone, round and round, past the Fourth and the Third, to her own stretch on the Second Fathom: Odo Marchant and Wenna Hale and Brother Cole Ansty in her best seizure hand. Below the last of them was a smooth blank stretch of pink salt above the step. Nobody would mind, she thought. Nobody would look.

She took the chalk out of her pocket and wrote, in capitals, pressing hard so that it squeaked:

*AMA TESSALY, CHOIR, 903.*

Then she sat on the step under it with chalk on her fingers and the Cold House still on her lips. She did not let herself think the thing that was waiting to be thought. She held it off as you hold a door against a wind, with her whole weight, not looking at it. She thought instead of the eleven names at the foot of the Stair, leaning a little to the left. She thought of Orsolya's voice in the dark, *so the others can sleep*. She thought of Orsolya in Russet, in the warm office with the Hask honey, saying tiredly, as if it were a small mercy: *The Stays have been kind this season. Kinder than they've any right to be.*

She licked her lips, and tasted it again, and sat there until the first bell.
