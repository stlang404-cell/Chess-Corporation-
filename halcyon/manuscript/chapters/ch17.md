# Chapter 17

## The Silent Supper

> On the ninth of Cinder, at the Lantern Hour, the whole Halcyon shall be still for one minute, and the Settled shall be named.
>
> — *Customs of the Collegium, as recorded by the Warden of the Stacks, 412 Y.E.*

They gave out the chalk at the sixth bell.

It came up from the Bursary in wooden trays, one stick for every soul in the Halcyon who could hold one. There were sticks for Readers and Fellows and aspirants, for porters and cooks and the delvers who worked the Halcyon's own pits under the Shelf, and for the Proctors, who took theirs without expression and went to their places like men on parade. Hester's was new and square, white, and left a powder on her fingers that tasted faintly of nothing at all. It was Varrow chalk. Dead stone. Nobody used grain for the names. Teo said it was the one place in the Halcyon where nobody wanted anything kept.

The Stair ran from the Crown to the Fifth Fathom in a single great spiral cut into the living salt, nine hundred years of feet worn into its treads. Its outer wall, the whole way down, was written on. Names, in columns, in chalk, each with a year beside it. Most of the year they faded. Salt drew damp out of the air; damp drew the chalk out of the salt; by Rime the oldest names near the Crown were ghosts, and you could read them only by the light of a lantern held flat to the wall. On the ninth of Cinder they were all written again.

Every aspirant was given a stretch of wall and a copy of the list for it, in the Bursary's careful hand, and told to write each name over its ghost exactly. Hester's stretch was on the Second Fathom, between a lattice-arch and the door to a disused stairwell, and it ran from 641 to 655 Y.E. She had never heard of any of them. *Odo Marchant, Choir, 641. Wenna Hale, Choir, 641. Brother Cole Ansty, Fathoming, 642.* She went down the column on her knees with her lantern on the step beside her, writing over the faded letters one by one. She found that she was doing it with her best customs hand, the hand she used for seizure notes, as if somebody were going to check.

*Hesk Rowan, Choir, 649. Tobit Marr, Choir, 649.* There were a great many Choir. In some years the whole column was Choir.

Below her on the Stair, round the curve, she could hear Teo saying names under his breath as he wrote them, the way he said grace, half to himself. Above her someone was crying quietly. She did not look up. That was the custom too. You did not look at anybody on the Stair on the ninth of Cinder, any more than you looked at a man talking to his daughter in the Pans.

When she had finished her stretch she sat back on her heels and looked at it, the column of fresh white letters on the pink salt. Then she went on down, because the Choir had been told to gather on the Fifth Fathom landing at the seventh bell, and she was Choir.

* * *

The Warden of the Lower Fathoms wrote her own names.

Hester had not known she would. She came round the last turn of the Stair above the Fifth with Ama Tessaly and two others of the Choir. There at the foot, in the cold blue light of the Fathom lamps, was Orsolya Thane, kneeling on the bottom tread with a stick of chalk in her left hand. She was writing.

These were the newest names. They had their own panel at the bottom of the Stair, a smooth stretch of salt the width of a door where the Fathom's own dead went. There were eleven names on it, from the last five years, and the Bursary's copy-list lay folded on the step beside Orsolya's knee, unopened. She did not need it. She wrote each name in big plain capitals, slowly, pressing hard, so that the chalk squeaked on the salt. Her white hair was tied back. Her hands, Hester saw, were blue at the knuckles with cold, as they always were.

*IDRIS FELL, CHOIR, 898.*
*MARRA COLE, CHOIR, 899.*
*JONAH SAINT, CHOIR, 899.*

The Choir stood on the stairs above her and waited, and nobody spoke. Orsolya wrote all eleven. When she had done she sat back on her heels, as Hester had, and looked at them for a moment, and then put the chalk in her pocket and stood up stiffly, one hand on the wall.

"Right, my chicks," she said. Her voice was perfectly ordinary. "Supper. Up you go. Mind the Stair, it's wet at the turn."

Ama Tessaly, beside Hester, said softly, "She does that every year. Won't let anyone else. Says it's her hand put them there."

Hester looked at the eleven names in their white capitals, and at Orsolya going up the Stair ahead of them with her hand on the wall.

The capitals leaned, every one of them. A small steady lean to the left, as if the chalk had been drawn by a wrist that turned against itself.

She looked away.

* * *

There were six hundred people at the Long Table, and more standing.

Hester had eaten at the Long Table every day for two months. She had stopped seeing it, as people stop seeing anything they eat at twice a day. Tonight she saw it. The table ran three hundred feet down the centre of the great hall on the First Fathom, carved from the living salt of the dome nine centuries ago, worn so smooth by elbows that it shone. Down its whole length tonight there were no dishes yet: only candles, a candle at every place, tallow, dead. No grain lights. The Lantern Well at the hall's centre, which on every other night of the year poured kept noon down onto the table at the Lantern Hour, was dark. On the ninth of Cinder they kept no light. They burned it.

The Chancellor was at the head of the table.

Hester had seen her twice, at a distance. Perpetua Holm was seventy-eight and had been Chancellor for thirty-one years. She was Settled further than anyone Hester had ever seen still upright. She sat in a high-backed chair of black Hask wood at the table's end with a rug over her knees. Her hands lay on the arms of the chair, white as the salt, and in the twenty minutes Hester watched her before the Minute began she did not visibly move them. Her eyes moved. Slowly. They travelled down the length of the table, face by face, as a lighthouse travels a coast. Beside her the Deputy Chancellor, Vey, sat upright and elegant in black, saying something now and then in her ear. Whether she heard it, Hester could not tell.

The Choir sat together near the foot, at the end nearest the Stair-head, because it always did. Hester was between Ama and a broad silent Delver-born man called Pask. Across from her, three places down, Orsolya sat with her hands round a cup of halcyon tea, her face tired and calm. Further up the table, among the first-years, Hester could see Teo's grey Tider shawl, and Saskia's dark head next to it, and, among the Upper Ward aspirants at Vey's end, the back of Lucan's.

"First time?" said Ama.

"Yes."

"Nothing to fear, lamb. It's only a minute." Ama was sixty-six, small and round-shouldered, with the sweet slow voice of the long Choir. She had wrapped Hester in a blanket after her first stay in Russet and had been wrapping her in things ever since: scarves, advice, slabs of Hask honey-cake from a tin she kept in her Choir locker. Her hair was white and her lips were blue and when she laughed, which was often, it took her a long time to stop. "You hold if you can. Most of the Readers do. The rest just sit quiet. But with all of us together, lamb—with all of us—" She made a small shape with her hands, a bowl. "You'll feel it. It's like the Choir, only the whole house. You'll see."

The Chancellor's bell rang.

It was not loud. It was a small handbell of old brass on the table at Holm's elbow, and it was Vey who lifted it, because her hand could not. He rang it once. The hall did not go silent at once. It went silent in a wave, from the head of the table down, a ripple of hush running three hundred feet along the salt like wind going over grass. When it reached the Choir's end it stopped. Six hundred people sat at the Long Table and did not move.

The Minute began.

* * *

Hester did not decide to hold. It was like falling asleep in a warm room: she found, after some seconds, that she was already doing it.

Breath slow. Heart slow. Hands flat on the cold salt of the table. It was the hold Crisp had drilled into her at the Pans, the posture and not the feeling, and she went into it as she had gone in a hundred times since Russet, the coins coming down on her eyelids, the weight settling behind her face. Only this time she did not go in alone.

She felt the table first.

It was one stone. Three hundred feet of carved salt, one block, unbroken, the dome's own living grain, and six hundred hands lay on it. A good half of them belonged to people with the sense, and most of those were holding, or nearly. She could feel them as she felt grain, through her palms. It was not a taste exactly. It was weight. Here a Reader holding deep and steady, like a lead sinker in a pond. There a nervous first-year holding badly in little jerks. There, three places down, someone holding so cold and so deep it was like a well. That was Orsolya. Beside her Ama, soft and sure. And all of it running into the one stone of the table, joining, a slow vast stillness spreading through the salt from six hundred places at once, like frost spreading on a window from every edge toward the middle.

The candles stood up straight.

That was what she would remember afterward, more than anything. Before the Minute the flames had been leaning and fluttering in the draughts that came down the Lantern Well, as tallow flames do. Now, one by one, down the whole length of the table, they stood up. They did not go out. They did not flicker. They stood up straight and still and burned, six hundred small yellow spear-points in a line in the dark, so steady that they looked painted. The smoke rose from them in six hundred perfectly straight grey threads to the roof.

She could hear her own heart. It was very slow. Between one beat and the next there was time to count to four.

And under the stillness, in the stone, the sound.

It was faint. It was the Roar, the thing she had first heard in the *Wren* grain at the Customs House and heard every Choir shift since: the long low rushing, the rise and the fall, the rise coming again before the fall had finished. She had never heard it in the Long Table. She did not think anyone heard the Long Table. It was the dome's own grain, Shelf-depth, too shallow to hold more than a whisper. But with six hundred of them holding together, the whisper was there. She could hear it under her hands. A breath going in and out of the salt beneath the whole Halcyon. She thought, from a long way off, *it sounds like something asleep*.

Ama's hand found hers on the table and held it. It was cold and dry.

*Idris Fell*, Hester thought. *Marra Cole. Jonah Saint.* She did not know why. She had never met them. The names came up through the stillness like bubbles through brine. *Odo Marchant. Wenna Hale.* And somewhere behind her eyes, where the coins were, Tib's hand curled on his chest, holding its pencil.

She thought the Minute must be nearly over. She could not tell. Time in a deep hold was like distance on the White, where the Pull made a mile look like a step and a step like a mile.

Then the bell rang.

Not the Chancellor's handbell. The other one.

* * *

It was iron, and it came from below.

Every Fathom of the Halcyon had a bell in its Stair-head, hung on a bracket, with a rope that ran down through the floors to the Fathom beneath. Hester had been taught the rings her first week in the Choir. One and one was a summons. Two and two was fire. Three and three, iron on iron, hard and fast, was the ring nobody wanted to hear. It meant a pocket had broken.

*Clang-clang-clang.* A pause the length of a breath. *Clang-clang-clang.*

The Minute broke like a plate.

Hester came out of the hold too fast and felt the world tilt and right itself. The candles were leaning again, all six hundred of them, thrown sideways at once by six hundred people moving. Somebody near the head of the table had stood up so quickly that his chair went over backward with a crack like a shot. A first-year girl was saying "What is it, what is it?" in a high voice to nobody. And down at the Choir's end, before Hester had even got her breath, Orsolya Thane was on her feet.

"Choir," she said.

She did not shout. She did not need to. Her voice went down the table like a hand laid flat on it, and the Choir rose. Sixty people in grey wool with iron-wave badges on their shoulders, rising in one movement from six hundred, the way a flight of salt-larks rises off a pan. Ama's hand pulled out of Hester's and Ama was up, and Pask on her other side, and Hester was up with them before she had thought about it at all.

"Pask, the stay-lines from the Fourth store, all of them. Ama, the trunk valves. Edwin—" Dunmore was there, at Orsolya's elbow, from nowhere, in his grey gown with ink on his cuffs. "Edwin, the Proctors. Clear the Fourth east gallery. Everyone out who isn't Choir. Now." She turned and her eyes found the rest of them. "With me. Run if you can. Don't if you can't. Down."

Behind them, at the head of the table, the Chancellor sat in her black chair with the rug on her knees and her white hands on its arms, and did not move. Her eyes followed them to the Stair-head. Hester saw that, in the one glance back she gave before the Stair took her. The lighthouse look, travelling the coast. Then the curve of the wall cut it off.

* * *

They went down the Stair in a grey river, past the fresh white names.

The Fourth Fathom was the Quiet Rooms and the Choir hall and, to the east, the long gallery where the Brinestacks kept their overflow. It held the records too bulky or too damaged for the shelves above, much of it salvage from the old fire, stacked in iron cases floor to ceiling. Hester had walked through it twice on Choir business. It was always cold, dry and silent, and it smelled of old paper and older salt.

It was not silent now. She heard it before she reached the gallery door. It came up the passage to meet them as they ran, a sound that was not any sound the Halcyon made. It was a rushing, and a slapping, and under the slapping a long hollow boom that came and went, came and went, so that the floor under her feet gave a small shiver each time it came.

"Tide take it," said Pask, beside her, quietly. He was a Delver's son. He had heard the White crack under him before.

Then they were through the door, and Hester saw water.

It was running across the floor of the east gallery. Not seeping. Running, a sheet of it an inch deep, black in the lamplight, moving in a slow flat current from the far end of the gallery toward the door and the Stair, carrying scraps of paper on it like leaves on a stream. It came from the far wall. There, fifty yards down, the salt of the gallery's end was wet from floor to ceiling, shining like a cliff in rain. Out of a crack in it, at about the height of a man's shoulder, water was coming in a thick clear rope. It hit the floor and spread. Every few breaths the rope swelled and spat, and that was the boom: a deep hollow thump from somewhere inside the wall, and a gush.

The smell hit her a second after the sight. It was cold, and green, and strong. It was a smell she did not have a name for and had smelled only once, faintly, in the warm wet salt of her first stay. It was like the Weeps at low water in summer, but cleaner, and brighter, and enormously more. It was alive. It was the smell of a great many things growing in water.

"Sweet sea," someone whispered.

Hester stood in the doorway in the water, which came over her shoes, and found that it was cold. Bitterly cold, the cold of deep winter. She bent and put two fingers in it and put them to her mouth before she had decided to.

Salt. Of course it was salt. But not grain. Not brine that anyone had mixed. It tasted the way the *Wren* grain had tasted under the weight: bright, metallic, startled. And there was more in it than salt. She tasted something living in it, green and faintly iodine. It tasted of the cry she had heard in the Customs House, if a cry could have a taste. It tasted *quick*.

"Vail!" Orsolya's voice. "Line, chick. *Now.*"

* * *

The stay went up in four minutes. Hester did not see most of it, because she was part of it.

Pask and the others came at a run with the stay-lines: long flexible hoses of oiled leather sleeved in brass, coiled on their shoulders like ropes. They ran them from the brass trunk valves at the gallery door, where Ama was on her knees with a spanner opening the cocks, out along the flooded floor to the wet wall. The Choir followed them in, splashing, and formed. Hester had done it a dozen times in drills on the dry Choir floor. She had never done it in water, with the boom in the walls and the black current round her ankles. It made no difference. The Choir knew its own shape. Sixty of them in a long curved line across the gallery, facing the wet wall, an arm's length apart. Each had a hand on the shoulder of the one before. Each line-leader had a stay-line clamped to his belt, its brass mouth pressed to the weeping salt.

Live brine came down the lines from the trunk. Hester heard it, a rush of liquid in the leather. Every chorister put a pinch of Middling under the tongue, as they always did, to tune, and went in.

And Orsolya sang.

It was not a song exactly. The Choir's singing never was. It was a note, low and long, held for a full breath, then let go, then held again, and every voice in the line took it and held it with her. That was how they did it. The note was a rope you could all hold at once. Your breath went out on it as slowly as hers did, and your heart came down to meet your breath, and sixty breaths became one breath and sixty holds became one hold, poured down the stay-lines into the wet salt.

*Hold the line,* Orsolya sang, on the long note. *Hold the line.*

*So the others can sleep*, the Choir answered.

The stillness went out of Hester through her hands and her mouth and the soles of her feet in the cold water. It ran down the line through Pask's shoulder under her palm and into the brass and the wall. She felt it go. She felt the wall take it. A quickening was a hungry thing; that was the first thing they taught you in the Choir. You poured still into it and it drank. The boom in the wall came again, weaker, and the rope of water from the crack thinned and shuddered.

*Hold the line.*

She was deep now. The coins had become a weight on her whole face. The cold of the water had stopped being cold and become simply a fact, like the dark. And she was listening, as she had learned to listen in the Choir, to the line itself: the long brass trunk that ran from the valve at the gallery door away into the Halcyon's walls, to the stores, to the Fifth, to wherever stays drew from.

Something was coming up it.

It came from behind her. From the trunk. From the east.

It was not the Choir. She knew the Choir's stillness. She had been swimming in it for six weeks: sixty human holds, warm at the edges, a little ragged, smelling of wool and breath and fear. This was not that. It came into the line from somewhere far down the trunk, and it was cold, colder than any hold she had ever felt, and it was old. It was not ragged at all. It was perfectly smooth and perfectly deep, the stillness of something that had not moved in years and years. It came up the line past her and into the wall like a tide of cold oil, steady, enormous. Under its weight the quickening's boom faltered and slowed and went *tick*, and *tick*, and stopped.

She knew it.

She had tasted it in Fallow, kneeling in the dark in the founders' brick under Coldwater Street with Lucan's lantern shaking in his hand. She had put her finger in the sleeve of brine round a brass rod and touched it to her tongue and nearly fallen over. *This is them.* She had known it for eleven years before that, too, in the cold sweet air of the charity ward, standing beside casing two hundred and eleven. She had tasted it again on the first of Russet, on a bead at the eastern foot of a plinth.

It was the stillness of the Cold House. It was the stillest thing in the world. Old keepings were.

It was Tib.

It was coming up the stay-line past her and pouring into the wall, and the wall was drinking it, and the stay was holding.

*Hold the line,* Orsolya sang.

Hester held. She did not know what else to do. Her hands were on Pask's shoulder and her mouth was full of Middling and her brother's stillness was going past her through the brass a yard away, as she had once watched the excisemen carry forty crates past her desk. She held, and the Choir held, and the wall went quiet, and the rope of water from the crack thinned to a trickle and then to a weep and then to nothing. The black sheet on the floor slowed, and slowed, and began, faintly, at its edges, to frost into salt.

* * *

The hold broke in a long ragged ending, the Choir coming out of it one by one, like people waking in a cold room. Somebody coughed. Somebody was sick into the water. Hester felt the lag take her for a moment, the world going grey and soundless, and then let her go. She was standing in water that was ice-crusted to the ankle, with her hand still on Pask's shoulder, and her teeth were chattering so hard she could hear them.

"Down," Pask was saying. "Down, down. Ama, love. Ama."

Ama was sitting in the water.

She was sitting where she had stood, at Hester's left, in the line. She had folded down so gently that Hester had not felt her go: knees bent, back straight, her hands open on her lap. The black water had frozen to salt around her skirt and her shoes. Her eyes were half open. Her mouth was a little open too. Hester bent down beside her in the ice, and heard, very faintly, the note.

She was still singing. Not aloud. It was the breath of it. The long low Choir note was going out of her on a breath that did not end, quieter and quieter, the hold still in her, held past the place where it could be let go.

"Ama," Hester said.

The note went on. Then it was quieter. Then it was a breath. Then it was not anything.

Hester put her fingers to the old woman's throat, as her mother had taught her to when she was ten, and felt for the beat. She waited. It was there, a long way off. One. She counted. She counted to twenty. She counted to forty. It did not come again.

"She held too long," Pask said, above her. His voice was rough. "She always held too long. Every stay, she held a breath past the rest of us, the old fool. She said someone ought to be last out." He knelt in the water on Ama's other side. "Oh, you old fool."

People were coming. Feet in the slush. Somebody brought a lantern. Hester knelt in the ice with two fingers on Ama Tessaly's throat and did not take them away, because taking them away would make it a fact, and she did not want it to be a fact yet.

Then Orsolya was there.

She came through the Choir, which parted for her, and stopped. She looked down at Ama, sitting in the frozen water with her hands open on her lap, and her face did not change at all. She went down on her knees in the ice beside Hester. She took Ama's face between her two cold hands, carefully, as you might take a bowl of something hot, and looked into it.

"Ama," she said. "Oh, my girl. Oh, my girl."

And then she wept.

Hester had never seen a Reader weep. She had seen people cry in the Cold House every first of the month for eleven years; she knew how grief looked. This was not like that. Orsolya Thane knelt in the frozen sea with her friend's face in her hands and wept without a sound, without moving, without bending. The tears ran down her face and as they ran they whitened. By the time they reached her jaw they were salt, little ridged crusts on her skin like frost on a pane, the long Settle showing in her as it showed in all of them. She did not wipe them. She held Ama's face, and the salt grew on her cheeks, and the Choir stood round them in the lamplight in silence. Somewhere far behind the wall, very faintly, there was a sound like the sea turning over in its sleep.

"Twelve," Orsolya said at last. To nobody. To Ama. "That's twelve."

She looked up.

Her eyes found Hester's across Ama's bowed head, only a foot away. They were wet and grey and tired beyond anything Hester had words for. Hester saw no guilt in them, no fear and no calculation. Only grief, and under the grief something that might have been love, for all sixty of them, for the whole cold shivering line of the Choir standing in the dark.

"Help me get her up, chick," Orsolya said. "She'll not want to be sat in the wet."

* * *

They carried her up the Stair between them, Pask and Orsolya and Hester and three others, on a Choir blanket, past all the fresh white names. Nobody spoke. At the Fifth Fathom landing they laid her down at the foot of the panel where Orsolya had written the eleven. Orsolya stood looking at it.

Hester did not stay. She could not have said why. She went back up the Stair alone, slowly, round and round, past the Fourth and the Third. On the Second Fathom she stopped at her own stretch of wall, the column she had written that evening, 641 to 655, Odo Marchant and Wenna Hale and Brother Cole Ansty. It was in her best seizure hand. She knelt.

There was still chalk in her pocket. She took it out. The bottom of her column ran into a smooth blank stretch of pink salt above the step. Nobody would mind, she thought. Nobody would look.

She wrote, in capitals, pressing hard so that it squeaked:

*AMA TESSALY, CHOIR, 903.*

Then she sat on the step under it, in the cold, with the chalk-dust on her fingers and the taste of the Cold House still at the back of her tongue. She did not let herself think the thing that was waiting to be thought. She held it off as you hold a door against a wind, with her whole weight, not looking at it. She thought instead about the eleven names at the foot of the Stair, in big plain capitals leaning a little to the left. She thought about Orsolya's voice in the dark, *hold the line, so the others can sleep*. She thought about Orsolya in Russet, in the warm office with the Hask honey, saying tiredly, as if it were a small mercy: *The Stays have been kind this season. Kinder than they've any right to be.*

Somewhere far below her, the sea turned over again in its sleep. She sat on the Stair and listened to it until the first bell.
