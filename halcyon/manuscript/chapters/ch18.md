# Chapter 18

## The Fourth Sleeper

Agathe's letter came up the Causeway on the sixteenth of Cinder with the afternoon post, in a cheap grey envelope with the Cold House's name printed on the flap. Hester's name was on the front in a round careful hand that had been taught its letters by Tider sisters sixty years ago and had never seen a reason to change them.

*My dear. Bett Linnow is coming back. The casing has cracked at the foot and the brinewright says there is no more to be done and Mr. Pell is at his sister's. Her mother is here. She asks for someone who understands the salt. I do not, not as you do. If you can come, come. A.L.*

Hester read it standing in the Rookery passage. Then she put on her coat and went.

It had been raining on the Rim for two days, the short hard rain of Cinder that came over the Varrow and fell on Ambry and never reached the White. The Causeway was black and shining, and on either side of it the salt was bone-dry, as if the rain had come to a wall. She walked the two miles fast, with her collar up and her hands in her armpits, and was at the Cold House by the ninth bell.

Nobody was at the desk. The great ledger lay open on the felt with a pen across it. She went past it and through the double doors and down the six worn steps. On the third step she smelled it: not the cold sweet cellar smell of the charity ward, but something warm. Warm, and sharp, and faintly like hot iron.

* * *

Bett Linnow's plinth was the third from the end of the sixth row.

Agathe had shown it to her on Lantern Night. Before that Hester had passed it a hundred and thirty times on the first of the month without looking: a stout woman of about thirty in a brown dress, kept on her side with her knees drawn up and both hands pressed to her mouth, as if she had coughed and been surprised by it. From the aisle she had looked asleep. Most of them did.

She did not look asleep now. Agathe had hung a lantern above the plinth and the casing in its light was not clear any more. It was white. Not the grey of the dead, but a fine white crazing, like ice on a puddle when you put your heel on it, spreading from a crack at the casing's foot as long as Hester's hand. Brine was running out of the crack in a steady thread. The thread did not drip onto the floor. It ran sideways along the plinth's edge and down the eastern side and into the drain-grille beneath, as if it had somewhere to be.

The casing was warm. She could feel it from a yard away, like a stove going out. The arrears were coming.

An old woman sat on a stool beside the plinth with both her hands flat on the stone, as close to the casing as the stone allowed. She was small and upright, in a black wool coat that had been good once and a black bonnet. Agathe stood behind her with a hand on her shoulder.

"Ruth," Agathe said. "This is Hester. Tobias Vail's sister, from the fourth row. She's at the Halcyon now. She understands the salt."

The old woman looked up. Her eyes were pale and wet and enormously tired.

"Can you stop it?" she said.

* * *

Hester tried.

She knew before she began that she could not. She tried anyway, because the old woman had asked, and because it was the only thing in the world she knew how to do.

The house kept its recasting brine in a copper tank in the brinewright's closet, Middling grain dissolved in water, to be worked within the hour. Agathe drew a jugful. It was not good brine, and not fresh, and Hester was not licensed, and there was no Reader in the building to say so. She knelt by the cracked foot of the casing, poured a thin line of brine along the crack, and went into the hold.

To recast you had to find the hand that had made the casing and lay your own stillness along it, as a mason lays a new course of brick on an old wall. Bett Linnow's casing had been made twenty years ago by some Cold House brinewright long dead. Its hand was coarse and quick, a charity hand. Hester found it with her eyes shut and her palms flat on the crazed white salt, the warmth coming up through them. She laid herself along it, and held, and felt the fresh brine begin to take.

Then she felt it go.

It went east. As soon as the new stillness was in the crack it was moving, not down but sideways, along the brine-thread, into the drain, under the floor, away. She held harder. It went faster. She was pouring into a sieve. Under her hands the crack widened with a sound like a fingernail drawn across slate.

"No," Hester said aloud. "No, no, come on."

"My dear." Agathe's hand was on her shoulder, harder than Hester had known it could be. "Leave it now. You'll lag."

She left it. She sat back on her heels with her hands burning cold. The crack was longer than it had been. The white was spreading up the casing toward Bett Linnow's hands.

"I'm sorry," she said to Ruth Linnow. "It's being drawn. Something's pulling it out faster than I can put it in."

The old woman nodded slowly, as if this were what she had expected.

"Then sit with me," she said. "Please. Until."

* * *

Bett Linnow had been a laundress at the Glassmarket. Her mother told it while they waited, in a low steady voice, to the casing and not to Hester, as if Bett could hear.

On the twelfth of Rime in 883 a brewer's dray had come down Glass Street too fast on the ice, and its near-side wheel had gone over Bett where she had slipped at the kerb with her basket. She had been thirty-one, with a husband who drank and a boy of four. The dispensary physician said she was bleeding inside and would not see the morning, and the Cold House had a bed. Ruth had signed. Bett's husband had been at the Anchor and could not be found until the next day. By then it was done.

"He's dead now," Ruth said. "Ten years. The boy's twenty-four, a cooper in Harl. He comes on her birthday. He doesn't remember her, only from me telling." She looked at the casing. "I've paid twenty years. I sold the house in Glass Street to pay. I live in one room over the chandler's. I told myself, Ruth, they'll find a way. They'll mend the inside of a person, and then I'll take her home." She smiled. It was a terrible thing to see. "I'm seventy-nine. I thought I'd see it."

Hester said nothing. There was nothing in her mouth to say.

"Do you think she knows?" Ruth said. "That it's been twenty years?"

"No. It's no time at all, for her. It's the same moment."

"Then she'll not know me."

The casing cracked.

It was not loud. It was a sound like river ice going in Thaw, a deep flat crack that Hester felt in her teeth. The white crazing ran over the whole casing at once, top to bottom. Then the warmth came off the plinth in a wave, the arrears of twenty years, a dry hard heat like opening an oven. The lantern on its hook swung. The casing fell in.

It did not shatter. It slumped, as a snow-figure slumps in sun, all at once. The salt lost its hold and became hot brine, pouring off the plinth on every side and steaming on the cold floor. Inside it, Bett Linnow moved.

She drew in breath, a great dragging gasp, the second half of a breath begun twenty years before in the gutter of Glass Street. And she screamed.

It was the scream she had been screaming when they kept her. Hester understood that a moment after she heard it. It was not a new scream; it was the rest of an old one. It went up and broke. Bett's body arched on the stone. Her hands came away from her mouth dark and wet and there was blood on the plinth, a great deal of it, running into the hot brine and turning it pink.

"The wheel," Bett Linnow said. "Mam. The *wheel*—"

"I'm here," said Ruth. "I'm here, my lamb."

Bett's eyes opened. They went round the ward, the high windows, the lantern, the rows of glittering casings, and understood none of it. Then they found the small old woman in the black bonnet bending over her, with a face like a dried apple. Hester saw them look.

"Mam?" said Bett Linnow, uncertainly. Not as if she knew. As if she were asking.

"Yes," said Ruth. "Yes, my lamb. It's me."

Bett frowned. It was a young woman's frown, puzzled, a little cross. She opened her mouth to say something else. Then her eyes went past her mother's face to the window, and stopped there, and the frown went out of them, and so did everything else.

It took perhaps three minutes from the crack. Hester counted afterward. Three minutes, for twenty years.

* * *

Agathe did what had to be done. She had done it before. Hester helped her because her hands needed something to do. They washed Bett Linnow in the warm pink brine, and closed her eyes, and laid her straight under a grey Cold House sheet. Ruth sat on her stool and held her daughter's hand through the sheet and did not cry. When the porters came with the long basket she walked beside it to the door without looking back. At the foot of the six steps she turned and said to Hester, in a perfectly clear voice, "Thank you for sitting with me." Then she went up, a step at a time, holding the rail.

Hester stood in the aisle. After a while she went to the fourth row, and under the third window, in the dark, found casing two hundred and eleven by memory.

Agathe hung the lantern over Tib's plinth without being asked and stood back.

The beads were still there, more of them than in Russet, and fatter, running in their lines toward the eastern foot and joining into runnels. At the foot itself, where the casing curved down over his heels and his rough boy's boots, the salt had gone thin. A month ago it had been a full two inches of clear grain. Now, at the very heel, it was perhaps half that. She could see the leather of his boot more sharply than she had ever seen anything of him through the salt. She could see a thread hanging loose from the stitching, a thread their mother would have snipped.

It was still clear. It was not crazed. It was not white.

"How long?" Hester said.

Agathe looked at the heel for some time. She was seventy and had walked these rows twice a day since before the Great Bloom, and Hester had never heard her say anything she did not believe.

"Weeks, my dear," Agathe said. "Not months." She hesitated. "I'll write. Every day, if you want."

"Every day."

Tib lay with his head turned a little to the window, his eyes half open, the pink of the bloom high on his cheeks. Hester looked at the pink, as she always did.

"Do you know what it is?" she said. "The bloom. Where it comes from."

"Nobody knows that." Agathe drew her shawl round her. "It's an old sickness. Older than the Ebb, they say. The old ballads have it. The sea-cough, they call it there. *She coughed the sea-cough and she went down to the water.* My grandmother used to sing that one." She looked at Tib. "It was in the world before the salt was. That's what the Order says. It came with the sea and it stayed when the sea went, like the tide-marks on the harbour wall."

"The sea-cough," Hester said.

"It's only a song, my dear."

* * *

She did not go to her father that night, or the next. She went back over the Causeway and did a Choir shift with her hands on the brass and the taste of hot iron still at the back of her throat. Agathe's first letter came on the seventeenth: *No change. A.L.* On the eighteenth Hester went down to the harbour and found her father at the Drowned Man.

It was the tavern he drank in when he did not want to be found in Lowside. It had a bowed ceiling and a fire that smoked, and over the bar the bowsprit of a real sea-ship, black with age, carved with a woman's face worn to a smooth knot. Aldous sat under it on a bench by the wall with a pot of Harl cider between his burned hands. He looked up when she came in. He saw her face and put the pot down.

She sat across from him and told him about Bett Linnow, plainly, in order. The crack, and the heat, and the scream, and *Mam?*, and the three minutes. Then she told him about Tib's heel.

He listened without moving. When she had finished he drank the whole pot, slowly, and put it down.

"Weeks," he said.

"Agathe says."

"Agathe's right, generally." He turned the empty pot on the table. "I'll go up tomorrow."

She did not understand him at first. Then she did.

"No," she said.

"I'll go up tomorrow and I'll sign the paper. Release. I'm his father, Hes. I'm next of kin before you, in law. I let you sign the first one because I was drunk and you were twenty-two and I hadn't the stomach. I've the stomach now." His eyes were small and red and steady. "I'll not have him come back like that lass. Screaming. In the dark. With nobody he knows."

"He'll come back with me."

"He'll come back with a woman of thirty-three he's never seen. He'll come back in the last hour of the bloom with his lungs full and he'll die in three minutes, *like that lass*, and that'll be the last thing he sees. If they do it now, quiet, with a physician and a draught—"

"They can't do it quiet. Nobody can. The arrears—"

"Then they'll do it loud. But they'll do it *for him*." His voice had come up. People at the bar were looking round. He did not lower it. "Not because a crack ran up the side. Not because you couldn't make the fee. Because it's time, and it's been time for eleven years, and somebody in this family's got to say so."

"He's *alive*, Da."

"He's kept."

"He's alive in law. His casing's clear. If they find a cure—"

"Who's they?" Aldous put both his burned hands flat on the table. "Who's *they*, Hes? You've been at the Halcyon two months. Have they a cure? Have they so much as looked for one? Or are they too busy keeping their lamps lit?"

She had no answer to that. She did not try to find one.

"I'll not let you," she said.

"It's my name on the form."

"I'll stop you."

"How?"

She did not know. She sat across from him in the smoke with her hands clenched in her lap and could think of nothing. Not law, not money, not a single argument that would stand up in the Drowned Man under the black bowsprit with its worn-away face. All she had was *no*, and she said it again. Her father looked at her across the table for a long time. Then he said the thing.

He said it quietly. He was not a cruel man and he did not say it cruelly. He said it as if he had been carrying it a long way and had finally set it down.

"You didn't keep him for him," Aldous said. "You kept him for you."

She stood up.

She did not remember deciding to. She was simply standing, and her bench was over on the floor behind her, and the whole Drowned Man had gone quiet. She could not see her father's face clearly, and did not know why until she understood that her eyes were full. She felt the tears come over her lids and run down, and felt them, as they ran, go stiff and gritty on her cheeks. *Salt.* Eight weeks in the Choir. It was starting already.

"Hes," her father said, differently.

She went out.

* * *

She did not remember the Causeway. She remembered the brass Line in the Gatehouse floor, and stepping over it in the dark, and the Stair, and the door of R-41, and Teo looking up from his bed in his Tider shawl with a book in his lap and the cracked kept-light jar glowing on the sill.

He took one look at her and put the book down.

She sat on her own bed. She told him about Bett Linnow, and Tib's heel, and the Drowned Man, and what her father had said. Teo listened, as he always listened, without moving and without helping. When she stopped he said nothing for a while. The jar on the sill made its faint summer sound, a hum like bees, the ghost of a July noon.

"Was he right?" Teo said.

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do."

She looked at him. He was not being unkind. He had the face he had in Fane's classes when he heard something under the Roar and had decided, this time, to say so.

"You told me about the night he was kept," he said. "In Russet, the night we read Aske's sheet. The fever, and your father, and the desk. You told it well. But there was a place in the middle where you went round something. I could hear it. Like a step missing on a stair."

The jar hummed. Out on the White the tiles ticked in the cold.

"He said *let me sleep*," Hester said. She heard her voice from a long way off, flat and exact. "He opened his eyes and he looked at me and he knew me. He was quite clear. He said, *Let me sleep, Hes. Just let me sleep.* And he said, *I'm so tired.* And he said, *Don't make me do it any more.*" She stopped. "He meant breathing. He'd been fighting to breathe for three weeks. He didn't mean sleep. He meant let him die. He knew it. I knew it when he said it. I knew it all the way down Coldwater Street."

Teo did not say anything.

"And I went to the desk," Hester said, "and I signed. Because I was twenty-two and I'd buried Mam and I wasn't going to bury him. Because it was there and it was six sols a month and I could keep him. So I did. And for eleven years I've told everyone, I've told myself, that keeping's sleep. That I did what he asked." The salt was stiff on her cheeks. She did not touch it. "He asked me to let him go and I kept him. That's what I did. That's all I've ever done."

She had not said it aloud before. Not to Agathe. Not to Daisy. Not to her father, who had heard it from the stair and known. Not even to herself, alone in the room in Tanner's Row with the landships on the walls. Saying it now was like returning a keeping. Something came out of her with a crack and a heat, and left a space behind it that had been full for eleven years.

Teo was quiet for a long time.

He did not say *it's all right*. He did not say *you did your best*. She had known he would not, and she had told him for that reason. He had sat nine years in the confessional at Tidewater Abbey and he did not give absolution lightly; she suspected he did not believe he had any to give. He got up and came and sat beside her on her bed, close, his shoulder against hers. The cold of him came through his shawl. He was always cold, like all of them.

"Then you've been keeping two people, Hes," he said.

It was the first time he had called her that. She heard him choose it.

"Him," Teo said. "And the girl who signed. Eleven years. Both of them. Exactly as they were." He did not look at her. He looked at the jar on the sill. "That's a great deal of stillness for one person to hold."

She did not answer. After a while she put her head down on his shoulder, on the rough grey wool of the Tider shawl, and he let her, and neither of them said anything else. The jar hummed out its old July. Outside on the White the cold came down and the tiles ticked, ticked, ticked.

* * *

She did not sleep. In the small hours she got up and dressed and went out, and Teo did not wake.

She went down the Stair, past the Second Fathom and Ama Tessaly's name in her own hand, past the Fourth, where the east gallery was boarded off with fresh planks and a Proctor's notice and smelled, still, faintly green. Down to the Fifth.

Every stay down here was fed from the Cold House, and every one of them was Orsolya's. Hester could go to Gage in the morning, or over Gage's head to the Assize, as Sorle had said. If they believed her, they would cut the line, and the Cold House would keep its own again. And the stays would starve. Nine days ago she had stood in the east gallery and watched one quickening come through a wall with sixty of them holding it and the Cold House pouring in behind. Take the Cold House away and the Fourth was the whole Halcyon: three thousand people, and the White under them, and whatever the salt had stopped in the middle of. To save her brother she had to name Orsolya, and naming Orsolya would drown the house. She could not find the third thing. She went on down looking for it.

Her Choir pass took her through the grille at the landing. The Fathom lamps were turned low and blue, and somewhere far off a skeleton watch of four choristers was holding the night stays in a slow murmur. She kept away from them. She went along the inner gallery to the Choir store: a long low room cut into the salt, with shelves of oiled stay-lines and racks of brass fittings and barrels of Middling for tuning. And behind a cage of iron wire at the back, the residue.

She had seen it on every shift and never looked at it. The warm wet Hush-grade crust the Choir scraped off the stays after a quickening was held, packed into sealed boxes to be carried up the lift. A dozen of them stood stacked inside the cage, numbered in chalk, waiting for a clerk with ink on his cuffs. The cage was padlocked. The wire was old. On one box near the front the lid had been knocked askew in the stacking, and through the gap, in the blue lamplight, she could see grain: white, faintly blue, sharp-cornered. She could feel the warmth of it through the wire.

If she could not stop the draw, she could outrun it. Hush grain kept anything clear, almost at once. A handful in fresh brine, laid along the heel of casing two hundred and eleven, would close that thinning in a single hold, better than any recast. Nobody counted residue by the handful. Nobody would know.

She put her fingers through the wire.

The gap between the box and the cage was a hand's width. She could reach the grain. It was warm, a warm-stone warmth, and the taste of it came up her arm into her mouth before it ever reached her tongue, the vast bright startled weight of it. *Quick.* Her fingers closed on a little of it. Enough, perhaps. She stood in the cold blue dark with her arm through the cage to the wrist and her fist full of felony salt.

*Kept beyond return.*

The Physic's voice, in Cell 3-17, over Aske's reaching arm. *You'd never get the arrears out of Hush grain without killing him.*

She would mend his heel, and she would be laying along the foot of her brother a stillness no one alive knew how to give back. She would never be able to let him go. Not for a cure. Not for her father. Not if Tib himself sat up in his casing and asked her. She would have made sure of it.

And some part of her—she felt it quite clearly, standing in the cold with her fist in the cage—*wanted that*. It wanted it more than it had ever wanted anything. To have him kept so well that no one could ever take him, not the draw, not the arrears, not her father with his paper, not the boy himself.

*You didn't keep him for him.*

She opened her hand.

The grain fell back into the box with a small dry hiss, like sand. She drew her arm out of the cage. Then she stood in the Choir store with her hands at her sides and found that she was shaking from head to foot, as she had shaken in the ice of the east gallery. Not from cold. From the lag of something she had nearly done.

The four night choristers murmured on somewhere in the blue dark, holding the line.

She went up as far as the Rookery well-room and no further. The pump-handle was furred with frost. She worked it until the water came, and held her hands under it, and washed the salt-dust off her fingers, front and back and between, and under the nails, slowly, until there was nothing left on them at all.
