# Chapter 18

## The Fourth Sleeper

Agathe's letter came up the Causeway on the sixteenth of Cinder with the afternoon post. It was in the Proctors' bag, in a cheap grey envelope with the Cold House's name printed on the flap. Hester's name was on the front in a round careful hand that had been taught its letters by Tider sisters sixty years ago and had never seen a reason to change them.

*My dear. Bett Linnow is coming back. The casing has cracked at the foot and the brinewright says there is no more to be done and Mr. Pell is at his sister's. Her mother is here. She asks for someone who understands the salt. I do not, not as you do. If you can come, come. A.L.*

Hester read it standing in the Rookery passage. Then she put on her coat and went.

It had been raining on the Rim for two days, the short hard rain of Cinder that came over the Varrow and fell on Ambry and never reached the White. The Causeway was black and shining. Out on the plain on either side of it the salt was bone-dry, and the rain stopped at the Causeway's edge as if it had come to a wall. She walked the two miles in the wet, fast, with her collar up and her hands in her armpits. She was at the Cold House by the ninth bell.

Ennis was not at the desk. Nobody was at the desk. The great ledger lay open on the felt with a pen across it. She went past it and through the double doors and down the six worn steps. On the third step she smelled it: not the cold sweet cellar smell of the charity ward, but something warm. Warm, and sharp, and faintly like hot iron.

* * *

Bett Linnow's plinth was in the second row, near the eastern wall.

Hester had passed it a hundred and thirty times on the first of the month without looking. A woman of about thirty, kept on her side with her knees drawn up and both hands pressed low against her belly. Her hair was the colour of wet sand and her face was turned into the crook of her arm. Hester had always taken her for asleep. Most of them looked asleep from the aisle.

She did not look asleep now. Agathe had hung a lantern on a hook above the plinth and the casing in its light was not clear any more. It was white. Not the grey of the dead, but a fine white crazing, like ice on a puddle when you put your heel on it, spreading out from a crack at the casing's foot. The crack was as long as Hester's hand. Brine was running out of it in a steady thread. The thread did not drip onto the floor. It ran sideways along the plinth's edge and down the eastern side and into the drain-grille in the floor beneath, as if it had somewhere to be.

The casing was warm. She could feel it from a yard away, like a stove going out. The arrears were coming.

An old woman sat on a stool beside the plinth with both her hands flat on the stone, as close to the casing as the stone allowed. She was very small and very upright, in a black wool coat that had been good once and a black bonnet. Agathe stood behind her with a hand on her shoulder.

"Ruth," Agathe said. "This is Hester. Tobias Vail's sister. From the fourth row. She's at the Halcyon now. She understands the salt."

The old woman looked up. Her eyes were pale and wet and enormously tired.

"Can you stop it?" she said.

* * *

Hester tried.

She knew before she began that she could not. She had known on the Causeway, reading Agathe's letter again under a lamp-post with the rain on it. She tried anyway, because the old woman had asked, and because it was the only thing in the world she knew how to do.

The house kept recasting brine in a copper tank in the brinewright's closet, Middling grain dissolved in water, to be worked within the hour. Agathe drew a jugful. It was not good brine, and not fresh, and Hester was not licensed, and there was no Reader in the building to say so. She knelt on the cold floor by the cracked foot of the casing and poured a thin line of the brine along the crack, slowly. She went into the hold.

It was not like keeping a falling drop at the Pans for Crisp. It was not even like holding with the Choir. A casing was a made thing. To recast it you had to find the hand that had made it, the grain-pattern of the first keeping, and match it, and lay your own stillness along it like a mason laying a new course of brick on an old wall. Bett Linnow's casing had been made twenty years ago by some Cold House brinewright long dead. Its hand was coarse and quick, a cheap hand, a charity hand. Hester felt for it with her eyes shut and her palms flat on the crazed white salt, the warmth coming up through them. She found it. She laid herself along it, and held, and felt the fresh brine begin to take.

Then she felt it go.

It went east. As soon as the new stillness was in the crack it was moving. She felt it slide out of the casing the way water slides out of a cracked jug, not down but sideways, along the brine-thread, down into the drain, under the floor, away. She held harder. It went faster. She was pouring into a sieve. Under her hands the crack widened with a sound like a fingernail drawn across slate.

"No," Hester said aloud. "No, no, come on."

"My dear." Agathe's hand was on her shoulder, harder than Hester had known it could be. "My dear, leave it. Leave it now. You'll lag."

She left it. She came out of the hold and sat back on her heels with her hands burning cold. The crack was longer than it had been. The white was spreading up the casing toward Bett Linnow's sand-coloured hair.

"I'm sorry," she said to Ruth Linnow. "It's being drawn. Something's pulling it out faster than I can put it in. I can't."

The old woman nodded, slowly, as if this were what she had expected.

"Then sit with me," she said. "Please. Until."

* * *

Bett Linnow had been a laundress at the Glassmarket. Her mother told it while they waited, in a low steady voice, to the casing and not to Hester, as if Bett could hear.

On the twelfth of Rime in 883 a brewer's dray had come down Glass Street too fast on the ice, and its near-side wheel had gone over Bett where she had slipped at the kerb with her basket. She had been thirty-one. She had a husband who drank and a boy of four. The Exchequer dispensary physician had said she was bleeding inside and would not see the morning, and the Cold House had a bed. Ruth had signed. Bett's husband had been at the Anchor and could not be found until the next day. By then it was done.

"He's dead now," Ruth said. "Ten years. The boy's twenty-four. He's a cooper in Harl. He comes on her birthday. He doesn't remember her, only from me telling. I've told him every week of his life." She looked at the casing. "I've paid twenty years. I sold the house in Glass Street to pay. I live in one room over the chandler's. I told myself, Ruth, they'll find a way. The Halcyon. They find ways every year. They'll find a way to mend the inside of a person, and then I'll take her home." She smiled. It was a terrible thing to see. "I'm seventy-nine. I thought I'd see it."

Hester said nothing. There was nothing in her mouth to say.

"Do you think she knows?" Ruth said. "That it's been twenty years?"

"No," Hester said. "No. It's no time at all, for her. It's the same moment."

"Then she'll not know me."

The casing cracked.

It was not loud. It was a sound like river ice going in Thaw, a deep flat crack that Hester felt in her teeth. The white crazing ran up over the whole casing in an instant, top to bottom, like frost racing across a pane. Then the warmth came. It came off the plinth in a wave, the arrears of twenty years, a dry hard heat like opening an oven. Hester's eyes watered with it and the lantern on its hook swung. The casing fell in.

It did not shatter. It slumped, the way a snow-figure slumps in sun, all at once. The salt lost its hold and became brine, hot brine, pouring off the plinth on every side in a rush and steaming on the cold floor. Inside it, Bett Linnow moved.

She had been kept on her side with her hands pressed to her belly. Now she drew in breath, a great dragging gasp, the second half of a breath begun twenty years before in the gutter of Glass Street. And she screamed.

It was the scream she had been screaming when they kept her. Hester understood that a moment after she heard it. It was not a new scream; it was the rest of an old one. It went up and up and broke. Bett's body arched on the stone. Her hands came away from her belly dark and wet and there was blood on the plinth, a great deal of it, running into the hot brine and turning it pink.

"The wheel," Bett Linnow said. "Mam. The *wheel*—"

"I'm here," said Ruth. "I'm here, my lamb. I'm here."

Bett's eyes opened. They went round the ward, the high windows, the lantern, the rows of glittering casings. They did not understand any of it. Then they found the old woman bending over her, the small old woman in a black bonnet with a face like a dried apple. They looked at her. Hester saw them look.

"Mam?" said Bett Linnow, uncertainly. Not as if she knew. As if she were asking.

"Yes," said Ruth. "Yes, my lamb. It's me."

Bett frowned. It was a young woman's frown, puzzled, a little cross. She opened her mouth to say something else. Then her eyes went past her mother's face to the window, and stopped there, and the frown went out of them, and so did everything else.

It took perhaps three minutes from the crack. Hester counted afterward. Three minutes, for twenty years.

* * *

Agathe did what had to be done. She had done it before. Hester helped her because her hands needed something to do. They washed Bett Linnow in the warm pink brine on the plinth, and closed her eyes, and laid her straight, and covered her with a grey Cold House sheet. Ruth sat on her stool and held her daughter's hand through the sheet and did not speak and did not cry. When the porters came with the long basket she stood up and walked beside it to the door without looking back. At the foot of the six steps she turned and said to Hester, in a perfectly clear voice, "Thank you for sitting with me." Then she went up, slowly, a step at a time, holding the rail.

Hester stood in the aisle.

She went, after a while, to the fourth row. Under the third window, in the dark, she found casing two hundred and eleven by memory.

Agathe brought the lantern. She hung it on the hook over Tib's plinth without being asked and stood back. Hester looked.

The beads were still there. More of them than in Russet, and fatter. They ran in their lines toward the eastern foot of the plinth, joining into runnels. At the foot itself, where the casing curved down over his heels and his rough boy's boots, the salt had gone thin. She could see it. A month ago the casing had been as thick there as everywhere else, a full two inches of clear grain. Now, at the very heel, it was perhaps half that. She could see the leather of his boot through it more sharply than she had ever seen anything of him through the salt. She could see a thread hanging loose from the stitching, a thread their mother would have snipped.

It was still clear. It was not crazed. It was not white.

"How long?" Hester said.

Agathe did not answer at once. She looked at the heel of the casing for some time. She was seventy and had walked these rows twice a day since before the Great Bloom, and Hester had never heard her say anything she did not believe.

"Weeks, my dear," Agathe said. "Not months."

Hester nodded.

"The Hallam boy went faster. Bett went slower. It's not the same each time." Agathe hesitated. "I'll write. Every day, if you want."

"Every day."

"Every day, then."

They stood. Tib lay in his casing with his head turned a little to the window and his half-open eyes and the pink of the bloom high on his cheeks. Hester looked at the pink, as she always did, and found herself thinking of nothing else.

"Do you know what it is?" she said. "The bloom. Where it comes from. What it is."

"Nobody knows that." Agathe drew her shawl round her. "It's an old sickness. Older than the Ebb, they say. The old ballads have it. The sea-cough, they call it there. *She coughed the sea-cough and she went down to the water.* My grandmother used to sing that one." She looked at Tib. "It was in the world before the salt was. That's what the Order says. It came with the sea and it stayed when the sea went, like the tide-marks on the harbour wall."

"The sea-cough," Hester said.

"It's only a song, my dear."

* * *

She found her father at the Drowned Man.

It was the harbour tavern he drank in when he did not want to be found in Lowside. It had a bowed ceiling and a fire that smoked, and the bowsprit of a real sea-ship over the bar, black with age, carved with a woman's face worn away to a smooth knot. Aldous sat under it on a bench by the wall with a pot of Harl cider and his burned hands round it. He looked up when she came in. He saw her face and put the pot down.

She sat down across from him. She told him about Bett Linnow, plainly, in order, as she would have written up a seizure. The crack, and the heat, and the scream, and *Mam?*, and the three minutes. Then she told him about Tib's heel.

He listened to all of it without moving. When she had finished he picked up his cider and drank the whole pot, slowly, and put it down.

"Weeks," he said.

"Agathe says."

"Agathe's right, generally." He turned the empty pot on the table. "I'll go up tomorrow."

She did not understand him for a moment. Then she did.

"No," she said.

"I'll go up tomorrow and I'll sign the paper. Release. I'm his father, Hes. I'm next of kin before you, in law. I let you sign the first one because I was drunk and you were twenty-two and I hadn't the stomach. I've the stomach now." He looked at her. His eyes were small and red and steady. "I'll not have him come back like that lass. Screaming. In the dark. With nobody he knows."

"He'll come back with me."

"He'll come back with a woman of thirty-three he's never seen. He'll come back in the last hour of the bloom with his lungs full and he'll die in three minutes, *like that lass*, and you'll stand and watch it, and that'll be the last thing he sees. If they do it now, quiet, with a physician and a draught, with the fever down—"

"They can't do it quiet. Nobody can. The arrears—"

"Then they'll do it loud. But they'll do it *for him*." His voice had come up. People at the bar were looking round. He did not lower it. "Not because a crack ran up the side. Not because you couldn't make the fee. Because it's time, and it's been time for eleven years, and somebody in this family's got to say so."

"He's *alive*, Da."

"He's kept."

"He's alive in law. His casing's clear. If they find a cure—"

"Who's they?" Aldous put both his burned hands flat on the table. "Who's *they*, Hes? You've been at the Halcyon two months. You've seen them. Have they a cure? Have they so much as looked for one? Or are they too busy keeping their lamps lit?"

She had no answer to that. She did not try to find one.

"I'll not let you," she said.

"You can't stop me. It's my name on the form."

"I'll stop you."

"How?"

She did not know. She sat across from him in the smoke with her hands clenched in her lap and could think of nothing. Not law, not money, not a single argument that would stand up in the Drowned Man under the black bowsprit with its worn-away face. All she had was *no*, and she said it again. Her father looked at her across the table for a long moment. Then he said the thing.

He said it quite quietly. He was not a cruel man and he did not say it cruelly. He said it as if he had been carrying it a long way and had finally set it down.

"You didn't keep him for him," Aldous said. "You kept him for you."

She stood up.

She did not remember deciding to. She was simply standing, and her bench was over on the floor behind her, and the whole Drowned Man had gone quiet. She looked down at her father. She could not see his face clearly, and she did not know why until she understood that her eyes were full. She felt the tears come over her lids and run down, and felt them, as they ran, go stiff and gritty on her cheeks. *Salt.* Six weeks in the Choir. It was starting already.

"Hes," her father said, differently.

She went out.

* * *

She did not remember the Causeway. She remembered the Gatehouse, and the brass Line in its floor, and stepping over it in the dark. She remembered the Stair. She remembered the door of R-41 and Teo looking up from his bed in his Tider shawl with a book in his lap and the cracked kept-light jar glowing on the sill.

He took one look at her and put the book down.

She sat on her own bed. She told him about Bett Linnow. She told him about Tib's heel. She told him about her father, and the Drowned Man, and what her father had said. Teo listened, as he always listened, without moving and without helping. When she stopped he said nothing for a while. The jar on the sill made its faint summer sound, a hum like bees, the ghost of a July noon.

"Was he right?" Teo said.

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do."

She looked at him. He looked back. He was not being unkind. He had the face he had in Fane's classes when he heard something under the Roar and had decided, this time, to say so.

"You told me in Russet," he said, "that Tib asked to sleep. That your father heard him. That you thought keeping was sleep, so you did what he asked. That's what you said. I've thought about it since. I wondered if it was true."

The jar hummed. Out on the White the tiles ticked in the cold.

"He said *let me sleep*," Hester said. She heard her voice from a long way off, the customs voice, flat and exact. "He opened his eyes and he looked at me and he knew me. He was quite clear. He said, *Let me sleep, Hes. Just let me sleep.* And he said, *I'm so tired.* And he said, *Don't make me do it any more.*" She stopped. "He meant breathing. He'd been fighting to breathe for three weeks. He meant he didn't want to do it any more. He didn't mean sleep. He meant let him die. He knew it. I knew it. I knew it when he said it. I knew it all the way down Coldwater Street."

Teo did not say anything.

"And I went to the desk," Hester said, "and I signed. Because I couldn't. Because I was twenty-two and I'd buried Mam and I wasn't going to bury him. Because I could. Because it was there and it was six sols a month and I could keep him. So I did. And for eleven years I've told everyone, I've told myself, that he asked for it. That keeping's sleep. That I did what he wanted." The salt was stiff on her cheeks. She did not touch it. "He asked me to let him go and I kept him. That's what I did. That's all I've ever done."

She had not said it aloud before. Not to Agathe. Not to Daisy. Not to her father, who had heard it from the stair and known. She had not said it aloud even to herself, alone in the room in Tanner's Row with the landships on the walls. Saying it now was like returning a keeping. Something came out of her with a crack and a heat, and left a space behind it that had been full for eleven years.

Teo was quiet for a long time.

He did not say *it's all right*. He did not say *you did your best*. She had known he would not, and she had told him for that reason. He had sat nine years in the confessional at Tidewater Abbey and he did not give absolution lightly; she suspected he did not believe he had any to give. He got up off his bed and came and sat beside her on hers, close, his shoulder against hers. The cold of him came through his shawl. He was always cold, like all of them.

"Then you've been keeping two people, Hes," he said.

It was the first time he had called her that. She heard him choose it.

"Him," Teo said. "And the girl who signed. Eleven years. Both of them. Exactly as they were." He did not look at her. He looked at the jar on the sill. "That's a great deal of stillness for one person to hold."

She did not answer. After a while she put her head down on his shoulder, on the rough grey wool of the Tider shawl, and he let her, and neither of them said anything else. The jar hummed out its old July. Outside on the White the cold came down and the tiles ticked, ticked, ticked.

* * *

She did not sleep. At the second bell after midnight she got up, and dressed, and went out, and Teo did not wake.

She went down the Stair. Past the Second Fathom and Ama Tessaly's name in her own seizure hand. Past the Fourth, where the east gallery was boarded off with fresh planks and a Proctor's notice and smelled, still, faintly green. Down to the Fifth.

Her Choir pass took her through the grille at the Fifth Fathom landing. The Fifth at night was cold and quiet. The Fathom lamps were turned low and blue, and somewhere far off a skeleton watch of four choristers was holding the night stays in a slow murmur. She knew where they were and kept away from them. She went instead along the inner gallery to the Choir store.

It was a long low room cut into the salt, with shelves of oiled stay-lines and racks of brass fittings and, at the far end, barrels of Middling for tuning. And behind a cage of iron wire at the very back, the residue.

She had seen it on every shift and never looked at it. Stay salt. The grain that came out of the walls after a quickening was held, the warm wet Hush-grade crust the Choir scraped off the stays and packed into sealed boxes to be carried up the lift. The boxes stood stacked inside the wire cage, a dozen of them, numbered in chalk, waiting for the tenth bell and a clerk with ink on his cuffs. The cage was padlocked. The wire was old. On one of the boxes, near the front, the lid had been knocked askew in the stacking. Through the gap, in the blue lamplight, Hester could see grain. White, faintly blue, sharp-cornered. She could feel the warmth of it through the wire from where she stood.

Hush grain. It kept anything clear, almost at once. Gage had said so, over Aske's casing, and Gage was wrong about a great deal but not about that. A handful of it in fresh brine, laid along the heel of casing two hundred and eleven, would close that thinning in a single hold. It would close it better than the Cold House brinewright could, better than any recast. A handful. Nobody counted residue by the handful. Nobody would know.

She put her fingers through the wire.

The gap between the box and the cage was a hand's width. She could reach the lid. She could reach the grain. She touched it. It was warm, a warm-stone warmth, and the taste of it came up her arm into her mouth before it ever reached her tongue, the vast bright startled weight of it. *Quick.* Her fingers closed on a little of it. Enough, perhaps. She stood there in the cold blue dark with her arm through the cage to the wrist and her fist full of felony salt.

*Kept beyond return.*

Gage's voice, in Cell 3-17, over Aske's reaching arm. *Hush arrears would kill him. In law, dead.*

If she laid Hush grain on Tib's casing, she would mend the heel. And she would be laying, along the foot of her brother, a stillness that no one alive knew how to give back. The arrears of Hush grain could not be paid. Every Reader in the Halcyon agreed on that, even Sorle. A man kept in Hush was a man kept for ever. The law had a name for it, and the name was *dead*.

She would mend his heel, and she would never be able to let him go. Not for a cure. Not for her father. Not if Tib himself sat up in his casing and asked her. She would have made sure of it.

And some part of her—she felt it, quite clearly, standing in the cold with her fist in the cage—some part of her *wanted that*. It wanted it more than it had ever wanted anything. To be sure. To have him kept so well that no one could ever take him, not the draw, not the arrears, not her father with his paper, not the boy himself.

*You didn't keep him for him.*

She opened her hand.

The grain fell back into the box with a small dry hiss, like sand. She drew her arm out of the cage. Her fingers were white with salt-dust to the knuckle and she wiped them on her gown, carefully, front and back. Then she stood in the Choir store with her hands at her sides and found that she was shaking from head to foot, as she had shaken in the ice of the east gallery. Not from cold. From the lag of something she had nearly done.

The four night choristers murmured on somewhere far off in the blue dark, holding the line.

Hester went back up the Stair. She did not go to R-41. She stopped on the Second Fathom by her own stretch of wall, 641 to 655, and the new name at the bottom of it, and sat down on the step. She sat with her back against the cold salt and her knees drawn up. She did not cry, because she found she had nothing left to cry with. She sat there until the first bell rang down the Stair from the Crown, and the grey began, faintly, to come down the Lantern Well.
