# Chapter 19

## The Notebook

> The archive is a keeping like any other, and like any other it is owed.
>
> — *Wynn Callow, Under-Archivist, the only sentence he is known to have published (*Proceedings of the Brinestacks*, 851 Y.E.)*

Agathe wrote every day, as she had promised. Most of the letters were a line long. *No change. A.L.* The one dated the nineteenth of Cinder was longer, and Hester read it standing at the post-tray in the Gatehouse with her back to the room.

*My dear. Your father came to the desk this afternoon. He asked Ennis for the form and he gave it him. He sat with the pen in his hand for as long as it takes to say the Ebb-prayer twice through. Then he put the pen down and went out. He did not sign. He did not come down to the ward. I thought you would want to know it from me. Tib is as he was. A.L.*

She folded it along its creases and put it in her pocket with the others. She did not go to the Pans. She thought of her father's burned hands round a pen, holding it the way he held a rake, and could not think about it for long.

* * *

Wynn Callow had been sitting at his desk in the Brinestacks for longer than most of the Readers had been alive.

He sat at the foot of the Second Fathom's main stair, behind a desk of black oak worn pale where his forearms rested: a small man in a grey gown so old the wool had gone the colour of ash, his white hair standing out a little from his skull like a dandelion's. His hands lay on the desk one over the other and did not move. He had the Settle so deep in him that he breathed perhaps four times a minute, and an aspirant could stand at the desk asking for a catalogue number for a full minute before deciding there was nobody there. Hester had decided exactly that on her first day. Teo said everyone did.

*Corvin sat there too.* He had said it to them in Russet as they were leaving, out of nowhere, and then, *There. On the first of the month.* The only words she had ever heard him speak. She had taken them for an old man's remark about a dead colleague. They came back to her the night after Bett Linnow died, lying awake in R-41. *Sat there.* Not *read there*. Sat, as you sit at someone's elbow when you want something from them and know it will take time.

She wanted proof. She had had a name in her since Fallow, since a candle flame in the Warden's Gallery, and she had said *Dunmore* aloud to Teo for three weeks while she thought it. A name was not proof. Aske had been taking his proofs somewhere.

So on the twenty-fifth of Cinder, at the fifth bell, when she should have been in the Black Bench taking down Sorle's lecture on the decay curves of kept light, she took a stool from the reading room, set it at the end of Callow's desk, and sat down.

He did not look at her. She had not expected him to.

"Under-Archivist," she said. "I'm Hester Vail. Reader Aske's privilege. Reader Sorle's Bench, now." She waited. "You said Corvin sat here too."

Nothing. Two second-years went up the stair behind her arguing about the Assay. The lamp on Callow's desk was an old kept-light lamp, its glass yellowed, giving off the faint unchanging warmth of a summer afternoon that had been over for decades.

She folded her hands in her lap and let her breathing slow, not into the hold, only toward it.

After a time Callow's eyes moved. They came round from the middle distance to the corner of the desk, where her hands were, and rested there.

The sixth bell rang somewhere above. Faint through the salt she heard the Long Table being laid for the Lantern Hour. Her left foot went to sleep. She did not move it.

"He brought," said Callow.

His voice was dry and soft and astonishingly clear, like paper being unfolded. Then it stopped. She counted, and got to forty.

"Something back."

"Back where?"

Fifty. Sixty. Somebody laughed in the reading room and was shushed.

"Where it was not," said Callow, "to be looked for."

"Where's that?"

He did not answer for so long that she thought he had gone. Then, with a deliberation she would not have believed of a living man, Wynn Callow stood up.

It took him nearly a quarter of an hour. Callow did not so much stand as unfold, a joint at a time, each movement starting so slowly that she only knew it had happened because his shoulder was higher than it had been. She did not help him. It would have been like helping a glacier. Then he drew a key on a black string out of the breast of his gown, a long brass key with a halcyon on its bow, and held it in his palm.

"Where the founders," he said, "sleep."

* * *

The Founders' Gallery was at the far end of the Second Fathom, through a door of lattice-grown salt with a brass grille in it and a sign: RESTRICTED. BY LEAVE OF THE WARDEN OF THE STACKS. It took Callow twenty minutes to walk there. Aspirants coming the other way stopped and stared, and stopped again at the corners to look back.

The key turned with a sound like a cough.

Inside was cold and dry and dark. Hester found a kept-light lantern on a hook by the door and dissolved its cake, and a spring morning came up yellow-white around them and showed her a long vaulted room lined to the roof with drawers: thousands of them, small and shallow, in black wood, each with a label in faded copperplate.

*230 Y.E. — Fire-salvage — Founders' Tablets — I.*

On and on along the wall, into the dark, drawers of salt tablets burnt black and slumped like candle-wax.

Callow had already started down the gallery, trailing his fingertips along the drawer-fronts. At XIV he stopped. Then his hand came up, a fraction at a time, toward the brass pull.

"I'll do it," she said.

He took his hand away.

The drawer was full of char, like the others. She pulled harder and it came out of its runners altogether, heavier than she expected, and she set it on the floor. Behind it, where the back of the drawer should have met the back of the cabinet, there was a gap of perhaps four inches. Wedged upright in the gap was a bundle in brown oilcloth tied with tarred string.

It was the size and weight of three thick books. Something small and hard shifted inside it. She sat back on her heels and untied the string.

Two marbled stationer's notebooks of the kind the Glassmarket sold for four flecks, their pages swollen from use; and the black leather one from his table, with a stub of torn stitching near the back where a leaf was gone. Ink showed through in black and blue in a small fast sloping hand. Tucked between them was a horn case no bigger than a thumb. She knew it before she opened it. Inside, on green baize, lay a tasting spoon of fine white metal, finer than any on her old rack at the Customs House.

*May I?*

She closed the case, and had to sit a while.

When she looked up, Callow was watching her. Not the middle distance: her. It might have been grief in his pale eyes, or satisfaction, held still so long they had become the same thing.

"He said," Callow said, "if anyone came."

She waited.

"Who sat."

* * *

Teo did not go to bed that night. Neither did she.

They sat in R-41 with the door wedged shut and the candle on the floor between them and the three notebooks open on Teo's blanket. Teo had his Tider shawl round his shoulders, a pencil behind his ear and another in his hand, and a slate on his knee. He had eaten, by her count, four slices of saltcake since the tenth bell without noticing any of them.

"It's not a cipher," he had said at the start. "Don't hope for anything dramatic. It's cutters' shorthand. The Abbey used it for the tablet copies. Nobody uses it any more except old copyists and, apparently, Reader Aske, who uses it badly, with personal abbreviations, in two colours, for reasons known only to God." He had squinted. "Blue is grain. Black is people, I think. Give me an hour."

It had taken him six.

He read aloud as he went, in pieces, and sometimes stopped for twenty minutes over a single line and swore at it softly in the vocabulary of a man who had spent nine years in a religious house. Hester sat against the wall with her knees up and refilled his water.

The first marbled book began in Thaw of 902 and was grain, in blue: a crate of curing salt from a Ferrers yard that tasted of the Fifth. Another. *Warm. Bead. Spat in the basin.* By midsummer he had stopped being surprised.

The second was people. Black ink. It began with Dunmore.

*E.D. — Edwin Dunmore, clerk to the Warden's office. Handles the residue contracts. Residue = quickened grain cut out of the stays when the stays are re-laid. Should be dissolved in the Fifth and the brine pumped to the sinks. Is not. Is boxed. Boxes go up the box-lift, Fifth to Third, at the tenth bell, three nights a week, carried by aspirants paid two sols a run.*

Teo looked up. "That's Saskia."

"I know."

"He doesn't name her. Just *aspirants*." He read on. *Ferrers & Lisk freighter. East to Quell, a Ferrers warehouse, relabelled Shelf, then back west and sold as curing grain to the Hask kept-fish trade. Hask buyers complain their fish spit when the keep is broken. Nobody listens to Hask buyers.* And under that, in a different pen: *W.L.F. per E.D. on the ladings. Warden of the Lower Fathoms, per Dunmore. Bold as brass. The office has signed residue contracts for two hundred years. Nobody reads them.*

"You knew that," Teo said.

"I knew that." She had known it since Lucan came back from the Upper Ward with the contract in his coat. She had given Teo a small, neat, clerkish theory to go with it, with Dunmore at the centre: a bland man who sold what he was told to destroy, and a Warden who signed what her clerk put in front of her. She had not believed a word of it herself.

Teo opened the black book. The ink was blue again.

*1 Russet. Stacks: Founders' Cistern plans, for the east trunk survey. Why from the city?*

*1 Russet. Miss Vail. Taste and weight. Graded my twelve blind. Named No. 9 as twice-kept. Heard a cry in the Wren grain, did not know it. I did not tell her. God forgive me, I want to see her face when she hears it in the Stacks.*

Teo stopped and looked at her over the candle. She looked at the window.

"Go on," she said.

*5 Russet. Cold House, Coldwater St. Director at his sister's; Mother Agathe walked me down the rows. Her brother in 211. 14 weeping. All east. Taste: quick. Tasted the run-off myself. Quick, and under it something stiller than I have ever had in my mouth. Old keepings get still. These are being bled.*

*Harl St Brinery — 0. Varenne Private — 0. Charity only. Of course charity only. Nobody audits charity.*

*A draw? Still runs to quick. It wants a brine contact. A line. Cold House stands on the old reservoir—Cistern?? The founders' lines. Bricked in 400. Bricked by whom?*

"That's the sheet," Hester said. "From his blotter." She put her thumb to the torn stitching. "That's the leaf. He tore it out for me and took the book away."

Teo read ahead with his lips moving. "Hester. He went into the Cistern."

*12 Russet. Under the Cold House by the cellar. Agathe's key. The old main line is open. Brass sleeves, new, Lattice marks. Live brine in the sleeve. Still as death. Runs east.*

*13 Russet. Walked the plans. The main line runs under the White, two miles, and comes up in the Fifth Fathom at the Old Tap. Founders' brass. I have passed it a hundred times. The east gallery stay-lines are fed from it. The stays have been holding beautifully all summer, and I am a fool.*

*14 Russet. Measured. The east stays draw 30 parts in 100 of their stillness from the Old Tap. Without it they would have failed in Seedmonth. Without it the Hush would be in the Fifth by now.*

Teo put down the pencil.

"Thirty parts in a hundred," he said. "From the Cold House."

Hester did not answer. She had tasted it at the Silent Supper, with the brine splashing her mouth and the sea roaring in the walls: the cold, old stillness of the line under Coldwater Street, coming up the stay-line from the east. She had known since that night that the stays were fed by the sleepers. Here it was in a Reader's hand, measured. Tib had been in the line that night, holding the east gallery up with her.

"Is there more?" she said.

"The last page."

"Read it."

Teo read it slowly, in the voice he used for scripture when he did not want to mock it, and she listened with her eyes shut.

*15 Russet. Told Miss V. tomorrow at ten. Showed her the beating bead. She went white. She has the tongue for this, God help her, and she has a brother in the line.*

*I know whose office this is. I have known since the 13th and pretended not to, because I have known her near thirty years and she taught me to hold in the Choir when I was nineteen and too frightened to breathe. She is the best of us. I think she has done this because she is the best of us.*

*She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held. The pressure wants a road, not a lid. Vent? Let a little of it go somewhere it can do no harm, a little at a time. Need Sorle's arrears for the shaft—he'll crow for a decade, let him. I'll ask O. tomorrow, before I tell Gage. I owe her that.*

*If anything—tell Hester to taste it.*

The candle had burned to a stub. The moon had gone behind the Lantern, and its mirrors made a thin black bar across the White.

"She," Teo said quietly.

"I heard."

"Dunmore's not a she."

"No."

He closed the notebook and sat with his hand flat on its black cover, and looked at her, and saw that she was not surprised.

"How long?" he said.

"Since Fallow. She kept a candle for me in the Gallery and showed me her hand in it." She heard how flat it came out. "I wanted to be wrong. I'm on her books. She put a blanket on me after my first stay. I wanted it to be Dunmore so badly I said it out loud to you every night."

He did not say the name either. He was too kind to, and she loved him for it and hated him for it, both at once.

"*Ask O.*," he said instead. "And you let me go on about the Order."

"Yes." She stood up. Her knees were stiff and she was thirsty, the Halcyon thirst, as though she had been holding. "He said taste it. I haven't. I've had a candle and a notebook. A notebook says *she*." She took her gown from its peg. "If I'm going to say it, I'll say it off the salt. Off my tongue."

"It's the middle of the night."

"Then nobody will be in the Quiet Rooms."

He looked at her over the guttering candle, crumbs on his chin.

"I'll be here," he said.

* * *

The Fourth Fathom in the small hours was dark and cold, and there was no sound but the hum, the long low pressure from below that she had felt in her teeth on her first day and long since stopped noticing. She noticed it now.

The Quiet Rooms were not locked. Nobody locked them. Who would steal a keeping?

Aske's casing stood where the porters had set it in Russet, between a girl kept in the act of laughing and a Reader in a gown three hundred years out of fashion. Hester set the lantern at the plinth's foot and looked up at him.

He had not changed. That was what keeping meant, and still it went through her. His arm was out, the ink-stained fingers spread. His mouth was open on whatever he had been about to say. Through the casing, clear as clean water, she could see the frayed cuff of his shirt, and a fleck of salt-mud on his gown at the knee.

The casing was smooth as glass at its outer face and frosted against his skin, set from the outside in. Poured, by someone standing in front of him. Sorle had said it in Fallow: *a child could see that casing was poured*.

She knelt, took off her right glove as she had in the Cold House on the first of Russet, and put the tip of her smallest finger to the casing where it met the plinth. She touched the finger to her tongue.

The weight came down. Hush grain: the door closing, the white, the vast pressing on the far side of her face. She went into the hold. It came easily now; three months ago it had been something she found by accident on a customs stool, and now she could put it on like a coat. The taste opened, and she read.

She did not try to read Aske. She did not want to hear what he had been saying when the brine went over him. She went past the moment and into the hand of whoever had set it. She knew what she was looking for. She had known since Fallow. She had come down here to be told she was wrong.

The grain turned. Under the weight and the white she felt it as a slow corkscrew of set salt, layer winding on layer, every one turning the same way: from the right hand toward the left, widdershins, against the sun. At the end of each turn there was a small hard counter-flick, like the snap of a wrist.

*Every worker leaves a hand, chick.* Orsolya in the Warden's Gallery in Fallow, a tallow candle between them and a dish of live brine, her wet left fingers flicking at the wick, the wrist snapping back. The flame going still, a little yellow tongue in a bead of clear salt. *Mine's a corkscrew. My mam said I came out twisting.* Both of them laughing, and something at the back of Hester's mind giving a small hard tug, like a fish on a line. She had felt it then. She had gone up the Stair with it and told no one.

It was the same hand. Not like it. The same.

She came out of the hold too fast, with a sound in her throat.

She was on her knees on the Fourth Fathom floor, her bare hand flat on the plinth. Above her Aske reached out of the salt toward nothing.

She had carried the rest for weeks, figure by figure, and had told herself each one could be carried another way. She went down them now with the hand under her tongue, and none of them could.

The Causeway at dawn on the sixth of Russet, and a woman in iron-grey coming up behind her from the city, bone-cold of hand, with salt-mud dried to crusts on her boots. There was no salt-mud on the Causeway. There was salt-mud at the end of Coldwater Street, by the old sea-wall, where the brine seeped up between the cobbles from the reservoir underneath.

The Warden's Gallery, and tea. *The Stays have been kind this season. Kinder than they've any right to be.* Thirty parts in a hundred.

The skiff on the White in Fallow, Orsolya easy at the rail. *How's your brother? Still at the Cold House?* Hester had never told her which brinery. Orsolya's da, nineteen years in the Cold House, explained the street. It had never explained how she knew Tib was in it.

The sixteenth of Russet, the tenth bell, the cold corridor outside Cell 3-17. Iron wheels on the turn below; Hester at the rail; a small blue lantern and a trolley under canvas, and two or three people in grey wool, standing still. Waiting, she saw now. Waiting for her to go.

*Edwin handles the residue contracts; it's all above board, Conclave-approved.*

*Oh, Corvin. Oh, you daft man.*

Hester knelt by the plinth with her hand on the cold salt, and the hum came up through the floor into her knees, and she did not get up for a long time.

* * *

She went back to Callow on the twenty-eighth.

She had not slept much in between. She had done a Choir shift in the east gallery and felt the cold old stillness come up the line from the east, and had held anyway, because the stay needed holding and there were twenty people in the line whose lives depended on it. Orsolya had walked the line in her iron-grey, a hand on a shoulder here, a word there. *Steady, chick. That's it.* She had put her hand on Hester's shoulder. Hester had not moved. When Orsolya walked on she had found that she was shaking, and had told the chorister beside her it was lag.

She had gone to Crisp's release hour at the Pans as well, because it was on the board and missing it would be noticed. Crisp walked the rim with her hands behind her back. "You are strangling it, Vail. Again." Hester kept a drop and closed on it, and closed on the next. On the third the drop went soft along one edge, a hair's breadth of water, before her hand clamped. Crisp stopped behind her. "Better," she said, and walked on, which from Crisp was a medal.

There was one thing left. He had said it to her in Russet, and she had heard it as an old man's kindness: *The dead bloom. A month or so. Whatever the grade.* She had been carrying it for two days like a stone in her shoe, not looking at it, because looking at it would mean hoping.

She sat on the stool at the end of Callow's desk. It was the fifth bell again.

"Under-Archivist," she said. "I need to know something about Hush grain."

She waited.

"The living keep clear and the dead keep grey. The Chief Proctor told me, the morning they found Reader Aske, that Hush grain's different. That it keeps everything clear, so the clear casing meant nothing. And I took his word, because I'd never had Hush grain in my mouth. You told me otherwise, and I didn't listen. Is it true?"

She counted to sixty, and past it. She watched his face, which did not move, and his hands, which did not move.

At a hundred and twenty his eyes came round to the corner of the desk.

"Hush grain," said Callow, "keeps clear."

Her heart went down like a dropped weight.

He breathed. One breath. It took a long time.

"Quickly."

She waited.

"Then it blooms," said Callow, "like any other."

The lamp gave off its old summer warmth.

"How long?"

Another breath.

"A month."

She did not move. She did not trust anything she might do if she did.

"He's been there since the sixteenth of Russet," she said. "Two months and more. I was in the Quiet Rooms three nights ago with a lantern. His casing is as clear as the day it was poured."

Callow said nothing. Then, so slowly that she was not sure until it was done, one of his hands turned over on the oak, palm up, and lay open there in the lamplight.

She looked at it, and understood that it was not helplessness. It was a release, as Crisp taught it at the Pans to first-years who could not do it, as Orsolya had taught it on the White over a man-shaped casing hanging in the mouth of a sink. *Don't push it out, chick. Open your hand.*

The living keep clear.

* * *

She went down to the Quiet Rooms again that evening, in the Lantern Hour, when the whole Halcyon was at the Long Table under the poured noon light and the Fourth Fathom was empty.

She took no lantern. There was light enough from the Well, where it passed through the Fourth on its way down to nowhere: gold and borrowed, a summer noon from some year she did not know, falling in a long column through the dark and making the casings along the gallery glitter like ice on a pond.

Aske reached out of the light toward her. His spectacles were full of gold.

She stood with her hands in the sleeves of her gown, against the cold.

"You daft man," she said.

He did not answer. He was not dead. He was between one moment and the next, and had been for ten weeks. In the moment he was in, he had his arm out to stop a woman he had known for thirty years from doing what she was doing. And she had a dish of live brine in her left hand, and had turned her wrist back on itself, and poured.

"I tasted it," Hester told him. "Like you said."

It was not so different, she found, from the fourth row of the Cold House. You stood beside a casing and told the person in it what you knew. You did not expect anything back. She had not said the name aloud to anyone, not even to Teo.

"It's Orsolya," she said.

The words sat in the cold air. The Well-light poured past. Far above, faint through two fathoms of salt, the Long Table was laughing and scraping its benches, six hundred people at their supper.

"It's Orsolya," Hester said again, as though she were writing it in a ledger in ink, so that it would be there in the morning. "And you're alive."
