# Chapter 19

## The Notebook

> The archive is a keeping like any other, and like any other it is owed.
>
> — *Wynn Callow, Under-Archivist, the only sentence he is known to have published (*Proceedings of the Brinestacks*, 851 Y.E.)*

Wynn Callow had been sitting at his desk in the Brinestacks for longer than most of the Readers had been alive. Newcomers took him for a piece of the furniture, and so did some Readers who had been at the Halcyon for twenty years.

He sat at the foot of the Second Fathom's main stair, behind a desk of black oak worn pale where his forearms rested. He was a small man in a grey gown so old that the wool had gone the colour of ash. His hair was white and fine and stood out a little from his skull, as a dandelion's does. His hands lay on the desk in front of him, one over the other. They did not move. Nor did his face. He had the Settle so deep in him that he breathed perhaps four times a minute, and an aspirant who did not know better could stand at the desk asking for a catalogue number for a full minute before deciding there was nobody there.

Hester had stood at that desk on her first day and decided exactly that. Teo had told her later that everyone did.

"Corvin sat there too." He had said that to them in Russet, as they were leaving, out of nowhere, the only sentence she had ever heard him speak. Hester had taken it at the time for an old man's remark about a dead colleague. She had not thought about it again until the night after Bett Linnow died. She had been lying awake in R-41 with her eyes open, listening to Teo breathe on the other side of the cell, and the sentence had come back to her with a weight it had not had before. *Sat there.* Not *read there*. Not *came here*. Sat, as you sit at someone's elbow when you want something from them and know it will take time.

So on the twenty-fifth of Cinder, at the third bell of the afternoon, when she should have been in the Black Bench taking down Sorle's lecture on the decay curves of kept light, she took a stool from the reading room, set it at the end of Callow's desk, and sat down.

He did not look at her. She had not expected him to.

"Under-Archivist," she said. "I'm Hester Vail. Reader Aske's privilege. Reader Sorle's Bench, now." She waited. "You said Corvin sat here too."

Nothing. On the stair behind her, two second-years went up arguing about the Assay, their voices bright and hard on the salt. A porter wheeled a cart of returned tablets past, the iron wheels grinding. The lamp on Callow's desk was a kept-light lamp, an old one, its glass yellowed. It gave off the faint unchanging warmth of a summer afternoon that had been over for decades.

She did not say anything else. She folded her hands in her lap. She had learned patience on a customs stool, waiting for scales to settle, and she had learned it again in the Choir, where you held until the line was steady and not a breath before. She let her breathing slow, not into the hold, only toward it.

After a time Callow's eyes moved. They did not move far. They came round from the middle distance to the corner of the desk, where her hands were, and rested there.

The fourth bell rang somewhere above. She heard the Lantern Hour preparations begin in the First Fathom, the clatter of the Long Table being laid, very faint through the salt. The light from the reading room changed as somebody turned down a lamp. Her left foot went to sleep. She did not move it.

"He brought," said Callow.

His voice was dry and soft and astonishingly clear, like paper being unfolded. Then it stopped. She waited. She counted, as she would have counted a scale's swing, and got to forty.

"Something back."

"Back where?"

Fifty. Sixty. Somebody laughed in the reading room and was shushed.

"Where it was not," said Callow, "to be looked for."

"Where's that?"

He did not answer for so long that she thought he had gone. His hands lay on the oak. His breathing was a slow tide. Then, with a deliberation she would remember for the rest of her life, Wynn Callow began to stand up.

It took him nearly a quarter of an hour. She had seen Settled people move before; she had seen Ama Tessaly rise from a Choir bench like a woman lifting a full bucket. This was something else. Callow did not so much stand as unfold, a joint at a time, each movement starting so slowly that she could not see it begin and only knew it had happened because his shoulder was higher than it had been. She did not help him. She sensed that he would not want it, and that it would, in any case, have been like helping a glacier. When he was upright he stood a while, as if to let his blood catch up with him. Then he reached into the breast of his gown and drew out a key on a black string, a long brass key with a halcyon on its bow, and held it in his palm, and looked at it.

"Where the founders," he said, "sleep."

* * *

The Founders' Gallery was at the far end of the Second Fathom, beyond the last reading well, through a door of lattice-grown salt that had a brass grille in it and a sign: RESTRICTED. BY LEAVE OF THE WARDEN OF THE STACKS. Hester had passed it a dozen times. It took Callow twenty minutes to walk there from his desk. She walked beside him at his pace, which was a pace she had never walked at in her life. Aspirants coming the other way stopped and stared, and then hurried, and then stopped again at the corners to look back. Nobody had seen Callow walk. There was a rumour in the Rookery that he had been carried to his desk in 860 and never moved since.

The key turned with a sound like a cough.

Inside was cold and dry and dark. Callow did not light anything. Hester found a kept-light lantern hanging on a hook by the door and dissolved its cake in the little reservoir at its base, and a spring morning came up yellow-white around them and showed her a long, low, vaulted room. It was lined to the roof with drawers. Thousands of them, small and shallow, in black wood, each with a brass label-holder and a label in faded copperplate.

*230 Y.E. — Fire-salvage — Founders' Tablets — I.*

*230 Y.E. — Fire-salvage — Founders' Tablets — II.*

On and on along the wall, into the dark. She pulled one at random. Inside, on grey felt, lay salt tablets the size of a hand, black. They were not painted black. They were burnt black, crazed and warped, their edges slumped like candle-wax. Some had split. One had fused to its neighbour in a single lump. She could see, here and there under the char, the ghost of a cut letter.

"What happened to them?"

Callow did not answer that. He had already started down the gallery, slowly, his hand out, trailing his fingertips along the drawer-fronts as he went. She followed with the lantern. He passed I and V and X, and at XIV he stopped. He stood there so long that she thought he had forgotten why he had come. Then his hand came up, a fraction at a time, and rested on the brass pull.

"I'll do it," she said.

He took his hand away.

She pulled the drawer. It was full, like the others, of char. She pulled harder and it came out of its runners altogether and was heavier than she expected, and she had to set it on the floor. Behind it, in the space where the back of the drawer should have met the back of the cabinet, there was a gap of perhaps four inches. In the gap, wedged upright, was a bundle in brown oilcloth tied with tarred string.

She lifted it out. It was the size and weight of three thick books. Something small and hard shifted inside it as she turned it over. She sat back on her heels with the bundle on her knees and untied the string, because there was no point now in pretending she would not.

Three notebooks, cheap stationer's notebooks with marbled covers, the kind they sold in the Glassmarket for four flecks. Their pages were swollen from use. Ink showed through the paper, black and blue, in a small fast sloping hand. Tucked between the second and the third was a horn case no bigger than a thumb. She knew it before she opened it. Inside, on green baize, lay a tasting spoon of fine white metal, finer than any on her old rack at the Customs House.

*May I?*

She closed the case. She found that she had to sit there for a little while before she could do anything else.

When she looked up, Callow was watching her. Not the middle distance: her. His eyes were pale and very clear in the spring lantern-light, and there was something in them she could not read, because nobody on earth could have read Wynn Callow's face. It might have been grief. It might have been satisfaction. It might have been both, held still so long they had become the same thing.

"He said," Callow said, "if anyone came."

She waited.

"Who sat."

* * *

Teo did not go to bed that night. Neither did she.

They sat in R-41 with the door wedged shut and the candle on the floor between them, and the three notebooks open on Teo's blanket, and outside the window the White lay silver under a half moon all the way to Ambry, where a few lamps still showed on the hill. Teo had his old Tider shawl round his shoulders and a pencil behind his ear and another in his hand, and a slate on his knee. He had eaten, by her count, four slices of saltcake since the tenth bell, without noticing any of them.

"It's not a cipher," he had said, at the start, turning the pages. "I want to be clear about that before you hope for anything dramatic. He's not hiding anything from anybody clever. It's brine-script shorthand. Cutters' shorthand. The Abbey used it for the tablet copies, so you could cut a page of scripture into salt in an afternoon instead of a week. Nobody uses it any more except old copyists and, apparently, Reader Aske, who uses it badly, with personal abbreviations, and in two colours, for reasons known only to God." He had squinted. "Blue is grain. Black is people, I think. Give me an hour."

It had taken him six.

He read aloud as he went, in pieces, in a murmur. Sometimes he stopped for twenty minutes over a single line and swore at it softly in the vocabulary of a man who had spent nine years in a religious house. Hester sat against the wall with her knees up and listened, and from time to time she got up and refilled his water, and once she went down to the Rookery's well-room and came back with a jug of halcyon tea that had gone cold by the time she reached the cell.

The first notebook began in Thaw of 902. It was grain. Page after page of grain, in blue: samples taken from trade, from the Exchequer Yard, from the dry harbour. Grades. Depths. Every few pages, a sample that did not belong. A crate of curing salt from a Ferrers yard that tasted of the Fifth. Another. *Warm. Bead. Spat in the basin.* By midsummer he had stopped being surprised.

The second notebook was people. Black ink. It began with Dunmore.

*E.D. — Edwin Dunmore, clerk to the Warden's office. Handles the residue contracts. Residue = quickened grain cut out of the stays when the stays are re-laid. Should be dissolved in the Fifth and the brine pumped to the sinks. Is not. Is boxed. Boxes go up the Fifth lift after the eleventh bell, two nights a week, carried by aspirants paid two sols a run.* Teo looked up. "That's Saskia."

"I know."

"He knew it was Saskia." Teo turned the page. "No. He knew it was *aspirants*. He doesn't name her." He read on. *Boxes go to the dry harbour. Ferrers & Lisk freighter. East to Quell, a Ferrers warehouse there, relabelled Shelf — Quell certificate forged or bought — then back west to the Ferrers Yard and sold as curing grain to the Hask kept-fish trade. Hask buyers complain their fish spit when the keep is broken. Nobody listens to Hask buyers.* And under that, in a different pen: *W.L.F. per E.D. on the ladings. Warden of the Lower Fathoms, per Dunmore. Bold as brass. He signs as the office because the office has signed residue contracts for two hundred years. Nobody reads them.*

"You knew that," Teo said.

"I knew that." She had known it since Lucan came back from the Upper Ward with the contract in his coat. She had known it and had built a small, neat, clerkish theory around it, with Dunmore at the centre: a bland man who sold what he was told to destroy and kept the money, and a Warden who signed what her clerk put in front of her, as Wardens did. She had found the theory in the Choir and in the Rookery and on the Stair for three weeks. She had needed it.

Teo turned the page. The ink changed back to blue.

*4 Russet. Miss Vail. Taste and weight. Graded my twelve. Named nine as twice-kept. Heard a cry in the Wren grain, did not know it. I did not tell her. God forgive me, I want to see her face when she hears it in the Stacks.*

Teo stopped. He looked at her over the candle. She looked at the window.

"Go on," she said.

*5 Russet. Went to the Cold House on Coldwater St, the day before the Line. Sister's brother in 211. Agathe Linden showed me the ward. 14 weeping. All east. Taste: quick. Tasted it off the plinth myself — the casing salt that has run off. Quick, and under it something stiller than I have ever had in my mouth. Old keepings get still. These are being bled.*

*Harl St Brinery — 0. Varenne Private — 0. Checked the Exchequer returns. Charity only. Of course charity only. Nobody audits charity.*

*A draw? Still runs to quick. Sorle's law. It wants a brine contact. A line. Cold House stands on the old reservoir — Cistern?? The founders' lines. Bricked in 400. Bricked by whom?*

"That's the sheet," Hester said. "The one from his blotter. That's where it came from."

"He copied it out," Teo said. "Or the sheet was the draft." He was quiet a moment, reading ahead with his lips moving. "Hester. He went into the Cistern."

*12 Russet. Under the Cold House by the cellar. Agathe's key. The old main line is open. Brass sleeves, new, Lattice marks — someone in the Yards made these this year. Live brine in the sleeve. Still as death. Runs east.*

*13 Russet. Walked the plans. The main line runs under the White, two miles, and comes up in the Fifth Fathom at the Old Tap. Founders' brass. I have passed it a hundred times. The stay-lines for the east gallery are fed from it. The stays have been holding beautifully all summer, and I am a fool.*

*14 Russet. Measured. The east stays draw 30 parts in 100 of their stillness from the Old Tap. Thirty. Without it they would have failed in Seedmonth. Without it the Hush would be in the Fifth by now.*

Teo put down the pencil.

"Thirty parts in a hundred," he said. "From the Cold House."

Hester did not say anything. In the Choir, on the night of the Silent Supper, holding in the line with her hands on the wet brass and the sea roaring in the walls, she had felt it come up the stay-line from the east: a cold, old, vast stillness that was not the Choir's and was not hers. She had known its taste. She had stood on the Stair afterwards with a stick of chalk in her hand and written Ama Tessaly's name, and had not let herself finish the thought. She let herself finish it now. *The stays are fed by the sleepers.* Tib had been in the line that night. Tib had been holding the east gallery up with her.

"Is there more?" she said.

"There's the last page."

"Read it."

Teo read it. He read it slowly, in the voice he used for scripture when he did not want to mock it, and she listened with her eyes shut.

*15 Russet. Told Miss V. tomorrow at ten. Showed her the beating bead. She went white. She has the tongue for this, God help her, and she has a brother in the line.*

*I know whose office this is. I have known since the 13th and I have pretended not to, because I have known her thirty years and she taught me to hold in the Choir when I was nineteen and too frightened to breathe. She is the best of us. I think she has done this because she is the best of us.*

*She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held. The pressure wants a road, not a lid. Vent? Let a little of it go somewhere it can do no harm, a little at a time. Need Sorle's arrears for the shaft — he'll crow for a decade, let him. I'll ask O. tomorrow, before I tell Gage. I owe her that.*

*If anything — tell Hester to taste it.*

The candle had burned down to a stub. Outside the window the moon had gone behind the Lantern, and the mirrors at its head made a thin black bar across the White. Somewhere below them in the Rookery a door closed.

"She," Teo said, very quietly.

"I heard."

"Dunmore's not a she."

"I heard, Teo."

He closed the notebook. He sat with his hand flat on its marbled cover, the way Aske had sat with his hands flat on her slate on the night they met. He did not say the name. He was too kind to say the name, and she loved him for it and hated him for it, both at once.

"*Ask O.*," he said instead. "We thought it was the Order. Or Saskia."

"We thought a lot of things."

"Hester—"

"Not yet." She stood up. Her knees had gone stiff from the wall and her feet were cold and she was very thirsty, the Halcyon thirst, as though she had been holding. She had not been holding. "He said taste it. I haven't tasted it. Not properly. Not knowing what I'm looking for." She took her gown from its peg. "If I'm going to think it, I'll think it with my tongue. Not with a notebook."

"It's the fifth bell of the night."

"Then nobody will be in the Quiet Rooms."

He looked at her a long moment over the guttering candle, a big soft-faced man in an old grey shawl, with crumbs on his chin.

"I'll be here," he said.

* * *

The Fourth Fathom at the fifth bell of the night was dark and very cold. There was no sound but the hum, the long low pressure from below that she had felt in her teeth on her first day and had long since stopped noticing. She noticed it now. She went down the Stair with a hooded lantern, past the chalk-names. She did not read them. She did not need to; she knew where Ama's was.

The Quiet Rooms were not locked. Nobody locked them. Who would steal a keeping?

Aske's casing stood where the porters had set it in Russet, on a plinth of plain salt between a kept girl of the Great Bloom and a Reader of Lattice who had been kept in 840 with a falling beam across his back. Hester did not look at either of them. She uncovered the lantern and set it on the floor at the plinth's foot and stood looking up at him.

He had not changed. That was what keeping meant, and still it went through her. His arm was out, reaching, the ink-stained fingers spread. His mouth was open on whatever he had been about to say. His spectacles were pushed up on his nose and the lantern-light swam in them. Through the casing, which was as clear as clean water, she could see the frayed cuff of his shirt. She could see a fleck of salt-mud on his gown, at the knee.

The casing fitted him close at every point. Except that it did not. She saw it now, as she had seen it in his Cell on the morning they found him and had let Gage talk her out of seeing it. A self-keeping grew outward from the worker's skin. The worker went into the hold with brine on their own body and the salt bloomed out from them, layer on layer, like the rings of a tree. Such a casing was thinnest at the skin and thickest at the edges, and its grain ran outward. This one was thickest close to his body and thinnest at the edges. It had been poured over him from above, by someone standing in front of him, and it had run down and set from the outside in.

She had known that on the seventeenth of Russet. She had known it, and Gage had said *self-experiment*, and Orsolya had come in with her cold hand and her voice saying *oh, you daft man*, and Hester had let the knowing go where the Tib-knowing went. She had walked round it. Quick and practised.

She knelt at the plinth's foot. She took off her right glove, as she had taken it off in the Cold House on the first of Russet, and put the tip of her smallest finger to the casing where it met the plinth. The salt was dry and very cold. She touched her finger to her tongue.

The weight came down. Hush grain: the door closing, the white, the vast pressing on the far side of her face. She had expected that. She went into the hold.

It came easily now. Three months ago it had been something she found by accident on a customs stool. Now it was something she could put on like a coat: breath, heart, hands, the muscles of the face, all of it slowing and settling until she was nearly as still as the salt on her tongue. Then the taste opened, and she read.

She did not try to read Aske. She did not want to hear what he had been saying when the brine went over him. She went past the moment and into the grain itself, into the way it had set, into the hand of whoever had set it. Every worker leaves a hand. She had known that before Crisp ever said it, from a hundred casings she had tasted off customs crates, keepings of fish and fruit and medicine laid down by a hundred different brinewrights. Some were tidy, some were careless. Some were laminar, like the pages of a book; some grew in fans. Her own was squared and dense, an assayer's hand, every grain set where it was put. Crisp's was laminar. Aske's own, she had learned in her first week, from his samples, was fine and radial, like the spokes of a wheel.

This was none of those.

The grain turned. Under the weight and the white she felt it as a slow corkscrew of set salt, layer winding on layer, every one of them turning the same way. It turned from the right hand toward the left, widdershins, against the sun, as an old woman stirs a pot with her left wrist turned back on itself. It had a twist at the end of each turn, a small hard counter-flick, like the snap of a wrist.

She knew it. She had watched it happen in a candle flame.

*Every worker leaves a hand, chick.* Orsolya in the Warden's Gallery in Fallow, with a tallow candle burning on the table between them and a dish of live brine. Showing her how to keep a flame, because Hester had asked. The brine poured over the wick in one smooth movement of the left wrist, the flame going still, a little yellow tongue in a bead of clear salt. Orsolya turning the bead in the lamplight so Hester could see the grain inside it winding round and round. *Mine's a corkscrew. My mam said I came out twisting.* And laughing, and Hester laughing with her, and something at the back of Hester's mind giving a small hard tug, like a fish on a line, which she had not let herself feel.

She came out of the hold too fast, as she had come out of it on the customs stool, with a sound in her throat.

She was on her knees on the Fourth Fathom floor in the lantern-light. Her bare hand was flat on the plinth. Above her Aske reached out of the salt toward nothing, mouth open.

It came then. It came all at once, the way a stack of crates comes down when you pull the wrong one: everything she had seen and set aside, everything she had walked round, in the order she had walked round it.

The Causeway at dawn on the sixth of Russet, and a woman in iron-grey coming toward her from the city, not from the Halcyon. She had been tired and cold-handed, with salt-mud on her boots, and she had greeted Hester in a Lowside voice. *You're Aske's privilege. Well done, chick.* Salt-mud. There was no salt-mud on the Causeway; the Causeway was stone. There was salt-mud in Coldwater Street, at the end by the sea-wall, where the brine seeped up between the cobbles from the old reservoir underneath.

The Warden's Gallery in Russet, with tea, and the first descent. Orsolya tired at her table: *The Stays have been kind this season. Kinder than they've any right to be.* Kind. Thirty parts in a hundred.

The skiff on the White in Fallow, the hiss of the runners, the glare, Orsolya at the tiller, easy, chatting. *How's your brother? Still at the Cold House?* Hester had never told her which brinery. She had told her there was a brother, kept, and nothing else, because she told nobody anything else. She had thought, *Aske must have said*, and it had been perfectly reasonable, and she had stepped round it.

The sixteenth of Russet, the tenth bell, the cold corridor on the Third Fathom outside Cell 3-17, waiting in the dark, annoyed. And on the Stair below her, coming up from the Fourth, from the Fifth, the sound of something heavy on wheels. Iron wheels grinding on salt, slow, two people's breathing, a scrape at each turn. She had thought, *porters, with brine casks*. Who brings brine casks up from the Fifth at the tenth bell of the night?

*Edwin handles the residue contracts; it's all above board, Conclave-approved.*

*Oh, Corvin. Oh, you daft man.*

Hester knelt by the plinth with her hand on the cold salt, and the hum came up through the floor into her knees, and she did not get up for a long time.

* * *

She went back to Callow on the twenty-eighth.

She had not slept much in the two days between. She had gone to lectures and taken notes she could not afterwards read. She had done a Choir shift in the east gallery with her hands on the brass. She had held, and felt the cold old stillness come up the line from the east, and had held anyway, because the stay needed holding and there were twenty other people in the line whose lives depended on it. She had watched Orsolya walk along the line in her iron-grey, a hand on a shoulder here, a word there. *Steady, chick. That's it.* Orsolya had put her hand on Hester's shoulder. Hester had not moved. She had felt the cold of the hand through the wool, and she had not moved, and when Orsolya walked on she had found that she was shaking, and had told the chorister beside her it was lag.

She had not told Teo what she had tasted. She had not needed to. He had looked at her face when she came back up to R-41 at the sixth bell of the morning, and put his arms round her, and she had let him, which she did not do.

There was one thing left. She had been carrying it for two days as she might carry a stone in her shoe, not looking at it, because looking at it would mean hoping, and she did not trust herself to hope.

She sat on the stool at the end of Callow's desk. It was the fourth bell again. He did not look at her. She did not care.

"Under-Archivist," she said. "I need to know something about Hush grain."

She waited.

"You know the rule. The living keep clear and the dead keep grey. Death-bloom. Any brinery keeper can tell you. A casing kept round a body that's dead, or dying past saving, goes grey inside a few days, like breath on a mirror." She heard her own voice, level and dry, an assayer giving a grade. "The Chief Proctor told me, the morning they found Reader Aske, that Hush grain is different. He said Hush grain keeps everything clear. That's why the casing was clear: because it's Hush. And I took his word, because I'd never had Hush grain in my mouth till Russet and I didn't know any better."

Callow's hands lay one over the other on the oak.

"Is it true?" she said.

She counted. She got to sixty, and past it. On the stair behind her, a Reader went down with a stack of slates, and a porter came up with an empty cart, the wheels grinding. She did not look round. She watched his face, which did not move, and his hands, which did not move. She thought he had not heard, or did not intend to answer, or had gone wherever the deeply Settled went in the long gaps between their breaths.

At a hundred and twenty his eyes came round to the corner of the desk.

"Hush grain," said Callow, "keeps clear."

Her heart went down like a dropped weight.

He breathed. One breath. It took a long time.

"Quickly."

She waited.

"Then it blooms," said Callow, "like any other."

The lamp on his desk gave off its old summer warmth. Somebody in the reading room turned a page.

"How long?"

Another breath.

"A month."

She sat very still. She did not go into the hold. She simply did not move, because she did not trust anything she might do if she did.

"He's been there since the sixteenth of Russet," she said. "Two months. More. I was in the Quiet Rooms two nights ago with a lantern. His casing is as clear as the day it was poured."

Callow said nothing. His eyes had not left the corner of the desk. Then, so slowly that she was not sure until it was done, one of his hands turned over on the oak, palm up, and lay open there in the lamplight.

She looked at it. Then she understood that it was not a gesture of helplessness. It was the hold being let go. It was a release, as Crisp taught it at the Pans to first-years who could not do it, as Orsolya had taught it on the White over a man-shaped casing hanging in the mouth of a sink. *Don't push it out, chick. Open your hand.*

The living keep clear.

* * *

She went down to the Quiet Rooms again that evening, in the Lantern Hour, when the whole Halcyon was at the Long Table under the poured noon light, and the Fourth Fathom was empty.

She did not take a lantern this time. There was light enough from the shaft of the Well, where it passed through the Fourth on its way down to nowhere. It was gold and borrowed, a summer noon from some year she did not know, falling in a long column through the dark and making the casings along the gallery glitter like ice on a pond. She walked past the girl of the Bloom and the Reader with the beam on his back and stopped at the plain salt plinth.

Aske reached out of the light toward her. His mouth was open. His spectacles were full of gold.

She stood with her hands in the sleeves of her gown, against the cold.

"You daft man," she said.

He did not answer. He was not dead. He was between one moment and the next, and he had been there since the sixteenth of Russet, ten weeks. In the moment he was in, he had his arm out to stop a woman he had known for thirty years from doing what she was doing. And she had a dish of live brine in her left hand, and had turned her wrist back on itself, and poured.

"I tasted it," Hester told him. "Like you said."

It was not so different, she found, from the fourth row of the Cold House. You stood beside a casing and you told the person in it what you knew. You did not expect anything back. It was the telling that mattered, or it was the only thing you had.

"It's Orsolya," she said.

The words sat in the cold air of the Quiet Room. The Well-light poured past. Far above her, faint through two fathoms of salt, the Long Table was laughing and scraping its benches, six hundred people at their supper.

"It's Orsolya," Hester said again, as though she were writing it in a ledger in ink, so that it would be there in the morning. "And you're alive."
