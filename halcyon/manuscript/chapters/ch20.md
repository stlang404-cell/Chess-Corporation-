# Chapter 20

## The Warden

> I have buried eleven of mine. I'll not bury more.
>
> — *Orsolya Thane, Warden of the Lower Fathoms, address to the Stay Choir on the Silent Supper, 899 Y.E.*

She told nobody.

It did not feel like a decision. It felt like reasoning, and she reasoned it through in the cold of R-41 with Teo asleep across the cell and the three notebooks wrapped in their oilcloth under her mattress. But it had been settled before she began, somewhere at the back of her, in the place where she had settled the night at the Cold House desk eleven years ago without consulting anyone, least of all herself.

If she told Sorle, Sorle would go to the Conclave with his slates and his thirty-two years of being laughed at, and the Conclave would listen to him exactly as it had in 871. Vey would thank him for his diligence and move to the price of lamp oil, and somebody would come to the Brinestacks with a key, and the notebooks would be fire-salvage for good.

If she told Lucan, Lucan would go to the Assize. It would be the word of a Lowside aspirant and a boy whose father owned the freighter against the Warden of the Lower Fathoms. And if by some miracle they were believed, the first thing the Assize would do was order the draw cut. She had read the fourteenth of Russet in Aske's hand. *Without it the Hush would be in the Fifth by now.*

If she told Saskia, Saskia would want to know what it was worth, and would be right to want to know, and Hester had no answer.

If she told Teo, Teo would come with her.

And under the reasons, which were good reasons, there was a thing she did not look at directly. *I'll ask O. tomorrow, before I tell Gage. I owe her that.* Aske had owed Orsolya thirty years and a Choir bench at nineteen. Hester owed her twelve weeks. A blanket on the Choir floor after her first stay, a cup of tea in the Warden's Gallery, a voice on the White saying *open your hand*. The Lowside in her voice, the only Lowside voice under the Shelf except Hester's own. The plain relief, on the Causeway in the pink dawn of the sixth of Russet, of a woman who has walked a long way among strangers and hears someone from her own street.

Aske had gone to ask, and had been standing in the Quiet Rooms with his arm out ever since.

She knew that. She went anyway.

She wrote two lines on a page of her ledger, folded it, and slid it under Teo's pillow without waking him. *If I'm not back by the first bell, take the notebooks to Sorle. Under my mattress. H.* Then she put on her gown and her gloves and went down.

* * *

It was the second of Rime, the eleventh bell. The Assay was nineteen days off and the Rookery had gone quiet early all week, aspirants in their cells with their slates and their dishes of practice grain. On the Second Fathom the chalk-names went past her in the lantern-light, and among them, in her own seizure hand, AMA TESSALY, CHOIR, 903. She did not stop.

The Fifth Fathom was never asleep. A half-Choir of twenty-two held the east gallery through the night in four-hour watches, and their singing came up the galleries faint and slow, like a congregation heard through a church wall. She felt it in the soles of her feet as she came off the last turn of the Stair. The Warden's Gallery opened off to the left: a long room cut into the salt, with a row of brass gauges along one wall, needles trembling behind glass, each labelled for a stay; a table, a stove, a kettle; shelves of returns bound in iron-grey cloth; a single kept-light lamp.

Dunmore was coming out as she came in, a stack of ledgers under his arm and ink on his cuffs, with the bland, faintly put-upon courtesy of a clerk who has been kept late.

"Miss Vail. The Warden's in."

"Ta."

He went past her toward the lift. She heard the cage rattle and the brine-counterweight gurgle, and the cage go up. She had wondered, once, in Russet, what kind of man stood beside a dray at night with a lantern and a paper while other men lifted. She thought she knew now. A man who did what he was told and went home at a reasonable hour.

Orsolya was at the table with her back to the door and a shawl round her shoulders, writing.

"Is that you, chick? There's tea in the pot. It's stewed. I'll make fresh."

"Don't."

Orsolya's pen stopped.

She put it down. She sat a moment with her hands flat on the table either side of the page. Then she turned round on her chair, slowly, as Settled people turned, and looked at Hester in the doorway. She looked at her face for a long time, and Hester let her.

"Well," Orsolya said. "Sit down before you fall down."

* * *

Hester did not sit. She stood on the other side of the table, under the lamp, and told it the way she would have told a seizure to the Customs Board: in order, without adjectives, evidence first and conclusion last. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to, because it was a voice she had used for twenty-one years to tell men in good coats that their grain was short.

The *Constant Wren* and *W.L.F. per E.D.* The fourteen casings weeping east. The founders' line under Coldwater Street, sleeved in new Lattice brass, running to the Old Tap. Pell's ten sols a month from a gentleman of the Warden's office. Thirty parts in a hundred. Aske's notebooks, in a drawer of fire-salvage where nobody would look. *She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held.*

"And his casing," Hester said. "Poured, not grown. The grain turns widdershins, with a flick at the end of every turn, like a wrist snapping back. I watched you keep a candle flame in Fallow. You showed me." She did not stop. "On the sixth of Russet you came toward me on the Causeway from the Ambry end with salt-mud on your boots. There's salt-mud at the end of Coldwater Street, where the old reservoir seeps. And on the White you asked me if my brother was still at the Cold House. I never told you which brinery. I never told anyone."

There was nothing more. She had laid it all on the table, and it lay there.

"It's you," she said. "It's been you all along."

The kettle on the stove ticked as it cooled.

Orsolya turned her left hand over, palm up, and looked at the wrist as though she had never seen it before.

"My mam always said I'd get caught by it," she said. "That wrist. Said I'd never pass for anybody's hand but my own." She almost smiled. "Corvin knew it too. That's what he said, at the Tap, before. *I'd know your hand anywhere, Orsolya.* Like it was a compliment."

"Tell me why."

"You know why."

"I want you to say it."

Orsolya looked up at her. In the lamplight her face was older than Hester had seen it: the white hair, the slack skin of the neck, the slow blue at the lips that every Choir worker carried. The eyes were the eyes Hester had liked on the Causeway, quick and grey and Lowside-shrewd. They were not ashamed. They were tired past shame.

"All right," Orsolya said. "But I'll not say it to you standing over me like a Proctor. Sit."

Hester sat.

* * *

Orsolya got up, slowly, and took down a ledger bound in iron-grey cloth and opened it on the table between them, turned so the columns faced Hester. Returns. Hester knew a returns ledger the way other women knew a hymn-book: stays by number down the left, dates across the top, in each square a figure in Orsolya's upright hand.

"That's 896," Orsolya said, putting a cold finger at the top of the page. "Four quickenings in the Fifth. Same as my first year as Warden, same as it had been for as long as anyone kept the returns. We'd stay them, and they'd quieten, and every few years one would break out again and we'd stay it again. Like weeding a garden." She turned the page. "897. Six." Another. "899. Nine." Another. "This summer, fourteen. And bigger. Hotter. Coming up through the Fifth from the Hush like water through a cellar floor, and I've had one break into the Fourth, you were there. Above the stays. That's never happened. Not in nine hundred years of returns."

She turned to the back of the ledger. There was a list of names, eleven of them, with dates, and at the bottom a twelfth, the ink still dark. *Ama Tessaly. 9 Cinder.*

"Them," Orsolya said. "That's what a stay costs, chick. You've held. You lend it and it doesn't all come back. Choir does twenty years if it's lucky; it's not lucky now. With fourteen pockets and twenty-two in a half-Choir, you hold double watches and you Settle double fast. Danny Horrocks was thirty-four. He sat down on the floor of the east gallery in Thaw of 901 and didn't get up. Leni Ash was twenty-nine. She had a baby." Her finger moved down the list without touching it. "I buried them. Me. I went to their mams."

"You could have—"

"I went to the Chancellor." Orsolya's voice did not rise. It went flat and plain. "Seedmonth of 901, with that ledger under my arm. I showed her the figures and the names. I asked for an inquiry. I asked for money for twice the Choir. I asked for the Delvers to be stopped cutting the Shelf pits any deeper, because every fathom they take off the top is a fathom of weight off the Hush. And she sat there, the old woman, still as a stone, and the Deputy did the talking. The Halcyon could not afford to be *seen* to have a problem in the deep. Not with the Exchequer renegotiating the Charter tithe. Not with the Lodge at Quell petitioning the Concord. *Manage it quietly, Warden. You've always managed. We have every confidence.*" She shut the ledger. "No inquiry. No money. No noise."

Hester thought of Aske in Quill Street, turning his cup: *the Deputy Chancellor thanked me for my diligence and moved to the next business.*

"So I managed," Orsolya said.

She sat down again and put her hands round the cold teapot, as if for warmth.

"I went to the Stacks looking for anything the founders had done about pressure in the deep. They built down here. They must have known. And I found the Cistern plans, the old ones, from before the Fire. Water Board had them filed as municipal brine supply." She laughed, a short dry sound. "Nobody pipes brine two miles under the White to fill Lowside's washtubs, chick. It's a stay-line. The biggest ever cut, from the old reservoir under Coldwater Street straight to the Old Tap. There's a halcyon cut on the valve-wheel. I've had my hand on it. They built the Cistern for this. To feed the stays from the city, when the stays ran short." She looked at Hester. "It was always meant."

"Meant to feed them with what?"

"With whatever was stillest. Whatever the city kept. Cellars of kept fish, in their day, I suppose. I don't know what the founders kept." She shrugged, a small heavy movement. "I know what we keep."

* * *

"Tell me about the sleepers," Hester said.

Her voice cracked on *sleepers*, like a pan-skin. She put both hands flat on the table and held them there until it passed.

"There's three hundred and four in that charity ward," Orsolya said. "You've walked the rows. Bloomers, mostly. Girls kept in childbed. Old folk kept with growths nobody's found a cure for in a hundred years. And the Coldbank men from 856, that the Guild paid to keep and stopped paying for in 870." She did not look away. "My da was one. I paid the rest of him myself. Nobody's coming back from that ward, chick. Not cured. Agathe knows it. Pell knows it, God knows. They're kept because somebody couldn't bear the bill of letting them go. And the families pay. Six sols a month, year on year. Ruth Linnow paid for Bett twenty years on a laundress's wage. That's the better part of fifteen hundred sols, for a lass who was never going to wake up and live. And when they can't pay they borrow, and when they can't borrow they sell the chairs. I've seen Lowside rooms with nothing in them but a bed and a Cold House receipt pinned to the wall. So have you."

"So you took them."

"I took what they had." Orsolya leaned forward. "Old keepings are the stillest things in the world. Sixty years in a casing, eighty, and the salt soaks up the life inside it till you could stay a quickening with a fingernail of it. Three hundred of them in a cold room on top of the founders' own line, costing their mams' furniture. And down here I've a Choir dying at thirty and a pocket in the east gallery that'll put the sea up the Stair the night it breaks." She opened her hands. "I didn't kill anybody, Hester. I let them go. Slowly. Usefully. Every one of them that's held up that gallery this summer has kept fifty of my Choir alive. They're holding the line so the others can sleep."

"Bett Linnow died gasping on a plinth," Hester said, "with her mother holding her hand."

"Bett Linnow was dying gasping in 883. That's when she was kept. I didn't do that to her. Somebody else did, by signing a paper. All I did was spend the twenty years she'd been given, that weren't doing her a blind bit of good."

"You didn't ask."

"Ask who?" Orsolya said. "Bett?"

The kettle ticked on the stove.

"I'll tell you about asking." Orsolya's voice had gone quiet and very plain. "Nineteen years I stood by my da on the first of the month. He was kept standing up, the way they found him, under the beam. When his casing wept and the brinewright said it'd not take another coat, I signed. I was thirty. I'd been paying since I was twenty-five, on a chorister's three sols, and I'd have paid till I died, and I signed. They took the beam off him in the bath and he came back with his chest stove in and the third gallery's dust in his mouth, and he looked at me, a woman of thirty with white coming in her hair, and called me by my mam's name. Then he died. It took about as long as it took your Bett." She turned the cold teapot a quarter-turn on the wood. "That's what nineteen years bought him. Not a day. Not an hour he could use. So don't tell me what I'm taking off them. I know to the fleck what I'm taking off them. It's nothing they'll ever have." She looked at Hester. "Nobody asked him, either. He'd have been hard put to answer, under the beam. Did your lad sign his own form, chick?"

Hester did not move. She found she could not. It was not the hold; it was the opposite of the hold, everything in her running too fast to be still and too fast to move. Orsolya watched her, not unkindly. She could not know. She could not possibly know. It was only that she was Lowside, and so was Hester, and on Coldwater Street nobody signed their own form.

"No," Hester said at last.

"No. Nobody does." Orsolya sat back. "So don't tell me about asking."

* * *

"Reader Aske," Hester said.

That one landed. Orsolya's face did not change, but her hands came off the teapot and lay in her lap, and for a while she said nothing at all.

"He sent word he wanted to see me at the Tap," she said. "The sixteenth, the tenth bell. I knew what it meant. He'd got there first. He had his hands on the wheel." She swallowed. "He'd found it all, you'll have read it. And he'd made up his mind, Corvin, all at once, like a man jumping off a wall. He was going to shut the Tap. Then and there. And go up to Gage in the morning."

"And you stopped him."

"If he'd shut that Tap the line would've backflowed. Still runs to quick. With the Tap shut and the line full, the east gallery stays would have bled their still back down the line toward the Cold House, and the east gallery would have woken inside the hour. Twenty-two of my Choir were on watch. I told him. I begged him. He kept saying *there's another way, Orsolya, there's always another way*, like he was explaining a sum. And the wheel was turning." Her left hand moved in her lap, a small twist back on itself, and stopped. "I had the dish in my hand. I'd brought it down to top the stay. Hush brine, from the line. And I—"

She stopped.

"I kept him," she said. "God help me. I didn't kill him. I kept him. It's the only thing I know how to do."

Hester's throat had closed. She waited for it to open.

"You knew," she said. "When Gage was saying *kept beyond return*. You knew he was alive."

"Of course I knew. What d'you take me for?" It was the first anger she had shown. "Edwin and I brought him up on a cask-trolley at the half bell. It took us both; a casing weighs. We put him in his own Cell, and I put the dish in his basin so Gage would have something to write down, and I went to my bed and didn't sleep. And in the morning I came up and stood there and cried over him, and every tear of it was true." Her mouth worked. "You can't return a Hush keeping fast. The arrears would stop his heart. I'll bring him back myself when the Hush is quiet. Slow, in a warm bath, the way Physic does the old ones. I'll sit with him every hour of it. I kept him till it's over, chick. That's all."

"And when is it over?"

Orsolya did not answer that.

* * *

They sat across the table for a time Hester did not measure. The half-Choir's singing came and went down the gallery. A needle on the wall dipped and steadied.

"What are you going to do?" Orsolya said at last.

"I don't know."

"You'll go to Gage."

"Gage lost the rod I brought him."

"So he did. Vey had a word. Vey doesn't want anybody looking at the deep." She considered Hester with the quick shrewd Lowside look she had given her on the Causeway. "You'll go to Benedek, then. He's waited thirty years to see me in the dock; let him have his day. But you go to Benedek, or Gage, or the Assize, and somebody shuts that Tap, and the east gallery wakes. And then it's my Choir on the floor, and then it's the Fifth, and then it's the Stair, and then it's the Rookery with three hundred aspirants asleep in it. Your Teo. That Quell lass with the little girl." She said it plainly, not as a threat. It was a grade. "That's the bill. You're an assayer. You know how to read one."

She reached across the table. She did not take Hester's hand. She put her own cold hand down on the wood beside it, close, not touching.

"Your lad's line," she said softly. "Two hundred and eleven. Fourth row, under the third window. He's got a pencil in his hand."

Hester went cold to the roots of her hair.

"I go down to the reservoir every week or two to check the sleeves. I come up through the cellar at the third bell, when there's nobody but Agathe asleep in her chair. I've walked the rows. I know where they all lie." She paused. "I didn't know he was yours. Not till Corvin put your name on his privilege and I looked you up in the Customs returns and saw *Vail*. I've been sorry for it every day since."

"Don't."

"I can route round him."

The words lay on the table, next to the ledger with the twelve names in it.

"It's just sleeves," Orsolya said. "Brass, and a bit of lattice. Each plinth's got its own drain to the main; I put them in myself, in Thaw. I can close his. I can go down tonight and close it, and his casing'll dry inside the day, and he'll hold, chick. Till spring and past. As long as he's held these eleven years. Nobody need know. Not Pell. Not Agathe. Not your clever friends." She sat back. "There's three hundred and three others in that ward and I'll not pretend I can spare them all. But I can spare one. I'd like to do it for you."

Hester looked at the cold hand on the table. She looked at the twelve names, and the gauges trembling behind their glass, and the doorway to the gallery where twenty-two people were singing slowly in the dark so that the sea would stay where it was.

She did not say yes. She did not say no. She sat with her mouth shut and her hands flat on the wood. She knew that she had not said no, and she knew that Orsolya knew it.

After a while Orsolya nodded, as if that were an answer.

"Go on up," she said. "It's late, and you've the Assay in nineteen days, and you'll want to pass it, whatever else you want." She went to the stove and lifted the kettle and shook it, and set it back. "I'll not stop you. Tell who you like. I'll not lay a finger on you; I've not that in me, not for you. But you think about what it costs, before you tell." She turned round, and looked at Hester as she had on the Choir floor in Russet, with a blanket in her hands. "You're Lowside. You know what it is to choose who drowns."

* * *

Hester did not remember the climb. She remembered the cold brass rail under her glove, and then the door of R-41. Teo was asleep on his side with one hand under his pillow, over the folded note. She drew the note out from under his hand without waking him, put it in the stove and watched it go. Then she lay down in her gown on top of her blanket and did not close her eyes until the window went grey.

She went to lectures. She sat in the Black Bench while Sorle talked about the decay of kept heat, and wrote down every word and heard none. Twice at the Long Table Teo looked at her across the bench, and twice she looked away.

The letter came at the Lantern Hour, on the post-tray with the Rookery letters: a single sheet folded in three, sealed with a blob of tallow, *Miss H. Vail, Aspirant, the Halcyon* on the outside in Agathe's careful schoolroom hand. Hester broke the tallow with her thumbnail and read it under the poured noon light with the whole Long Table chattering round her.

*My dear,*

*I write because I promised. Something has changed and I think it is for the good. When I went round after noon today your Tib's casing was dry. Not a bead on it. The lines are all gone, and the salt at his feet is as dry as the day he was kept, and I put my hand near it and it is cold and still, as it should be. The others are as they were, I am sorry to say, but his is dry. I do not know why. Mr. Pell does not know. I have told him nothing.*

*Tide willing it will last. Come when you can.*

*A.L.*

She read it twice. She folded it along its creases and put it inside her gown, against her ribs, where her purse used to sit when she had a purse.

*I can go down tonight and close it.*

She had not said yes.

She had not needed to.

* * *

Later, when the Long Table had emptied and the Well had gone dark, she went down the Stair to her own stretch of wall on the Second Fathom and sat on the step under the chalk.

The salt was cold through her gown. Above her head, in her square assayer's hand, it said AMA TESSALY, CHOIR, 903, and above that, in other hands, older, fainter, rewritten every year at the Silent Supper for longer than anyone could remember, the names of the Settled went up the curve of the Stair into the dark.

Below her the hum came up out of the deep into the step and into her bones. Below that, so faint she could not be sure she was not imagining it, the half-Choir was singing. *Hold the line, hold the line.*

Two miles west, under Coldwater Street, in the fourth row, under the third window, her brother lay in a casing as dry as the day she had signed for it, and held. He was not holding the east gallery up any more. Somebody else's brother was, or somebody's mother, or somebody's girl with a red ribbon in her hair. They were holding it in his place, because a woman who was the best of them had closed a brass sleeve in the night, for Hester, without being asked.

She sat on the Stair with Agathe's letter against her ribs and did not move for a long time.
