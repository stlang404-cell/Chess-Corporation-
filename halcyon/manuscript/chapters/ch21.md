# Chapter 21

## Still Runs to Quick

> Law the Fifth. Still runs to quick. Stillness moves through continuous live brine from the stiller to the quicker, as heat moves through iron from the hot end to the cold. It does not leap a gap. It does not answer a wish.
>
> — *The Halcyon Primer, for the use of aspirants*

She told them in Sorle's study on the third of Rime. She told them everything, in order, as she would have read out a seizure.

There were five of them in a room built for one old man and his books. Sorle had the chair behind the desk. Saskia had the other chair, because she had got to it first and did not apologise. Lucan stood by the window with his back to the glass and his arms folded over ribs that still pained him in the cold. Teo sat on the floor against the bookshelves with his knees up and a cup of halcyon tea going cold between his feet. Hester stood. She had meant to sit, and found when she began that she could not.

She told them about the office in the Fifth and the spiral and the notebook. She told them what Orsolya had said about the eleven choristers, and the twelfth, and the Chancellor who had given her nothing, and the founders' lines that had been built, she said, for this. She told them about Aske, standing in his casing in the Quiet Rooms with his arm out and his mouth open on a word. He was not dead. He was waiting, because the woman who had kept him meant to let him out when she was finished. She told them about the offer.

Then she told them the rest.

"Agathe wrote yesterday," she said. "Tib's casing's stopped weeping. The east foot's dry. She says it's the first time since midsummer." She looked at the floor between Teo's boots, where the tea was. "I didn't say yes. I didn't say anything. She did it anyway."

Nobody spoke. Outside the study window the Lantern's mirrors were coming round. The flash crossed the ceiling, white, and went.

"So that's where I am," Hester said. "My brother's safe because a woman I'm going to see arrested decided to make him safe. There's thirteen others still weeping in that ward, and there'll be a fourteenth, and a fifteenth. And if the draw stops, the Hush comes up through the floor and drowns everyone in this building. That's the account."

"Salt and sorrow," said Teo softly.

Saskia was looking at her with an expression Hester had not seen on her before. It was not pity. Saskia did not do pity. It was something nearer to the look she gave a lantern-jar with a flaw in it, deciding whether it would hold.

"You could have kept your mouth shut," Saskia said. "About the lad. We'd never have known."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because it's part of the weight," Hester said. "You don't leave something off the scale because you'd rather not pay duty on it."

Saskia held her eyes a moment longer, and then nodded once, as if a grade had been confirmed.

* * *

They went at it for two hours. It was, Hester thought afterward, the most honest argument she had ever been part of. Nobody in it was stupid and nobody was wrong, and the room got hotter and the tea went undrunk.

"The Chancellor," said Lucan. "We go to the Chancellor. It's the obvious course and I'd like it on the record that I proposed it before anyone sneered."

"I sneer in advance," said Sorle. "It saves time. Go on."

"Holm is the head of this institution. Thane is a Warden. Thane has committed a felony under the Charter and kept a Reader against his will. The Chancellor must act."

"Thane went to the Chancellor," Hester said. "Before any of it. She told her the quickenings had tripled and asked for help. Holm gave her nothing. Told her to manage it quiet."

"So Thane says."

"I believe her."

"You believed her before," said Lucan, not unkindly.

That went in. Hester let it.

"Gage lost the rod," said Teo from the floor. "Not misplaced. Lost. And Vey was coming out of his office. Whatever the Chancellor knows, the Deputy Chancellor knows, and the Deputy Chancellor is the man who thanked Aske for his diligence and moved on to lamp oil." He picked up his cup and looked into it. "I'd want to know who I was telling before I told them."

"The Assize, then," said Lucan. "Outside the Halcyon. My father sits on the Exchequer bench. I know four aldermen who would give their teeth for a scandal under the Charter."

"And the Assize sends the city watch out along the Causeway," said Sorle, "and the Proctors close the Gatehouse, which they are entitled to do under the Charter, and there follows a fortnight of very fine correspondence. During which the draw continues, because the only people who can stop it are on our side of the gate. During which the Hush continues to fail, which it will do whether or not anybody writes a letter about it." He took his spectacles off and pinched his nose. "Mr. Ferrers, your instinct is admirable and your sense of time is a lawyer's. The Hush does not attend hearings."

"Then we cut it," said Saskia. "The draw. Tonight. Lucan and Teo know the way down from the Cold House cellar. Saw the rods out of the line and let the sleepers keep their own."

"And the stays starve," Hester said. "Orsolya's choir is a third short. Ama's dead. They've been holding the Fifth on the sleepers for a year. Cut the line and they've nothing to hold with. You've seen what a quickening does when the stay goes."

They had all seen it. At the Silent Supper they had seen black water run out of dry salt across a gallery floor, and an old woman sit down in it and not get up.

"Then what?" Saskia said. "There's three doors and we've just walled up all three. Tell the people who already know. Tell people who can't do anything. Or drown everyone to save fourteen. Pick one."

"That's what she said," Hester said.

"Who?"

"Orsolya. *You're Lowside. You know what it is to choose who drowns.*" She found she had sat down after all, on the edge of Sorle's desk, among his papers. He did not object. "That's the whole of her case. There's a weight on one side and a weight on the other, and you pick the lighter one to lose, and you hate yourself, and you do it. She thinks that's what grown women do."

"Isn't it?" said Lucan quietly.

"I don't know." Hester looked at her hands. "I've spent eleven years thinking there were two choices for Tib. Keep him, or let him go and die. I picked one and I hated myself and I paid for it on the first of every month. And I never once thought there might be anything else, because nobody in the Cold House ever said there was. That's the thing about a scale. It's very good at telling you which side's heavier. It doesn't tell you whether you've put the right things on it."

Teo had gone quiet in the particular way he had when he was listening to something under the words.

"Go on," said Sorle.

* * *

She did not know, when she began, that she knew it. That was the strange part. Afterward she thought it had been lying in her for weeks in separate pieces, like the bits of a broken scale in a drawer, and that she had only needed to be angry enough to tip the drawer out on the table.

"Aske's last entry," she said. "Teo. Read it again."

Teo had the notebook. He had not let it out of his reach since the night they found it. He opened it at the ribbon and read, in the careful flat voice he used for brine-script, as if the words might break.

"*She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held. The pressure wants a road, not a lid. Vent? Need Sorle's arrears for the shaft.*"

"A road, not a lid," Hester said. "My father said that. Near enough. Not to me about this. About a pan." She saw Lucan's face. "Brine-pans. Lowside. My father boils Weeps salt. When a pan spits, the trick everyone's taught is to drop the lid on it. Smother it. He doesn't. He draws the fire out a shovelful at a time and lets it talk, he says, and what's in the bottom of it comes up a bit at a time and nobody gets burned. And if you lid it, it either blows the lid through the roof, or goes quiet, and then you lift the lid and it all comes up in your face at once. Everything it's been saving."

"The stays are lids," said Teo slowly.

"The stays are lids. Every one of them. You pour still in on top of a quickening and it goes quiet, and the Fathomers write it off as held, and it isn't. It's saving. That's why they keep coming back bigger. Sorle, you said it. In your study, in Fallow. *Pour enough still into a quickening and it quiets, and grows hungrier.*"

"I said it," said Sorle. He had put his spectacles back on and was watching her with an expression she could not read. "I also said, when you asked me what would happen if one let it out, that the moment would proceed. I said it as a warning. You appear to be repeating it as a recommendation."

"It is one."

"Miss Vail—"

"Saskia," Hester said. "Lantern Night. The jars. Tell him what you told me."

Saskia frowned. Then her face changed, and she looked at Hester with real surprise.

"Annealing," she said. "Glass that's cooled fast keeps its stress inside it. You can't see it. It sits there in the jar for a year and one day you put it down on a table and it bursts. So you don't cool it fast. You put it in the annealing oven and bring it down a few degrees at a time, hours, days for the big pieces, and the stress walks out of it. You don't stop the stress. You can't stop it. You give it time to leave."

"Staged," said Hester. "In steps. Not all at once."

"In steps. Hold, drop. Hold, drop. If you get the steps wrong it cracks anyway. Too big a drop, it cracks. Too long a hold, you've wasted coal." Saskia's eyes had gone narrow and bright, the look she got in the kilns. "You'd have to know how much stress was in it to set the steps."

"Arrears," said Lucan.

They all looked at Sorle.

He did not say anything for some time. He sat with his hands flat on the desk on either side of the place where Hester was sitting, and looked at the wall of slates behind her, and his lips moved slightly, the way they did when he was counting.

"No," he said at last. "No, it's mad. It's quite mad. You are proposing to take a quickening, which is a pocket of the held moment under nine centuries of arrears, and let it *go*. Into what? Into the Fifth Fathom? You will flood the Fifth. Into the Hush? It *is* the Hush. The pressure wants a road, Corvin says, very good, Corvin always did write like a man composing his own epitaph. A road to where? There is no road. We are in a dome of salt in the middle of a dead sea. Every direction is down or sideways, and down and sideways is more salt and more moment and more—" He stopped.

"Up," said Hester.

He looked at her.

"There's a shaft," she said. "On the tour, the first day. The Proctor showed us from the Lantern gallery. Out on the White, a quarter-mile east. An iron lid in the salt with a chain on it. She said it went from the Hush to the surface, and it was the founders', and it had never been opened in living memory. The Well-Cap."

"The Founders' Shaft," said Teo.

"It goes up," said Hester. "Out onto the White. Where there's nobody. Where there's nothing to drown but salt."

Sorle got up. He did not say anything. He walked round the desk, past her, to the long wall of slates, and took the cloth off the nail and wiped the first slate clean in four strokes. Then he took a stick of chalk from his waistcoat pocket and began to write.

* * *

He did not stop for four days.

He wiped every slate in the study, and then he sent Teo to the Black Bench lecture room for the six big slates on their easels, and then he sent Lucan to the Brinestacks with a list. Lucan came back with the founders' survey of the Hush in a portfolio, under his coat, and did not say how he had got it out. Then Sorle wrote on the backs of the slates. When the slates ran out he wrote on the wall, on the plaster, between the shelves, in a small cramped hand that ran sideways into the corners. Hester brought him food from the Long Table and he ate it standing up without looking at it. On the first evening Lucan stood in the doorway with the portfolio under his arm and asked, formally, as if reading from a contract, whether Reader Sorle would consider a transfer to his Bench of an aspirant presently attached to the Deputy Chancellor's. Sorle did not turn round. "Can you do sums?" "Yes." "Then sit down and do these, and stop dripping on my floor." Lucan sat down. In the morning there was a black cord on the chair where he had slept, and he put it on without a word, and Hester saw him touch it twice before noon.

On the second night she found him asleep in the chair with the chalk still in his fingers and his mouth open. She put a blanket over him, and he woke an hour later and went on writing as if he had only blinked.

She did not understand most of it. She had never pretended to. Horology was a language she could read like a traveller reads a foreign street sign, enough to know which way the market was. But she sat with him for long hours of it, because he asked her to, and because he needed someone to tell him what things tasted like.

"Quickened grain at the Fifth east gallery," he would say, without turning round. "Your scale. How heavy?"

"Heavier than the *Wren* cargo. Lighter than the line under the Cold House."

"Lighter than the line. Interesting. How much?"

"I don't know how to say how much. It's a taste."

"Then invent a way. You've spent twenty years telling the Exchequer what a grain weighs by putting it on your tongue. Invent a unit. I'll convert it."

So she invented one. She called it a *thorne*, after Mr. Thorne, because she wanted to see Sorle's face. She saw it, and it was worth it. A thorne was the weight behind her eyes of one grain of the Deep at a hundred and forty from the old Customs House reference jar. The *Wren* cargo was eleven thornes. The Fifth east gallery was fourteen. The line under the Cold House, the stillest thing she had ever tasted, the stillness of three hundred sleeping people flowing east through the dark, was forty. Sorle wrote them down and put them through something that took up most of a slate. When he had finished he stood back from it and looked at it for a long time.

"What?" said Hester.

"Nothing. It agrees with Corvin."

"Is that bad?"

"It's unprecedented. Corvin and I have never agreed on anything in twenty years, except the quality of the Harl cider." He wiped his chalky hand down his waistcoat, leaving a white stripe. "It can be done. I want to be very exact about what I am saying, Miss Vail, because it will be quoted back to me at my trial. It can be done if the following things are true." He began to tick them off on the slate with the side of the chalk. "One. The pocket must have a road, a continuous one, to a place where its motion does no harm. That's your shaft, if the founders' survey is honest and the shaft is open below the plug. Two. The release must be staged, as Mrs. Orme says, annealed. The pressure comes down in steps, a third of the arrears at a time, with a hold between each, or the shock of it cracks the Hush floor and we all go into the dark together. Three. Someone must make the release."

"Someone."

"A worker. In the brine, at the boundary of the pocket, hands on the salt. Not keeping it. Letting it go. Giving the held moment permission to proceed, a step at a time." He looked at her over his spectacles. "It will be the hardest release ever made in this building. It is also, by your own account and Maudelaine Crisp's, the thing you are worst at in the world."

"I know."

"You hold like a miser. Her words."

"I know whose words they are."

"And four," said Sorle, as if she had not spoken, "and this is the part I should like you to think about very carefully, because it is not on any slate. The moment proceeds. Whatever is held in that pocket will move again. Water, certainly. Some of it will be nine hundred years old and it will come up the founders' shaft and out onto the White, and in the morning there will be water on the Hollow Sea where there has been none since the Ebb, and every person in Ambry will walk out over the Causeway to look at it." He set the chalk down on the ledge of the slate, very precisely. "And if I am right, if I have been right for thirty-two years, Miss Vail, then it will not only be water. And I will find out that I was right in public, in the worst possible way, at the worst possible time. I find I don't greatly mind. But you should know what you're starting."

She looked at the slates. She thought about the cry in the *Wren* grain. Two notes, and a third that cracked.

"I know what I'm starting," she said.

"No," said Sorle. "Nobody does. That's rather my point."

* * *

They chose the night on the sixth of Rime, in the Rookery, in Hester's cell, with the window black and the frost already feathering its corners. It was Saskia who said it, and as soon as she did, it was obvious.

"Assay Night."

The Midwinter Assay ran from the second bell to the ninth. After it, by a custom older than the Charter, the whole Halcyon fed. Every Reader and Fellow and aspirant sat down at the Long Table, the porters and cooks and delvers at trestles in the Gatehouse yard, under kept light, with the doors open to Ambry. Half the city came over the Causeway for it. The Choir was released from the Stays for the night, all but the skeleton watch, because the Choir was the Halcyon's own and you did not keep your own from the Assay Feast. The Fifth Fathom on Assay Night, Saskia said, who had carried boxes up its lifts in the summer, was emptier than on any night of the year.

"Fewer people down there if it goes wrong," said Teo.

"Fewer people to stop us," said Lucan.

"Both," said Hester. "Mostly the first."

They assigned it there, on the floor of R-41, with the stub of a kept-light candle in a saucer between them. Nobody wrote anything down. Hester had a ledger-mind and it was screaming at her to write it down, and she did not.

Lucan and Teo would go to Ambry after the Assay, with the crowd, and down through the Cold House cellar into the Cistern, with Agathe to let them in. Agathe had said yes before Hester had finished asking. They would have the saws. At the twelfth bell they would cut the draw-rods out of the founders' line, every one, and break the line beyond them. The sleepers' stillness would stop at the Cold House, where it belonged.

"And the moment we cut," said Lucan, "the stays begin to starve."

"The moment you cut, I'll know," said Hester. "I'll be at the Old Tap. I'll taste it go."

Saskia would get the key. She knew Dunmore's desk. She had stood beside it twenty times in the summer, waiting for a box to be signed out. She knew the drawer and she knew he kept the duplicates on a ring with a brass tag that said FIFTH, and among them a long iron key with a lattice bow that had never been signed out to her because she had never been allowed below the Fifth. She would take it during the Assay Feast, when Dunmore would be at the Long Table with every other clerk in the Halcyon, eating for once.

"And if he's not?" said Teo.

"Then I'll think of something," said Saskia. "I'm a widow with a child and a Quell accent. People tell me where things are to get me out of their offices."

Hester and Sorle and Saskia would go down. Through the empty Fifth to the Hush door, and through it, and across the Hush to the foot of the Founders' Shaft. They would open the plug, which the founders' survey said was a salt seal six feet thick, lattice-grown. Then, when Hester tasted the cut, they would go to the boundary of the east quickening and begin.

"Three steps," said Sorle. "A third at a time. Mrs. Orme counts. Mrs. Orme has the best sense of an interval of anyone I've met in forty years, though she will deny it, because it sounds like a compliment."

"It is one," said Saskia. "I'll allow it."

"And you," said Teo to Sorle. "What do you do?"

"I hold the slate," said Sorle, "and I tell Miss Vail when to stop. And if she cannot stop, I hold her."

There was a short silence in R-41.

"It's a felony," said Lucan eventually, in his precise voice. "I'd like that noted. Under the Charter, every part of this is a felony. Breaking the Hush seal. Unlicensed release. Interfering with Halcyon infrastructure. My father's lawyers would want me to mention it."

"Noted," said Hester.

"Good." He unfolded himself from the floor, wincing at his ribs. "I wanted to be sure I'd said it, so that afterward I can't pretend I didn't know." He stood in the low doorway with his head bent under the lintel and looked down at her. "I'll cut your line, Vail. I'd have cut it in Fallow if you'd let me."

* * *

On the ninth of Rime she went to see her father.

She had not been to the Pans since the night in Cinder when he had said the thing about who she had kept Tib for, and she had walked out into the snow. She half expected him not to look up. He looked up. He stopped raking, which he had never done for her in her life, and stood with the rake in his hand in the steam and waited.

"Da."

"Hes."

She had come with a speech. It went out of her head at the sight of him. He looked older. That was all. Thirty days older, and he looked it, as if he had been doing sums too.

"Tib's casing's stopped weeping," she said.

"Agathe told me." He saw her face. "She came round. Sunday. Brought me a pie." He looked down at the pan. "Is that your doing?"

"No. It's someone else's. It's—" She could not say it here. "I'll tell you after. There's something I have to do first. And I need to ask you something."

He waited.

"How do you let a pan down slow?"

He looked at her for a long moment through the steam. She thought he might laugh, or tell her to go away, or ask her why. He did none of those things. He put the rake down across the rim of the pan, carefully, and wiped his hands on his apron, and when he spoke it was in a voice she had not heard from him since she was a child and he had taught her to read the tide-marks on the harbour wall.

"First thing," he said, "is you listen to her. Before you touch anything. Every pan talks different. A pan that's just got muck in the bottom ticks, small and quick, tick tick tick, like a clock. A pan that's got air under the iron where the bed's cracked thumps. Slow. Deep. Like a heart. You want to know which you've got, because the one wants the fire down quick and the other'll crack her iron if you do it quick. So you listen. You stand there and you count and you listen, and you don't do anything at all for as long as you can stand, and longer.

"Then you go to the fire-door. You don't open it wide. You open her a hand. Cold air in on the coals, that's the first thing that changes, and you wait for her to answer. She'll answer. She'll go louder first. Always does. That's the bit that frightens the young lads, and they drop the lid. She goes louder because she's felt the change and she's not sure of it yet. You let her be louder. You stand there and let her shout at you. Then she goes quieter, a bit. And then you draw the first coals. Not many. A shovel. Lay them on the brick to the side where you can get them back if you need them. Then you wait again. And she'll go louder again, not so loud, and quieter again, and you draw the next. And each time you wait till she's said all she's going to say before you take the next shovel. You can't hurry her. You hurry her and she'll go up all at once, and then it doesn't matter how careful you've been.

"And the last thing." He looked down at his left hand, at the two shiny stiff fingers. "Last thing is, when she's talking, you don't stand over her. You stand to the side. With your face out of it. Because you're letting something out, Hes, and it's coming out where it wants, not where you want. You've given it the road. You don't get to pick which way it looks when it comes up." He picked the rake up. "That's all. That's how. Took me forty year and two fingers. You can have it for nowt."

She stood in the heat with her eyes stinging, and told herself it was the steam.

"Ta," she said.

"Hes." He did not look at her. "Whatever it is. Stand to the side."

"I will."

She did not tell him about the Assay. She thought about it all the way home, and did not.

* * *

The release was the thing she could not do, and there were twelve days to learn it.

Sorle could not teach her. He said so at once and without apology. "I am a theorist with indifferent hands. I could no more teach you a release than teach you to whistle. You need a practitioner."

There was only one practitioner who had ever taught her a release. On the White, in Fallow, with Lucan's casing hanging in the rim of the sink and her hands locked on it and everything in her saying *hold, hold*, a voice had said in her ear, low and Lowside: *Don't push it out, chick. Open your hand.*

She would not go to Orsolya. She went instead to the Pans on the Crown at the eighth bell every night, when the rose and green water was black under the stars and nobody else would be fool enough to be out there in the cold. She knelt at the edge with a flask of live brine and a dish. She kept a drop, and returned it. Kept a drop, and returned it. Kept a drop—

And it would not come back. It sat in her palm, clear and round as a seed pearl, kept, and she held it, and she could feel herself holding it. All of her clamped down round it the way her fist clamped round her purse in the Lowside crowd. She could not stop. She knew the theory. A release was the hold reversed: you gave the stillness up, you let go of the intent, you stopped wanting the thing to stay. She could recite it. She could not do it. Every time she reached the place where she should have let go, something in her said *no, not yet, it'll be lost*, and closed.

On the fourth night Teo came out and sat on the rim of the pan beside her with a blanket round both their shoulders and watched her fail for an hour.

"What are you thinking about," he said finally, "when you try?"

"The drop."

"No, you're not."

She was silent.

"You're thinking about what happens if you let go and it isn't there any more," Teo said. "You're not holding the drop. You're holding the not-losing it." He pulled the blanket tighter. "In the Abbey we had a thing we did at Vigil. You took a candle in both hands, cupped, out of the wind, all the way down to the water-stair, and at the bottom you held your hands out over the stair, flat, and let the wind take it. The flame. Not the candle, you kept the candle. Just the flame. The novices could never do it the first year. They'd hold their hands round it to the last second and then snatch them away, and the flame would go out because they'd snatched, not because they'd let go. The old sisters did it like this." He held his hands out in front of him over the black water, cupped, and then, slowly, he turned them palm-up and flat, and opened his fingers. "You don't take your hands away from it. You take your hands away from *round* it. It's still in your palms. It just isn't trapped."

She looked at his open hands.

"Did you ever manage it?"

"Once. In my last year. I'd stopped believing the sea was coming back by then." He smiled, a little crooked. "Maybe that's why I could. I wasn't trying to keep anything for it any more."

She kept a drop. It sat in her palm. She did not try to let go of it. She thought about Teo's hands turning over in the dark, the fingers opening. She thought of her father standing to the side of the pan with his face out of it. She opened her hand: not away from the drop, but out from round it. Flat, the way you hold your palm out to a horse.

The drop went soft. It slid. It ran down the crease of her palm, live brine, warm for an instant with the tiny shove of its own arrears, and dripped off the side of her hand into the pan, and was water among water.

She sat and looked at her wet palm for a long time.

"Again," she said.

She did it eleven times more that night, and failed on seven. On the ninth night she failed on two. On the eleventh she returned a kept moth from Sorle's desk, a brown one that had been in its casing for six years. It sat on her palm for the length of a breath, stunned, with the faint heat of six years coming off it, and then opened its wings and went up off her hand into the dark over the Pans, toward the Lantern.

She watched it go until she could not see it. She had a racing in her chest and a heat in her face that the frost could not touch, and she knew that was the rush, the small cost of a small release. She thought: one moth. She thought of the quickening in the Fifth east gallery, fourteen thornes, and the black water at the Silent Supper, and the founders' shaft going up through six hundred feet of salt to an iron lid in the dark.

She thought of Tib's hand, curled on his chest, holding its pencil.

Then she filled her flask from the pan and went in, because it was the nineteenth of Rime, and in two days she would have to do it in front of the whole city, and then again in the dark, where it counted.
