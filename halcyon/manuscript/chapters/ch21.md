# Chapter 21

## Still Runs to Quick

> Law the Fifth. Still runs to quick. Stillness moves through continuous live brine from the stiller to the quicker, as heat moves through iron from the hot end to the cold. It does not leap a gap. It does not answer a wish.
>
> — *The Halcyon Primer, for the use of aspirants*

She told them in Sorle's study on the fourth of Rime, everything, in order, as she would have read out a seizure.

There were five of them in a room built for one old man and his books. Sorle had the chair behind the desk. Saskia had the other chair, because she had got to it first and did not apologise. Lucan stood under the slit of salt-glass with his arms folded over ribs that still pained him in the cold. Teo sat on the floor against the bookshelves with a cup of halcyon tea going cold between his feet. Hester stood. She had meant to sit, and found when she began that she could not.

She told them about the office in the Fifth and the spiral and the notebook. She told them about the twelve names, and the Chancellor who had given Orsolya nothing, and the founders' lines that had been built, Orsolya said, for this. She told them that Aske was not dead. He was waiting, because the woman who had kept him meant to let him out when she was finished. She told them about the offer.

Then she told them the rest.

"Agathe wrote yesterday," she said. "Tib's casing's stopped weeping. The east foot's dry." She looked at the floor between Teo's boots, where the tea was. "I didn't say yes. I didn't say anything. She did it anyway."

Nobody spoke.

"So that's where I am," Hester said. "My brother's safe because a woman I'm going to see arrested decided to make him safe. There's a dozen others still weeping in that ward, and there'll be more. And if the draw stops, the Hush comes up through the floor and drowns everyone in this building. That's the account."

"Salt and sorrow," said Teo softly.

Saskia was looking at her as she looked at a lantern-jar with a flaw in it, deciding whether it would hold.

"You could have kept your mouth shut," she said. "About the lad. We'd never have known."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because it's part of the weight," Hester said. "You don't leave something off the scale because you'd rather not pay duty on it."

Saskia held her eyes a moment longer, and nodded once, as if a grade had been confirmed.

* * *

They went at it for two hours. It was the most honest argument Hester had ever been part of. Nobody in it was stupid and nobody was wrong, and the room got hotter and the tea went undrunk.

"The Chancellor," said Lucan. "It's the obvious course, and I'd like it on the record that I proposed it before anyone sneered."

"I sneer in advance," said Sorle. "It saves time. Go on."

"Thane has committed a felony under the Charter and kept a Reader against his will. The Chancellor must act."

"Thane went to the Chancellor," Hester said. "Before any of it. Holm gave her nothing. Told her to manage it quiet."

"So Thane says."

"I believe her."

"You believed her before," said Lucan, not unkindly.

That went in. Hester let it.

"Gage lost the rod," said Teo from the floor. "And Vey was coming out of his office. Whatever the Chancellor knows, the Deputy knows, and the Deputy is the man who thanked Aske for his diligence and moved on to lamp oil. I'd want to know who I was telling before I told them."

"The Assize, then," said Lucan. "I know four aldermen who would give their teeth for a scandal under the Charter."

"And the Assize sends the watch along the Causeway," said Sorle, "and the Proctors close the Gatehouse, as they are entitled to, and there follows a fortnight of very fine correspondence. During which the draw continues, and the Hush continues to fail, which it will do whether or not anybody writes a letter about it." He took his spectacles off and pinched his nose. "Mr. Ferrers, your instinct is admirable and your sense of time is a lawyer's. The Hush does not attend hearings."

"Then we cut it," said Saskia. "Tonight. Lucan and Teo know the way down from the Cold House cellar. Saw the rods out and let the sleepers keep their own."

"And the stays starve," Hester said. "The Choir's a third short. Ama's dead. They've been holding the Fifth on the sleepers for a year. Cut the line and they've nothing to hold with. I was in the east gallery at the Silent Supper. I've seen what a quickening does when the stay goes."

"Then what?" Saskia said. "Three doors, and we've just walled up all three. Tell the people who already know. Tell people who can't do anything. Or drown everyone to save a dozen. Pick one."

"That's what she said," Hester said. "*You're Lowside. You know what it is to choose who drowns.*" She found she had sat down after all, on the edge of Sorle's desk among his papers. He did not object. "That's the whole of her case. A weight on one side and a weight on the other, and you pick the lighter one to lose, and you hate yourself, and you do it."

"Isn't it what grown women do?" said Lucan quietly.

"I don't know." Hester looked at her hands. "I've spent eleven years thinking there were two choices for Tib. Keep him, or let him go and die. I picked one and hated myself and paid for it on the first of every month, and I never once thought there might be anything else. That's the thing about a scale. It's very good at telling you which side's heavier. It doesn't tell you whether you've put the right things on it."

"Go on," said Sorle.

* * *

She did not know, when she began, that she knew it. It had been lying in her for weeks in separate pieces, like the bits of a broken scale in a drawer, and she had only needed to be angry enough to tip the drawer out on the table.

"Aske's last entry," she said. "Teo. Read it again."

Teo had not let the notebook out of his reach since the night they found it. He opened it at the ribbon.

"*She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held. The pressure wants a road, not a lid. Vent? Need Sorle's arrears for the shaft.*"

"A road, not a lid," Hester said. "My father said that, near enough. About a pan." She saw Lucan's face. "Brine-pans. When a pan spits, the trick everyone's taught is to drop the lid on it. My father doesn't. He draws the fire a shovelful at a time and lets her talk, and what's in the bottom comes up a bit at a time and nobody gets burned. Lid her, and either she blows the lid through the roof, or she goes quiet and you lift the lid later and it all comes up in your face at once. Everything she's been saving."

"The stays are lids," said Teo slowly.

"Every one of them. You pour still in on top of a quickening and it goes quiet, and the Fathomers write it off as held, and it isn't. It's saving. That's why they come back bigger. Sorle, you said it, in Fallow. *Pour enough still into a quickening and it quiets, and grows hungrier.*"

"I said it," said Sorle. He had put his spectacles back on. "I also said, when you asked what would happen if one let it out, that the moment would proceed. I said it as a warning. You appear to be repeating it as a recommendation."

"It is one. Saskia. Lantern Night. The jars. Tell him what you told me."

Saskia frowned. Then her face changed.

"Annealing," she said. "Glass that's cooled fast keeps its stress inside. You can't see it. It sits in the jar a year and one day you put it down on a table and it bursts. So you put it in the lehr and bring it down a few degrees at a time, hours, days for the big pieces, and the stress walks out of it. You don't stop the stress. You can't. You give it time to leave." Her eyes had gone narrow and bright, the look she got in the kilns. "Hold, drop. Hold, drop. Too big a drop, it cracks anyway. You'd have to know how much stress was in it to set the steps."

"Arrears," said Lucan.

They all looked at Sorle.

He sat with his hands flat on the desk on either side of the place where Hester was sitting, and looked at the wall of slates behind her, and his lips moved slightly, as they did when he was counting.

"No," he said at last. "It's quite mad. You propose to take a pocket of the held moment under nine centuries of arrears and let it *go*. Into what? Into the Fifth? You will flood the Fifth. Into the Hush? It *is* the Hush. The pressure wants a road, Corvin says; Corvin always did write like a man composing his own epitaph. A road to where? We are in a dome of salt in the middle of a dead sea. Every direction is down or sideways, and down and sideways is more salt and more moment and more—" He stopped.

"Up," said Hester.

He looked at her.

"The Well-Cap," she said. "Saskia showed us, the first day, from the Lantern gallery. A quarter-mile east, an iron lid on the salt with a chain on it. The founders' shaft, she said, all the way down to the Hush, and nobody's opened it since they capped it."

"The Founders' Shaft," said Teo.

"It goes up," said Hester. "Out onto the White. Where there's nothing to drown but salt."

Sorle got up. He walked round the desk, past her, to the long wall of slates, took the cloth off its nail and wiped the first slate clean in four strokes. Then he took a stick of chalk from his waistcoat pocket and began to write.

* * *

He did not stop for four days.

He wiped every slate in the study, then sent Teo for the six big slates from the Black Bench lecture room, then sent Lucan to the Brinestacks with a list. Lucan came back with the founders' survey of the Hush in a portfolio under his coat and did not say how he had got it out. When the slates ran out Sorle wrote on the plaster between the shelves, in a small cramped hand that ran sideways into the corners. Hester brought him food from the Long Table and he ate it standing up without looking at it.

On the first evening Lucan stood in the doorway with the portfolio under his arm and asked, as if reading from a contract, whether Reader Sorle would consider a transfer to his Bench of an aspirant presently attached to the Deputy Chancellor's. Sorle did not turn round. "Can you do sums?" "Yes." "Then sit down and do these, and stop dripping on my floor." In the morning there was a black cord on the chair where Lucan had slept. He put it on without a word, and Hester saw him touch it twice before noon.

She did not understand most of the slates. Horology was a language she read the way a traveller reads a foreign street sign, enough to know which way the market was. But she sat with him through long hours of it, because he needed someone to tell him what things tasted like.

"Quickened grain at the Fifth east gallery," he would say, without turning round. "Your scale. How heavy?"

"Heavier than the *Wren* cargo. Lighter than the line under the Cold House."

"How much lighter?"

"It's a taste. I can't say how much."

"You've spent twenty years telling the Exchequer what a grain weighs by putting it on your tongue. Invent a unit. I'll convert it."

So she invented one. She called it a *thorne*, after old Mr. Thorne, who had had her stool before her, because she wanted to see Sorle's face. A thorne was the weight behind her eyes of one grain of the Deep at a hundred and forty from Thorne's own reference jar. The *Wren* cargo was eleven thornes. The Fifth east gallery was fourteen. The line under the Cold House, the stillness of three hundred sleeping people flowing east through the dark, was forty. Sorle put them through something that took up most of a slate, and stood back from it for a long time.

"What?" said Hester.

"It agrees with Corvin. Corvin and I have never agreed on anything in twenty years, except the quality of the Harl cider." He wiped his chalky hand down his waistcoat, leaving a white stripe. "It can be done. I want to be exact, Miss Vail, because it will be quoted back to me at my trial. It can be done if the following things are true." He ticked them on the slate with the side of the chalk. "One. The pocket must have a continuous road to a place where its motion does no harm. That's your shaft, if the founders' survey is honest. Two. The release must be staged. Annealed, as Mrs. Orme says. A third of the arrears at a time, with a hold between, or the shock cracks the Hush floor and we all go into the dark together. Three. Someone must make the release. A worker, in the brine, at the boundary of the pocket, hands on the salt, giving the held moment leave to proceed a step at a time." He looked at her over his spectacles. "It will be the hardest release ever made in this building. It is also, by your own account and Maudelaine Crisp's, the thing you are worst at in the world."

"I know."

"And four," said Sorle, "which is not on any slate. The moment proceeds. Whatever is held in that pocket will move again. Water, certainly, nine hundred years old, up the shaft and out onto the White, and in the morning every person in Ambry will walk over the Causeway to look at it." He set the chalk down on the ledge, precisely. "And if I have been right for thirty-two years, it will not only be water. I will find out that I was right in public, in the worst possible way, at the worst possible time. I find I don't greatly mind. But you should know what you're starting."

She thought about the cry in the *Wren* grain. Two notes, and a third that cracked.

"I know what I'm starting," she said.

"No," said Sorle. "Nobody does. That's rather my point."

* * *

They chose the night on the sixth of Rime, in R-41, with the frost feathering the corners of the window. It was Saskia who said it, and as soon as she did, it was obvious.

"Assay Night."

After the Assay, by a custom older than the Charter, the whole Halcyon fed: Readers and Fellows and aspirants at the Long Table, porters and cooks at trestles in the Gatehouse yard, the doors open to Ambry and half the city come over the Causeway. The Choir was stood down for the night, all but a skeleton watch, because you did not keep your own from the Assay Feast. The Fifth on Assay Night, said Saskia, who had carried boxes up its lifts all summer, was emptier than on any night of the year.

"Fewer people down there if it goes wrong," said Teo.

"Fewer people to stop us," said Lucan.

"Both," said Hester. "Mostly the first."

Nobody wrote anything down. Hester's ledger-mind was screaming at her to, and she did not.

Lucan and Teo would go to Ambry after the Assay with the crowd, and down through the Cold House cellar into the Cistern. Agathe would let them in; she had said yes before Hester finished asking. At the twelfth bell they would saw the draw-rods out of the founders' line, every one, and the sleepers' stillness would stop at the Cold House, where it belonged.

"And the moment we cut," said Lucan, "the stays begin to starve."

"The moment you cut, I'll know," said Hester. "I'll be at the Old Tap. I'll taste it go."

Saskia would get the key. She knew Dunmore's desk and its left-hand drawer, and the brass-tagged ring in it, *W.L.F. Duplicates*, and on the ring the long black iron key with a little bird sitting on a line on its bow. She would take it during the Feast, when Dunmore would be at the Long Table with every other clerk in the Halcyon, eating for once.

"And if he's not?" said Teo.

"Then I'll think of something. I'm a widow with a child and a Quell accent. People tell me where things are to get me out of their offices."

Hester and Sorle and Saskia would go down through the empty Fifth to the Hush door, and across the Hush to the foot of the Founders' Shaft, and open the plug, which the survey called a lattice-grown salt seal six feet thick. Then, when Hester tasted the cut, they would go to the boundary of the east quickening and begin.

"Three steps," said Sorle. "Mrs. Orme counts. Mrs. Orme has the best sense of an interval of anyone I have met in forty years, though she will deny it, because it sounds like a compliment."

"It is one," said Saskia. "I'll allow it."

"And you?" said Teo.

"I hold the slate," said Sorle, "and I tell Miss Vail when to stop. And if she cannot stop, I hold her."

There was a short silence in R-41.

"Every part of this is a felony," said Lucan eventually, in his precise voice. "Breaking the Hush seal. Unlicensed release. Interfering with Halcyon infrastructure. I wanted to be sure I'd said it, so that afterward I can't pretend I didn't know." He unfolded himself from the floor, wincing at his ribs, and stood in the low doorway with his head bent under the lintel. "I'll cut your line, Vail. I'd have cut it in Fallow if you'd let me."

* * *

On the ninth of Rime she went to see her father.

She had not seen him since the Drowned Man. She half expected him not to look up. He looked up. He stopped raking, which he had never done for her in her life, and stood with the rake in his hand in the steam and waited.

"Da."

"Hes."

She had come with a speech. It went out of her head at the sight of him. He looked older. Three weeks older, and he looked it, as if he had been doing sums too. Neither of them said anything about the desk at the Cold House, or the pen. It stood between them in the steam like a third person, and they let it.

"Tib's casing's stopped weeping," she said.

"Agathe told me. She came round Sunday. Brought me a pie." He looked down at the pan. "Is that your doing?"

"No. It's someone else's." She could not say it here. "I'll tell you after. There's something I have to do first. And I need to ask you something."

He waited.

"How do you let a pan down slow?"

He looked at her through the steam. She thought he might laugh, or tell her to go away. He put the rake down across the rim of the pan, carefully, and wiped his hands on his apron, and when he spoke it was in a voice she had not heard from him since he had taught her to read the tide-marks on the harbour wall.

"First thing is you listen to her. Before you touch anything. Every pan talks different. A pan with muck in the bottom ticks, small and quick, like a clock. A pan with air under the iron where the bed's cracked thumps. Slow. Deep. Like a heart. The one wants the fire down quick and the other'll crack her iron if you do it quick. So you stand there and you count and you listen, and you don't do anything at all for as long as you can stand, and longer.

"Then you go to the fire-door. You don't open it wide. You open her a hand, and you wait for her to answer. She'll go louder first. Always does. That's the bit that frightens the young lads, and they drop the lid. She goes louder because she's felt the change and she's not sure of it yet. You let her be louder. You stand there and let her shout at you. Then she goes quieter, a bit, and then you draw the first coals. A shovel. Lay them on the brick to the side where you can get them back if you need them. And she'll go louder again, not so loud, and quieter again, and you draw the next. Each time you wait till she's said all she's going to say before you take the next shovel. You hurry her and she'll go up all at once, and then it doesn't matter how careful you've been.

"And the last thing." He looked down at his left hand, at the two shiny stiff fingers. "When she's talking, you don't stand over her. You stand to the side, with your face out of it. Because you're letting something out, Hes, and it's coming out where it wants, not where you want. You've given it the road. You don't get to pick which way it looks when it comes up." He picked the rake up. "That's how. Took me forty year and two fingers. You can have it for nowt."

She stood in the heat with her eyes stinging, and told herself it was the steam.

"Ta," she said.

"Hes." He did not look at her. "Whatever it is. Stand to the side."

"I will."

She did not tell him about the Assay. She thought about it all the way home, and did not.

* * *

The release was the thing she could not do, and there were twelve days to learn it.

Sorle could not teach her. He said so at once. "I am a theorist with indifferent hands. I could no more teach you a release than teach you to whistle."

Crisp had taught her the theory on her first day at the Pans. Only one person had ever got a release out of her: on the White, in Fallow, with Lucan's casing hanging in the rim of the sink and everything in her saying *hold, hold*, a low Lowside voice in her ear. *Don't push it out, chick. Open your hand.*

She would not go to Orsolya. She went instead to the Pans on the Crown every night at the eighth bell, when the rose and green water was black under the stars and nobody else would be fool enough to be out in the cold. She knelt at the edge with a flask of live brine and a dish. She kept a drop, and returned it. Kept a drop—

And it would not come back. It sat in her palm, clear and round as a seed pearl, and she could feel herself holding it, all of her clamped round it as her fist clamped round her purse in a Lowside crowd. She knew the theory. You gave the stillness up, you let go of the intent, you stopped wanting the thing to stay. She could recite it. Every time she reached the place where she should have let go, something in her said *not yet, it'll be lost*, and closed.

On the fourth night Teo came out and sat on the rim of the pan beside her with a blanket round both their shoulders and watched her fail for an hour.

"What are you thinking about," he said finally, "when you try?"

"The drop."

"No, you're not. You're thinking about what happens if you let go and it isn't there any more. You're not holding the drop. You're holding the not-losing it." He pulled the blanket tighter. "At the Abbey, at Vigil, you carried a candle cupped in both hands down to the water-stair, and at the bottom you held your hands out and let the wind take the flame. The novices could never do it. They'd hold their hands round it to the last second and then snatch them away, and the flame went out because they'd snatched, not because they'd let go. The old sisters did it like this." He held his hands out over the black water, cupped, and then, slowly, turned them palm-up and flat, and opened his fingers. "You don't take your hands away from it. You take your hands away from *round* it. It's still in your palms. It just isn't trapped."

"Did you ever manage it?"

"Once. In my last year. I'd stopped believing the sea was coming back by then." He smiled, a little crooked. "Maybe that's why I could. I wasn't trying to keep anything for it any more."

She kept a drop. She did not try to let go of it. She thought of Teo's hands turning over in the dark, and her father standing to the side of the pan with his face out of it. She opened her hand: not away from the drop, but out from round it, flat, as you hold your palm out to a horse.

The drop went soft. It slid down the crease of her palm, live brine, warm for an instant with the tiny shove of its own arrears, and dripped off the side of her hand into the pan, and was water among water.

"Again," she said.

She did it eleven times more that night, and failed on seven. On the ninth night she failed on two, and afterwards, in R-41, she took down the two dishes that had stood on her shelf since Russet with her first two kept drops in them, Crisp's drop and the Pans drop, and opened her hand over each. They went to water. Teo, from his bed, applauded softly.

On the eleventh night she returned a kept moth from Sorle's desk, a brown one six years in its casing. It sat on her palm for the length of a breath, stunned, with the faint heat of six years coming off it, and then opened its wings and went up off her hand into the dark over the Pans, toward the Lantern.

She had a racing in her chest and a heat in her face that the frost could not touch, and knew it for the rush, the small cost of a small release. She thought: one moth. She thought of the east gallery, fourteen thornes, and the black water at the Silent Supper, and the Founders' Shaft going up through three hundred feet of salt to an iron lid in the dark.

She thought of Tib's hand, curled on his chest, holding its pencil.

Then she filled her flask from the pan and went in, because it was the nineteenth of Rime, and in two days she would have to do it in front of the whole city, and then again in the dark, where it counted.
