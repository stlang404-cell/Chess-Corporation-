# Chapter 21

## Still Runs to Quick

> Law the Fifth. Still runs to quick. Stillness moves through continuous live brine from the stiller to the quicker, as heat moves through iron from the hot end to the cold. It does not leap a gap. It does not answer a wish.
>
> — *The Halcyon Primer, for the use of aspirants*

She told them in Sorle's study on the fourth of Rime, everything, in order.

There were five of them in a room built for one old man and his books. Sorle had the chair behind the desk. Saskia had the other chair, because she had got to it first and did not apologise. Lucan stood under the slit of salt-glass with his arms folded over ribs that still pained him in the cold. Teo sat on the floor against the bookshelves with a cup of halcyon tea going cold between his feet. Hester stood.

She told them about the office in the Fifth and the spiral and the notebook. She told them about the twelve names, and the Chancellor who had given Orsolya nothing. She told them that Aske was not dead, only waiting, because the woman who had kept him meant to let him out when she was finished. She told them about the offer.

Then she told them the rest.

"Agathe wrote yesterday," she said. "Tib's casing's stopped weeping. The east foot's dry." She looked at the floor between Teo's boots. "I didn't say yes. I didn't say anything. She did it anyway."

Teo set his cup down.

"So that's where I am," Hester said. "My brother's safe because a woman I'm going to see arrested decided to make him safe. There's a dozen others still weeping in that ward, and there'll be more. And if the draw stops, the Hush comes up through the floor and drowns everyone in this building. That's the account." She turned to Lucan and Teo. "You'll be going under the Cold House again. To count the rods."

"On the seventh," Lucan said. "Why?"

"Open his sleeve. Two hundred and eleven, fourth row, under the third window. Put him back on the line with the rest of them."

"Hes," said Teo.

"He's drawn like the others till the cut, or I'm hers." Her voice did not stay as level as she wanted. "I'll not be hers. I'll not have him held up by her and the Corries bled for it."

"Salt and sorrow," said Teo softly.

Saskia was looking at her as she looked at a lantern-jar with a flaw in it, deciding whether it would hold.

"You could have kept your mouth shut," she said. "About the lad. We'd never have known."

"I'd have known."

Saskia held her eyes a moment longer, and nodded once.

* * *

They went at it for two hours. It was the most honest argument Hester had ever been part of. Nobody in it was stupid and nobody was wrong, and the room got hotter and the tea went undrunk.

"The Chancellor," said Lucan. "It's the obvious course, and I'd like it on the record that I proposed it before anyone sneered."

"I sneer in advance," said Sorle. "It saves time. Go on."

"Thane has committed a felony under the Charter and kept a Reader against his will. The Chancellor must act."

"Thane went to the Chancellor," Hester said. "Before any of it. Holm gave her nothing. Told her to manage it quiet."

"So Thane says."

"I believe her."

"You believed her before," said Lucan, not unkindly.

That went in. Hester let it.

"Gage lost the rod," said Teo from the floor. "And Vey was coming out of his office. I'd want to know who I was telling before I told them."

"The Assize, then," said Lucan. "I know four aldermen who would give their teeth for a scandal under the Charter."

"And the Assize sends the watch along the Causeway," said Sorle, "and the Proctors close the Gatehouse, and there follows a fortnight of very fine correspondence, during which the Hush continues to fail." He took his spectacles off and pinched his nose. "Mr. Ferrers, your instinct is admirable and your sense of time is a lawyer's. The Hush does not attend hearings."

"Then we cut it," said Saskia. "Tonight. Lucan and Teo know the way down from the Cold House cellar. Saw the rods out and let the sleepers keep their own."

"And the stays starve," Hester said. "The Choir's a third short. They've been holding the Fifth on the sleepers for a year. Cut the line and they've nothing to hold with."

"Then what?" Saskia said. "Three doors, and we've just walled up all three. Tell the people who already know. Tell people who can't do anything. Or drown everyone to save a dozen. Pick one."

"That's what she said," Hester said. "*You're Lowside. You know what it is to choose who drowns.*" She found she had sat down after all, on the edge of Sorle's desk among his papers. He did not object. "That's the whole of her case. A weight on one side and a weight on the other, and you pick the lighter one to lose, and you hate yourself, and you do it."

"Isn't that what grown women do?" said Lucan quietly.

"I don't know." Hester looked at her hands. "I've spent eleven years thinking there were two choices for Tib. Keep him, or let him go and die. I picked one and hated myself and paid for it on the first of every month, and I never once thought there might be anything else. That's the thing about a scale. It's very good at telling you which side's heavier. It doesn't tell you whether you've put the right things on it."

"Go on," said Sorle.

* * *

She did not know, when she began, that she knew it. It had been lying in her for weeks in separate pieces, and she had only needed to be angry enough to tip them out on the table.

"Aske's last entry," she said. "Teo. Read it again."

Teo had not let the notebook out of his reach since the night they found it. He opened it at the ribbon.

"*She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held. The pressure wants a road, not a lid. Vent? Need Sorle's arrears for the shaft.*"

"A road, not a lid," Hester said. "My father said that, near enough. About a pan." She saw Lucan's face. "Brine-pans. When a pan spits, the trick everyone's taught is to drop the lid on it. My father doesn't. He draws the fire a shovelful at a time and lets her talk. Lid her, and either she blows the lid through the roof, or she goes quiet and you lift the lid later and it all comes up in your face at once. Everything she's been saving."

"The stays are lids," said Teo slowly.

"Every one of them. You pour still in on top of a quickening and it goes quiet, and the Fathomers write it off as held, and it isn't. It's saving. That's why they come back bigger. Sorle, you said it, in Fallow. *Pour enough still into a quickening and it quiets, and grows hungrier.*"

"I said it," said Sorle. He had put his spectacles back on. "I also said, when you asked what would happen if one let it out, that the moment would proceed. I said it as a warning. You appear to be repeating it as a recommendation."

"It is one. Saskia. Lantern Night. The jars. Tell him what you told me."

Saskia frowned. Then her face changed.

"Annealing," she said. "Glass that's cooled fast keeps its stress inside. One day you put it down on a table and it bursts. So you put it in the lehr and bring it down a few degrees at a time, hours, days for the big pieces, and the stress walks out of it. You don't stop the stress. You can't. You give it time to leave." Her eyes had gone narrow and bright, the look she got in the kilns. "Hold, drop. Hold, drop. Too big a drop, it cracks anyway. You'd have to know how much stress was in it to set the steps."

"Arrears," said Lucan.

They all looked at Sorle.

He sat with his hands flat on the desk on either side of the place where Hester was sitting, and looked at the wall of slates behind her, and his lips moved slightly, as they did when he was counting.

"No," he said at last. "It's quite mad. You propose to take a pocket of the held moment under nine centuries of arrears and let it *go*. Into what? Into the Fifth? You will flood the Fifth. Into the Hush? It *is* the Hush. The pressure wants a road, Corvin says; Corvin always did write like a man composing his own epitaph. A road to where? We are in a dome of salt in the middle of a dead sea. Every direction is down or sideways, and down and sideways is more salt and more moment and more—" He stopped.

"Up," said Hester.

He looked at her.

"The Well-Cap," she said. "The Proctor showed us, the first day, from the Lantern gallery. A quarter-mile east, an iron lid on the salt with a chain on it. The Founders' Shaft, he said, all the way down to the Hush, and nobody's opened it since they capped it."

"The Founders' Shaft," said Teo.

"It goes up," said Hester. "Out onto the White. Where there's nothing to drown but salt."

Sorle got up. He walked round the desk, past her, to the long wall of slates, took the cloth off its nail and wiped the first slate clean in four strokes. Then he took a stick of chalk from his waistcoat pocket and began to write.

* * *

He did not stop for four days.

He wiped every slate in the study, then sent Teo for the six big slates from the Black Bench lecture room, then sent Lucan to the Brinestacks with a list. Lucan came back with the founders' survey of the Hush under his coat and did not say how he had got it out. When the slates ran out Sorle wrote on the plaster between the shelves.

On the first evening Lucan asked, as if reading from a contract, whether Reader Sorle would consider a transfer to his Bench of an aspirant presently attached to the Deputy Chancellor's. Sorle did not turn round. "Can you do sums?" "Yes." "Then sit down and do these, and stop dripping on my floor." In the morning there was a black cord on the chair where Lucan had slept. Hester saw him touch it twice before noon.

She did not understand most of the slates. But she sat with him through long hours of it, because he needed someone to tell him what things tasted like.

"Quickened grain at the Fifth east gallery," he would say, without turning round. "How heavy?"

She gave it to him in thornes, her private scale since the customs stool, named for old Mr. Thorne, and watched Sorle's face as he learned that a unit of Horology was now called after a dead customs man. The *Wren* cargo was eleven thornes. The Fifth east gallery was fourteen. The line under the Cold House, the stillness of three hundred sleeping people flowing east through the dark, was forty. Sorle put them through something that took up most of a slate, and stood back from it for a long time.

"What?" said Hester.

"It agrees with Corvin. Corvin and I have never agreed on anything in twenty years, except the quality of the Harl cider." He wiped his chalky hand down his waistcoat, leaving a white stripe. "It can be done. I want to be exact, Miss Vail, because it will be quoted back to me at my trial. It can be done if the following things are true." He ticked them on the slate with the side of the chalk. "One. The pocket must have a continuous road to a place where its motion does no harm. That's your shaft, if the founders' survey is honest. Two. The release must be staged. Annealed, as Mrs. Orme says. A third of the arrears at a time, with a hold between, or the shock cracks the Hush floor and we all go into the dark together. Three. Someone must make the release. A worker, in the brine, at the boundary of the pocket, hands on the salt, giving the held moment leave to proceed a step at a time." He looked at her over his spectacles. "It will be the hardest release ever made in this building. It is also, by your own account and Maudelaine Crisp's, the thing you are worst at in the world."

"I know."

"And four," said Sorle, "which is not on any slate. The moment proceeds. Whatever is held in that pocket will move again. Water, certainly, nine hundred years old, up the shaft and out onto the White." He set the chalk down on the ledge, precisely. "And if I have been right for thirty-two years, it will not only be water. I will find out that I was right in public, at the worst possible time. I find I don't greatly mind. But you should know what you're starting."

She thought about the cry in the *Wren* grain. Two notes, and a third that cracked.

"I know what I'm starting," she said.

"No," said Sorle. "Nobody does. That's rather my point."

* * *

They chose the night on the sixth of Rime, in R-41, with the frost feathering the corners of the window. It was Saskia who said it, and as soon as she did, it was obvious.

"Assay Night."

After the Assay, by a custom older than the Charter, the whole Halcyon fed, the doors open to Ambry and half the city come over the Causeway. The Choir was stood down for the night, all but a skeleton watch, because you did not keep your own from the Assay Feast. The Fifth on Assay Night, said Saskia, who had met its box-lift three nights a week all summer and heard what came up in it, was emptier than on any night of the year.

"Fewer people down there if it goes wrong," said Teo.

"Fewer people to stop us," said Lucan.

"Both," said Hester. "Mostly the first."

Nobody wrote anything down. Hester's ledger-mind was screaming at her to, and she did not.

Lucan and Teo would go to Ambry after the Assay with the crowd, and down through the Cold House cellar into the Cistern. Agathe would let them in; she had said yes before Hester finished asking. At the twelfth bell they would saw the draw-rods out of the founders' line, every one, and the sleepers' stillness would stop at the Cold House, where it belonged.

"And the moment we cut," said Lucan, "the stays begin to starve."

"The moment you cut, I'll know," said Hester. "I'll be on the line in the Hush. I'll taste it go. And from then till the shaft takes the water, there's a skeleton watch on the east stays with nothing in the line. That's the hole. I'll not pretend it isn't."

"Then fill it," said Saskia.

"Orsolya will fill it."

There was a silence in R-41 of a kind Hester had not heard in it before.

"She'll be at the Feast," Hester said. "She'll feel the line go soft before you've put your saws down; she's had her hands on it every day for a year. She'll come down. And when she sees the shaft take water, she'll hold the stays."

"Or she'll shut the shaft," said Lucan. "Or you. She has already kept one person who stood in her way at that Tap."

"It's not a plan," said Saskia. "It's a prayer with a name in it."

"Miss Vail," said Sorle, quite gently, "that is madness."

"I know her." Hester heard how it sounded, and said it anyway. "She's buried twelve of them. She went to their mams. Whatever else she is, she'll not stand in the Fifth and watch the Choir go under when there's a road open. She'll not let them drown."

Saskia would get the key from Dunmore's left-hand drawer: the brass-tagged ring, *W.L.F. Duplicates*, and on it the long black iron key with a little bird sitting on a line on its bow. She would take it during the Feast, when Dunmore would be at the Long Table eating for once.

"And if he's not?" said Teo.

"Then I'll think of something. I'm a widow with a child and a Quell accent. People tell me where things are to get me out of their offices."

Hester and Sorle and Saskia would go down through the empty Fifth and across the Hush to the foot of the Founders' Shaft, and open the plug, a lattice-grown salt seal six feet thick. Then, when Hester tasted the cut, they would go to the boundary of the east quickening and begin.

"Three steps," said Sorle. "Mrs. Orme counts. Mrs. Orme has the best sense of an interval of anyone I have met in forty years."

"I'll allow it," said Saskia.

"And you?" said Teo.

"I hold the slate," said Sorle, "and I tell Miss Vail when to stop. And if she cannot stop, I hold her."

"Hold her how?" said Teo.

"You will see. I hope you will not."

"Every part of this is a felony," said Lucan, in his precise voice. "Breaking the Hush seal. Unlicensed release. Interfering with Halcyon infrastructure. I wanted to be sure I'd said it, so that afterward I can't pretend I didn't know." He unfolded himself from the floor, wincing at his ribs, and stood in the low doorway with his head bent under the lintel. "I'll cut your line, Vail. I'd have cut it in Fallow if you'd let me."

* * *

Agathe's letter came on the morning of the ninth of Rime.

*My dear. Your Tib is wet again at the foot since the seventh, the beads running east as before. I am sorry. I know you will want it plain. And the Corrie twins have begun to craze, Nell first and Jem after her, fine lines all down the east side like a cup that has been in the oven too long. Mr. Pell says a crazed casing may go a month or may go in a week. A.L.*

She read it twice at the post-tray. Then she took it to Sorle's study and put it on the slate he was working at, over the chalk.

"Sooner," she said.

He read it. They went through it aloud, and she could not make the sums come out another way. The key was in Dunmore's drawer and Dunmore never left his desk but for the Feast. The Fifth was never empty but on Assay Night. The plug wanted the survey Lucan had not finished copying. And she could not yet give a drop of brine back out of her hand.

"Assay Night is not the convenient night," Sorle said. "It is the first one. I am sorry, Miss Vail."

She went to see her father that afternoon.

He looked up when she came along the pans. He stopped raking, which he had never done for her in her life, and stood with the rake in his hand in the steam and waited.

"Da."

"Hes."

She had come with a speech. It went out of her head at the sight of him. He looked three weeks older.

"Agathe says you came to the desk," she said.

"Aye."

"You had the pen."

"Aye."

"Why didn't you sign?"

He drew the rake through the crust to the far rim and back, and knocked it on the iron, and did not answer for so long she thought he would not. The steam came up between them and went away.

"Pen was heavy," he said.

She waited. She had learned waiting from Callow.

"Lad put the form in front of me and I read it. All of it, every line. Takes me a while." He looked at the pan, not at her. "There's a box at the bottom. *Signed for the family.* And above it, where they put who signed before, *H. Vail.* Your hand. Square, like you do it. Eleven year old, that ink, and still black." He laid the rake across the rim. "And I sat there and I thought: she put him in on her own, at twenty-two, with her mam a month buried and me on the stair with my mouth shut. And now I'll take him out on my own, and she'll come up the street on the first and find him gone. That's two of us did it alone, and him in the middle. That's no way to finish a thing. You don't carry half a load and call it delivered." He looked at his burned hands. "Your name's on the first one, Hes. The last one wants both. I'll not sign it without you. And you'll not keep him without me, neither. Not any more."

She could not speak for a moment.

"I said a hard thing to you," he said. "At the Drowned Man."

"It was true."

"Aye. Didn't make it kind." He was quiet a while. "Agathe says he's wet again. She says he was dry four days."

"He was. Somebody dried him who'd no right to, for my sake. I had him put back."

He looked at her a long time through the steam. Then he nodded once, as if she had told him the price of coal and it was what he'd expected.

"Then there's summat you mean to do."

"Yes. And I need to ask you something."

He waited.

"How do you let a pan down slow?"

She thought he might laugh, or tell her to go away. He wiped his hands on his apron, and when he spoke it was in a voice she had not heard from him since he had taught her to read the tide-marks on the harbour wall.

"First thing is you listen to her. Before you touch anything. Every pan talks different. A pan with muck in the bottom ticks, small and quick, like a clock. A pan with air under the iron where the bed's cracked thumps. Slow. Deep. Like a heart. The one wants the fire down quick and the other'll crack her iron if you do it quick. So you stand there and you count and you listen, and you don't do anything at all for as long as you can stand, and longer.

"Then you go to the fire-door. You don't open it wide. You open her a hand, and you wait for her to answer. She'll go louder first. Always does. That's the bit that frightens the young lads, and they drop the lid. She goes louder because she's felt the change and she's not sure of it yet. You let her be louder. You stand there and let her shout at you. Then she goes quieter, a bit, and then you draw the first coals. A shovel. Lay them on the brick to the side where you can get them back if you need them. And she'll go louder again, not so loud, and quieter again, and you draw the next. Each time you wait till she's said all she's going to say before you take the next shovel. You hurry her and she'll go up all at once, and then it doesn't matter how careful you've been.

"And the last thing." He looked down at his left hand, at the two shiny stiff fingers. "When she's talking, you don't stand over her. You stand to the side, with your face out of it. Because you're letting something out, Hes, and it's coming out where it wants, not where you want. You've given it the road. You don't get to pick which way it looks when it comes up." He picked the rake up. "That's how. Took me forty year and two fingers. You can have it for nowt."

She stood in the heat with her eyes stinging, and told herself it was the steam.

"Ta," she said.

"Hes." He did not look at her. "Whatever it is. Stand to the side."

"I will."

She did not tell him about the Assay.

* * *

The release was the thing she could not do, and there were twelve days to learn it.

Sorle could not teach her. He said so at once. "I am a theorist with indifferent hands. I could no more teach you a release than teach you to whistle."

Crisp's release hour had got her, since Fallow, to a drop half back on a good night. Only one person had ever got a whole release out of her: on the White, with Lucan's casing hanging in the rim of the sink, a low Lowside voice in her ear. *Don't push it out, chick. Open your hand.*

She would not go to Orsolya. She went instead to the Pans on the Crown every night at the eighth bell, when the rose and green water was black under the stars and nobody else would be fool enough to be out in the cold. She knelt at the edge with a flask of live brine and a dish. She kept a drop, and returned it. Kept a drop—

And it would not come back. It sat in her palm, clear and round as a seed pearl, and she could feel herself holding it, all of her clamped round it as her fist clamped round her purse in a Lowside crowd. She knew the theory. Every time she reached the place where she should have let go, something in her said *not yet, it'll be lost*, and closed.

On the fourth night Teo came out and sat on the rim of the pan beside her with a blanket round both their shoulders and watched her fail for an hour.

"What are you thinking about," he said finally, "when you try?"

"The drop."

"No, you're not. You're thinking about what happens if you let go and it isn't there any more. You're not holding the drop. You're holding the not-losing it." He pulled the blanket tighter. "At the Abbey, at Vigil, you carried a candle cupped in both hands down to the water-stair, and at the bottom you let the wind take the flame. The novices always held their hands round it to the last second and then snatched them away, and the flame went out because they'd snatched, not because they'd let go. The old sisters did it like this." He held his hands out over the black water, cupped, and then, slowly, turned them palm-up and flat, and opened his fingers. "You don't take your hands away from it. You take your hands away from *round* it. It's still in your palms. It just isn't trapped."

"Did you ever manage it?"

"Once. In my last year. I'd stopped believing the sea was coming back by then." He smiled, a little crooked. "Maybe that's why I could. I wasn't trying to keep anything for it any more."

She kept a drop. She did not try to let go of it. She thought of Teo's hands turning over in the dark, and her father standing to the side of the pan with his face out of it. She opened her hand: not away from the drop, but out from round it, flat, as you hold your palm out to a horse.

The drop went soft. It slid down the crease of her palm, live brine, warm for an instant with the tiny shove of its own arrears, and dripped off the side of her hand into the pan, and was water among water.

"Again," she said.

She did it eleven times more that night, and failed on seven. On the ninth night she failed on two, and afterward, in R-41, she took down the two dishes that had stood on her shelf since Russet with her first two kept drops in them, both from the Pans. She held her hand over them a long time. Then she put them back on the shelf. *Not yet.*

On the eleventh night Sorle came out onto the Crown in two coats with a kept moth from his desk, a brown one six years in its casing, and stood over her while she returned it. It sat on her palm for the length of a breath, stunned, with the faint heat of six years coming off it, and then opened its wings and went up off her hand into the dark, toward the Lantern.

The rush came after it: a racing in her chest, a heat in her face the frost could not touch, the stars too sharp. Sorle knelt, stiffly, and tipped his flask into her dish over her wet hand, and laid his own hand flat on hers in the brine. It was cold, and very still.

The racing slowed. She felt it go out of her as warmth goes out of a cup.

"That is how," said Sorle. "Physic does it at the bath when a returner's heart runs away with him. Still runs to quick, Miss Vail. It runs into a racing heart as well as into a quickening." He took his hand away and looked at it. "It is not free. It is merely shared."

She thought: one moth. She thought of the east gallery, fourteen thornes, and the black water at the Silent Supper, and the Founders' Shaft going up through six hundred feet of salt to an iron lid in the dark.

She thought of Tib's hand, curled on his chest, holding its pencil.

Then she filled her flask from the pan and went in, because it was the nineteenth of Rime, and in two days she would have to do it in front of the whole city, and then again in the dark, where it counted.
