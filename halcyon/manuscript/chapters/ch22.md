# Chapter 22

## The Midwinter Assay

> Discern. Keep. Return.
>
> — *The Assay formula, spoken by the presiding Reader before each candidate*

The Hall of Assay held eleven hundred people, and on the twenty-first of Rime it held more.

Hester had seen it empty, in Russet, on the tour. A dome of carved salt rose from the First Fathom floor so high that the light-shafts let into it from the Lantern were only threads at the top. Galleries ran round it in three tiers. Twelve chandeliers hung on chains as thick as her wrist, salt grown by the Lattice-masons over years on wire frames into branching trees of crystal. Empty, the hall had been dark, and had smelled of cold and old dust.

Full, it smelled of wet wool and oranges and people, and it was lit.

At the first bell the Glaziers had gone up into the dome on rope-cradles and dissolved a measure of kept noon into each chandelier's heart, and the light had gone out along the crystal limbs. It ran from cube to cube and broke on every face. The twelve trees hung burning over the hall, white-gold, and threw on the packed galleries and the upturned faces a light like nothing on the Rim in winter: the light of a particular afternoon in Seedmonth, warm and slanted, with a faint sound in it, if you listened, of bees.

Hester sat on the aspirants' benches on the hall floor with the rest of the Turning Intake in grey, and listened to the bees, and was afraid.

Agathe's letter had come up in the first-bell post-bag. It was inside her gown now, folded small against her breastbone.

*My dear. Jem Corrie came back on the nineteenth, near the tenth bell. The crazing went right through him all at once and he was out on the plinth before I could get Mr. Pell. He knew me, I think. He was gone by the half. Nell is holding, tide willing. I have oiled her more than I should. Do not come. Do what you must. A.L.*

The nineteenth. She had been on the Pans that night, opening her hand over a moth. She had had Agathe's first letter on the ninth, *the Corrie twins have begun to craze*, and had sat in Sorle's study with his slates and chosen the twenty-first anyway, because the Assay emptied the Fathoms and no other night did. Twelve days. She had done the sum, and Jem Corrie was the remainder. She had shown the letter to no one. It was hers to carry.

She had sat on a customs stool for fourteen years and never been frightened of a grade in her life. But she had never graded anything before a thousand people. The galleries were full to the rail: Upper Ward furs, Exchequer black, and Lowside in its Sunday coats, packed into the top tier where tickets were free, leaning over the rail with oranges. Children sat on shoulders. Three Proctors stood at the doors in their dark-blue capes trying to look as if this happened every day, which, once a year, it did.

And on the dais at the hall's east end, under the biggest chandelier, the panel.

There were five of them at a long table draped in grey. Reader Crisp, in the centre, presiding, small and upright. Reader Bassano on her left, in a gown that was technically grey and actually silver. Deputy Chancellor Vey on her right, smooth, attentive, with a slight smile that he seemed to have set at the first bell and would take off at the ninth. Warden Thane at the end.

And at the table's other end, in a chair a little apart from it, the Chancellor.

Hester had seen Perpetua Holm twice before, both times at a distance, both times sitting. She was very old and very small, swaddled in grey wool to the chin, with a white cap and white hands folded in her lap. Her face had the pale glaze of a Settled worker's face, like a pot that had been fired too often. She had not moved since the hall filled: not her hands, not her head, not her pale eyes, fixed on some point in the middle of the hall floor. If it had not been for the faint lift of the wool at her breast, every half-minute or so, slow as a tide, you would have taken her for one of the Quiet Rooms' keepings, set out on the dais for the occasion.

Orsolya was looking at the aspirants' benches. Hester did not look back.

* * *

They went in order of name. Anselm was the third called.

Teo walked up the dais steps in his grey gown with the green cord at the shoulder and his old Tider shawl folded underneath it, where nobody could see it but Hester. He looked, going up, like a man walking to the gallows who has decided to be polite to the hangman. He turned and bowed to the panel. Crisp spoke the formula, *Discern, keep, return*, in her dry small voice that somehow reached the top tier. The Assay began.

Discernment was a tray of seven grains in seven silver dishes, set by the panel. The candidate named each aloud for grade, depth and provenance, and a Proctor with a ledger wrote it down. Most aspirants named grade and depth and stopped, sweating. Teo did not taste. Teo never tasted. He put his fingertip to each dish, and bent his head close, and went into a light hold, and *listened*. Then he straightened up and told the panel, in his warm, slightly apologetic voice, what each grain had heard on the day it set. The sixth dish had been taken from a pan on the Harl margin in a hailstorm; you could hear it on the tiles. The second had been kept, briefly, round a dead bird. The fourth had come up out of the Fifth Fathom, and there was singing in it. *Choir singing, I think, Reader. Low. They were holding something.*

Bassano laughed aloud and applauded, alone, until Crisp looked at him.

Then the keeping. A Proctor lifted the cover off a jar, and a brown moth climbed out onto its rim. When it flew, the candidate was to keep it in the air with a flick of live brine, and then, when the panel had examined the casing, return it and let it go.

Teo's hold was the worst in the Turning Intake. Crisp had once said in class that holding with Anselm was like trying to frame a picture of a fountain. When the moth flew he flicked the brine and missed, and the hall drew one breath. He flicked again and caught it. The moth went still in the air with one wing kept and one half-kept, crooked, a casing like a broken lantern. It dropped into the dish with a click. The panel bent over it. Crisp's mouth went thin.

Then Teo put his fingertip to the casing and returned it, and his return was beautiful. It was easy and complete, like a man setting down a cup. The moth sat up in the dish with both wings whole and went off the dais toward the chandeliers, and the hall let out its breath in a long sigh.

"Assayed," said Crisp.

It was the lowest pass. Teo bowed to the panel as if he had been crowned, and came down the steps grinning so widely that Hester, who had not smiled all morning, found that she was.

* * *

It went on. It went on all morning and into the afternoon. The kept-spring light from the chandeliers did not change. Hester's backside went numb on the salt bench, and her hands went cold, and she drank from her flask until it was empty and then sat with it in her lap.

Ferrers was called in the early afternoon. Lucan went up with his black cord at the shoulder, new enough that the knot still sat stiffly. In the second tier, among the Exchequer men, a tall grey-haired man in black sat forward and did not take his eyes from him. Lucan did not look up at that tier once. He discerned all seven grains for grade and depth and named two of them by the pit they had come from. He kept the moth in a casing so precise and so thin that you could see the dust on its wings through it, and returned it cleanly. When Crisp said "Assayed with distinction," the man in black stood up slowly and clapped three times, and sat down. Lucan came down the steps with his face like a shut door. When he passed the bench he caught Hester's eye, and for an instant the door was open on something that looked more like grief than triumph.

Orme was called after the fifth bell. Saskia walked up the steps as she walked into the kilns, briskly, rolling her sleeves. She named the grains in her flat Quell voice as if she were pricing them. Her keeping was the best of the day. She did not flick the brine at the moth. She held the flask still and let the moth fly into the drops, and the casing closed round it as smooth as poured glass, and the light from the chandeliers went into it and came out the other side in colours. People in the galleries said *oh* aloud. Bassano put both hands to his heart. Her return was neat and dry. "With distinction," said Crisp, and Saskia nodded to her as one tradeswoman to another. Then, coming down, she looked up at the top tier, where a small fair-haired girl was standing on the rail with her grandmother's arms round her middle, waving with both hands. Saskia waved back once, briefly, with her whole face changed.

Then there were others. The light did not change. The bees went on.

At the eighth bell the Proctor with the ledger said, "Vail. Hester. Bench of Reader Sorle," and she stood up.

* * *

Her knees were stiff. She went up the dais steps carefully, as if they were the six steps down to the charity ward, worn into dishes in the middle.

At the top she turned and bowed to the panel, the way the others had. Crisp looked at her without expression. Bassano smiled. Vey's set smile did not alter. The Chancellor did not move.

Orsolya looked at her.

Hester had known she would. She had spent nineteen days making herself ready for it, on the Pans every night while the drops failed to leave her palm. It was still worse than she had prepared for. Not accusing, and not pleading: the look Orsolya had given her on the Causeway on the first morning, with the salt-mud on her boots and her hands tucked under her arms against the cold. *You're Aske's privilege. Well done, chick.* It was warm, and tired, and proud of her.

Hester turned to the tray.

"Discern," said Crisp. "Keep. Return."

There were seven silver dishes. She rinsed her mouth from the jug the Proctor held, and spat into the basin, and took up the horn spoon from the rack. Behind her, eleven hundred people went quiet. It was the quiet of an assay room at the second bell before Daisy came in. She found she had stopped being afraid.

She tasted the first grain.

"Shelf," she said. "Two fathoms. Southern margin. Somebody's dried it in an oven, and too fast. It's lost its sweet at the corners." The Proctor's pen scratched. "Second. Middling. Thirty fathoms. Halcyon pits, the north face. It's been out of the ground a month."

She went down the tray. Third, Deep at ninety, old, with a tiredness in it that meant someone had kept something in it once and returned it, and she said so. The Proctor's pen stopped. Crisp said "Go on" without inflection. Fourth, dead salt: Varrow rock, put in to trap the unwary. Fifth, Middling, Western Rim, perhaps fifteen. Sixth, Deep at two hundred, from the Halcyon's own reference cupboard, so clean it was almost a pleasure.

The seventh dish was on the end of the tray nearest Orsolya.

Each dish carried a small brass tag on its rim with the setter's initial, face-down. Hester did not turn the tag over. She did not need to. She put the spoon into the seventh dish and lifted a single crystal, white with a little blue in it, sharp-cornered, warm. Warm through the horn. She put it on her tongue.

The weight came down behind her eyes like a door closing.

Fourteen thornes. Sweet to the edge of bitter. Under the sweetness, bright and wet and metallic, the bitten tongue, the startle: quick. And under that, so faint she would not have caught it in Russet, a sound she had not known in Russet. A long low rushing, rising and falling, and the rise coming again before the fall had done.

She put the spoon down in the rack.

"Seventh," she said. Her voice came out clear to the top tier, and she let it. "Hush-grade grain. Quickened. Below the Fifth Fathom, and pushed up into it. It was taken from a stay in the east gallery of the Fifth, within the last week." She lifted her eyes from the tray. "There's sea in it."

The hall did not understand what she had said. She could hear it not understanding: a mutter in the galleries, a rustle, the word *Hush* going along the top tier where the Lowside people were. On the dais, the five of them understood perfectly.

Vey's smile came off. It did not slip; it was taken off, cleanly, and put away. Bassano looked at Crisp. Crisp looked at the brass tag on the seventh dish, and turned it over, and looked at it. Then she looked down the table at Orsolya.

"The east gallery," said Crisp. "Within the week. Vail, that is a level of specificity I would not expect from a Fellow of twenty years. How can you possibly—"

"It tastes of it, Reader."

"She's right," said Orsolya.

Her voice was quiet, Lowside and level, and it carried. "Taken the fourteenth. East gallery, stay nine. I set it." She was still looking at Hester. "Mark it, Maudelaine. She's right."

Crisp looked from one to the other of them for a long moment. Then she nodded to the Proctor with the ledger, and the pen scratched.

At the far end of the table, the Chancellor had not moved. But her eyes were no longer on the middle of the hall floor. They were on Hester. Pale, and wet, and very old, and fixed.

"Keep," said Crisp.

* * *

The Proctor brought the jar and lifted its cover. The moth climbed out onto the rim. It was brown, like all of them, with a darker bar across each forewing. It sat in the burning spring light and opened and closed its wings, and Hester took up the flask of live brine and the dish and stood with them, and went into the hold.

That part was easy. That part had always been easy. Breath slow. Heart slow. Down and down, silt settling in a glass. The hall went far away. The bees went far away. There was the moth, and the brine in her hand, and the deep square stillness in her that Crisp had once called a miser's. Hester had come to think of it as simply her own: the stillness of a woman who had stood at the end of a fourth row once a month for eleven years and not touched anything.

The moth flew.

She did not flick. She had watched Saskia. She lifted the flask and let the brine go in a short flat arc across the moth's path, and the moth flew into it, and she *held*. The brine closed on it in the air. The casing came down round it dense and squared, like a stone fitted in a wall, and the moth hung for an instant in the light, a brown thing in a clear cube. Then it dropped into the dish with a sound like a dropped coin.

The panel bent to it. Bassano took out a glass. Crisp looked a long time.

"Dense," said Crisp. "Squared. Heavy-handed." She sat back. "Not a flaw in it. Return."

Hester set the dish on the table in front of her and put out her right hand, palm down, and laid her fingertips on the casing.

This was the part. She had known it would be, since the first day at the Pans in Russet, when the drop had sat in her palm and stayed there and Crisp had said *You hold like a miser, Vail*, loud enough for Lucan Ferrers to smirk. She could feel it already: the clench. Everything in her that had been settling and stilling began, at the touch of the casing, to close. Not yet. It'll be lost. If you let go of a thing, it isn't there any more.

She thought of Teo on the rim of the pan in the dark, turning his hands over.

She thought of her father standing to the side, with his face out of it.

She did not take her hand away. She turned it. Slowly, in front of eleven hundred people, her right hand turned over on the casing until it was lying under it rather than over it, palm up, as you hold your hand out to a horse. She opened her fingers. Not away from it. Out from round it.

The casing went soft. It ran. It was brine in her palm, warm for a moment with the small shove of the moth's arrears. The moth sat in the middle of her hand in the wet, a brown moth with a dark bar on each wing, and she felt the rush come up in her, small, a flush and a quickening of the heart. The light seemed for an instant too bright and the bees too loud.

The moth opened its wings. It shut them. It opened them.

Then it went up off her palm in its jerky, stupid, moth's flight, up over the dais and the panel, toward the nearest chandelier, toward the light. It went up and up until it was a speck among the burning crystal limbs, and then she could not see it at all.

Someone in the top tier began clapping: hard hands that had raked a pan for forty years, slapping together slow and loud and alone. Hester looked up, and saw her father standing at the rail of the top tier in a coat she had never seen before. It was a good dark coat, brushed, too tight in the shoulders, and he was clapping with his face set and his chin up as if he were daring the whole of Ambry to tell him to sit down.

Daisy Fenn was beside him with a baby on her hip and four children round her knees, and she began to clap too. Then the Lowside tier, and then the second tier, and then, for a moment, the whole hall.

"Assayed," said Crisp, over it, in her small voice that reached the roof, "with distinction."

* * *

She came down the steps without feeling them. Teo caught her at the bottom by both arms and said something she did not hear. Saskia, on the bench, gave her a short nod. Lucan, two places along, did not look at her at all, which she understood to be his version of the same.

She sat. Her palm was still wet. She closed her hand on it.

When she looked up at the dais again, Orsolya was looking at her.

The hall was loud round them. Through all of it the Warden of the Lower Fathoms sat at the end of the panel table with her cold hands folded in front of her and looked down at Hester Vail across forty feet of salt floor and a winter's worth of lies. Hester looked back.

Orsolya knew. Hester saw her know it. It was in the slackening of her face, the way a sail slackens when the wind drops: not surprise, and not anger. Orsolya had seen her name the seventh dish and seen her open her hand. She knew there would be no answer to her offer, because the answer had been given, out loud, in front of the city. Whatever Hester meant to do, Hester had decided to do it.

Then Crisp said "Warden?" for the second time, about the next candidate's tray, and Orsolya turned her head and answered, and it was over.

* * *

The Assay ended at the ninth bell. Crisp spoke the closing formula, and the Glaziers went up in the rope-cradles and let the chandeliers run out. The spring light drained along the crystal limbs and went, and the hall seemed small and cold. Then the great doors at the west end were opened onto the Gatehouse yard, and the smell of roasting came in, and the noise of Ambry on the other side of the Line, and the Feast began.

The Long Table under the Lantern Well was laid from end to end, three hundred feet, with every Reader and Fellow and aspirant in the Halcyon crammed along both sides. In the Gatehouse yard there were trestles and braziers for the porters and cooks and delvers, roast mutton and saltcake by the yard and Harl cider in barrels, and the Lowside Bench singing a ballad about the sea, very badly. Above it all the Lantern's mirrors stood still, turned to the night, and down the Well came the last of the year's kept midsummer, gold, poured out by the Glaziers in honour of the Assayed.

The Choir came up from the Stays in its grey wool at the tenth bell and was cheered along the Long Table. Hester counted them as they came. Fifty-one. She knew the Choir's roll: sixty-two. Eleven left below, then. A skeleton watch, as Saskia had said.

Orsolya came up with them. She sat at the Readers' end of the table between Crisp and the Chancellor's empty chair, and did not eat, and did not look down the table.

Aldous found Hester in the yard. He had his good coat buttoned to the throat and a cup of cider he had not drunk, and he stood in front of her among the braziers and did not seem to know what to do with either of them.

"Didn't know you could do that," he said.

"Nor did I. Till this week."

"With the moth." He turned his hand over in the air, palm up, clumsily. "That."

"You taught me."

He looked at her. He looked down at the cider. Then he drank the whole of it in one swallow and gave her the empty cup, as if that were a thing a man did, and said, "Stand to the side," and went off through the gate toward the Causeway without looking back.

At the half bell after the tenth, Lucan and Teo went out through the gate with a crowd of Ambry people going home. Teo had a sack over his shoulder that clanked once when he set it down to pull his collar up. Lucan, among the furs and black coats, was suddenly only another Exchequer man's son walking his friend into town. Neither of them looked back at her. Teo, at the gate, lifted one hand above his head without turning round, the fingers open.

Saskia had gone a quarter of an hour before. She had kissed Pip goodnight at the gate and walked back in along the First Fathom past the Long Table. As she passed the clerks' benches she looked at a gap in them: an empty place halfway down, where a plate of mutton sat untouched with a knife and fork laid neatly across it. Hester had seen her see it.

Then Saskia had gone down the Stair.

Hester stood in the yard with her father's empty cup in her hand and the brazier-smoke in her eyes. Her palm was dry now, but she could still feel the place in the middle of it where the moth had sat. Against her breastbone, Agathe's letter had gone warm.

At the eleventh bell, Sorle came out of the Gatehouse door in his black gown with a lantern and a canvas satchel over his shoulder. The satchel was full of slates, and they knocked together as he walked. He looked at her across the yard, and did not need to speak.

She set the cup down on the edge of a trestle, and went in.
