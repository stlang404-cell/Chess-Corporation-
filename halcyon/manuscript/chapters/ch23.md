# Chapter 23

## The Old Tap

> Take her off the heat slow.
>
> — *Saying of the Lowside brine-boilers*

The Stair was empty all the way down.

On ordinary nights there was always someone on it. Tonight the whole Halcyon was up at the Long Table or out in the Gatehouse yard. The noise of the Feast came down the Well behind them, fainter at every turn, until by the Third Fathom it was only a murmur, like the sea in the Roar. Then it was nothing.

Sorle went ahead with the lantern. He took the steps one at a time, his free hand trailing along the wall, over the chalk-names of the Settled. Hester came behind with the satchel of slates, which he had let her carry after he had stopped twice to breathe. Neither of them spoke. On the Second Fathom she passed one that was newer than the rest and still sharp at the edges, in her own square hand. AMA TESSALY, CHOIR, 903.

At the Fourth they left the Stair. The Choir hall opened out to their left, black and empty. On a bench near the bottom tier someone had left a grey wool scarf. Sorle looked at it as they passed and did not stop.

Saskia was waiting at the foot of the box-lift, as she had said she would be.

The box-lift was the porters' cage at the back of the Choir hall. It ran from the Fifth to the Third, with a gate here at the Fourth. Three nights a week all summer, at the tenth bell, it had carried sealed grain boxes up from the Stays to the Third Fathom landing, where Saskia had met it with a barrow on Edwin Dunmore's pass. Saskia sat on an upturned crate beside it with her sleeves still rolled from the Assay, a shuttered lantern at her feet and a canvas sack across her knees. She stood and held out her hand, palm up, and on it was a black iron key longer than her hand, with a little bird sitting on a line on its bow.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"He's sixty-four," said Hester.

"I am present," said Sorle, "and sixty-four and a half. Was there any difficulty?"

"No." Saskia closed her hand on the key again. "That's what I don't like." She looked at Hester. "His desk was locked. I had the drawer open in a count of twenty. The ring was there, *W.L.F. Duplicates* on the tag. The key was there. And his chair was empty, and his coat was gone off the hook. And his plate at the Long Table hadn't been touched."

"Dunmore doesn't miss a Feast," said Hester.

"Dunmore doesn't miss anything." Saskia hitched the sack onto her shoulder; it clinked. "And the key to the Hush was sitting in his drawer where anyone could take it. So either he's stupid tonight, which he isn't, or he's somewhere else with something better to do."

They looked at each other in the lantern-light. Nobody said *Orsolya*.

"Well," said Sorle. "We knew we should not have the night to ourselves. We merely hoped." He held the lantern up to the lift-cage. "In we go."

* * *

The Fifth Fathom was warm.

The Fifth was warm as a cowshed is warm, damply, as if something large were breathing in it. The walls sweated. The salt underfoot was tacky, and their boots made a faint sucking sound on it. Brass stay-lines ran along the walls at shoulder height in sheaths green with age, and where they passed into the salt to feed a stay, the joint was beaded with brine like a glass of cold beer on a summer table.

Far off along the galleries to the west, people were singing.

Eleven voices, Hester thought, low and slow. The skeleton watch, holding the west stays from the Warden's Gallery. The song came along the stay-lines more than through the air; she could feel it in the brass when she put her palm flat on it. *Hold the line, hold the line.* It went on and on, patient, like a woman rocking a cradle in another room.

"This way," said Saskia. "East. Service passage. Nobody comes this way at night."

The service passage was narrow and low and smelled of weed. They went along it in single file. The warmth grew and the singing faded behind them, and another sound came up under it, out of the salt itself. Hester had heard it first in the *Wren* grain, at the Customs House, in another life: a long, low rushing. It rose and fell, and the rise came again before the fall had finished.

"Do you hear that?" said Saskia, low.

"Yes," said Hester.

"I never heard that in the summer."

"No," said Sorle. "I don't suppose it was so loud in the summer."

* * *

The passage came out at the lowest gallery of the Fifth, at the foot of a short flight of steps cut by the founders: shallow, worn into dishes, much older than the Stair. At the bottom the gallery widened into a round chamber. On its far side was the Hush door.

Hester had imagined it many times, and wrongly. It was a door a little taller than a man, set flush in the salt wall. But it was made of salt, lattice-grown so fine and so thick that it was nearly white, like frost on a window, and grown in the shape of the Halcyon's emblem, the bird with its wings folded, sitting on a single straight wave. In the centre of the wave, at the height of Hester's breast, was a keyhole of plain dark iron.

And to the left of the door, coming out of the floor and going into the wall, was the Old Tap.

She knew it from Aske's small sloping hand. A pipe of founders' brass as thick as her thigh, pitted and green-black, rose out of the chamber floor, bent once, and went into the wall beside the Hush door. At the bend was a valve-head the size of a cartwheel. Its spokes were cast in the shape of fishes, worn smooth by centuries of hands. The green on the spokes was rubbed through to bright brass in four places, recently, where hands had been.

From the valve-head a newer pipe ran off along the gallery wall toward the west, brass still yellow under its varnish, to join the stay-lines. The eastern trunk. The line from the Cold House came up out of the floor here and fed every stay in the Fifth.

Hester put the satchel down. She went to the Old Tap and laid her palm on the great curve of the pipe below the valve, slowly, as you lay a hand on a sleeping animal.

It was cold. It was the only cold thing in the Fifth.

There was a tasting-cock at the bend, a small founders' tap the size of a thimble with a fish for a handle, for drawing samples from the line. Someone had used it often; the fish was bright. She turned it a quarter-turn. A bead of brine swelled at its lip, clear, and hung. She took it on her fingertip and put it on her tongue.

Forty thornes.

The weight came down behind her eyes not like a door but like a mountain: the stillness of three hundred people lying in rows under high windows, drawn out of them through the dark. In it, under it, fainter than anything, she could taste—not taste, there was no word, there would never be a word—something that was not stillness at all, but was in the stillness as a voice is in a room. A boy who had whistled. A pencil.

She stood with her hand on the pipe and her finger in her mouth like a child.

"Hester," said Saskia, quite gently, behind her.

"I know." She took her finger out of her mouth and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, and was not surprised to find salt on it. "It's his. It's all of them." She turned. "It's not twelve yet."

Sorle had his watch open under the lantern, a fat Hask silver turnip that had been his wife's father's.

"Seven minutes before the half," he said. "Something over half a bell before Mr. Ferrers puts his saw to it, if Mr. Ferrers keeps time, which he will. He is the kind of young man who arrives early to his own funeral." He snapped the watch shut. "Mrs. Orme. The key."

* * *

The key turned stiffly, with a long grinding sound in the lattice. It went round once and stuck, and Saskia leaned on it with both hands and her whole weight, swearing in Quellish, and it went round again. Something deep in the door let go with a soft heavy click, like a bone going back into its socket.

The door swung inward on no hinge she could see. And the hum came out.

She had felt it in Russet, on the tour, as a faint pressure in her back teeth. It came out now through the open door and went straight into the bones of her face. It was quiet, and it was in her jaw and her breastbone and the roots of her hair. The lantern-glass sang faintly with it. Sorle put his hand to his ear and took it away.

"Well," he said. His voice was not quite steady. "There you are."

They went in.

The Hush was not a room. It was a floor.

It went back beyond the lantern's reach in every direction, flat and level, its low roof held up here and there by pillars of lattice-grown salt, white and slender, like birch trees in a winter wood. Old founders' brass with fish-head joints ran pillar to pillar and across the floor in channels, and every channel was dry. Here and there something brass glinted that she did not understand at all: a wheel, a great ring set in the floor, a bank of small handles like the stops of an organ.

The floor was salt. Not white salt. Clear.

Saskia had stopped. She was holding her lamp out at arm's length and looking down, and Hester looked down too, and stood still.

The light went into the floor as it would go into clear water, and it did not stop. It went down a fathom, two fathoms, more, and the salt below them was shot through with the fine planes of its crystals like the grain in a sheet of glass. Below the lantern's last reach, where the light failed, the dark had a shape.

She could not have said what shape. It was too large to be a shape: a darkness that had edges, far down, and the edges went on past where the light could follow. Rock would have been grey. This was black, and it was long, and at one place, where Saskia's lamp swung, Hester thought she saw a line along it, ruled, as straight as a thing that had been made. Then the lamp swung back and it was only dark.

"What is that?" said Saskia. Her voice was hardly a breath.

Sorle did not answer. He was standing beside them with the satchel of slates over his shoulder, looking down. His lips moved.

"Benedek," said Hester.

He did not move.

"Benedek. *Don't.*"

He looked up at her. In the lantern-light his face was naked. He had been looking for something under the salt for thirty-two years, and they had laughed at him in Conclave for it. Now it was under his feet. He could have stood there till he Settled.

"Not now," said Hester. "After. I swear to you. After, we'll come back with every lamp in the Halcyon and you can look till you're blind. Not now."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and took his eyes off the floor, and she saw what it cost him.

"East," he said. "The survey says east."

* * *

They heard the quickening before they saw it.

The rushing in the salt had been growing all across the Hush floor, rising under the hum until it was louder than the hum. Then there was a smell, and it was the smell from the Silent Supper: cold weed, green and alive, nothing rotten in it. The salt underfoot went from dry to tacky to wet. Then the lantern found the east wall.

It was wet from roof to floor. Not beaded, running. Brine came down the salt in thin glittering sheets and pooled in the old channels at the foot of the wall, and the channels were not dry any more. The wall was warm to the hand. It *moved*. When she put her palm on it she could feel it under her skin, a slow thrust and fall, like the side of a horse when it breathes. And the sound came out of it, and it was the sea: waves breaking on something a long way off, and wind, and once, far and faint, a cry she knew. Two notes, and a third that cracked.

Fixed into the wall at shoulder height was a great stay-cock of founders' brass. A founders' line came to it from the Old Tap, all the way across the Hush roof, to pour the trunk's stillness into the face of the quickening. The cock was open. The pipe was cold. Where it went into the salt the wall was not wet but dry, a great dry fan spread out round the joint like a hand laid flat over a mouth.

A lid, Hester thought. A lid with a hand on it.

"There," said Saskia.

The Founders' Shaft was ten yards along the wall to the north, at the foot of it. A collar of bronze as wide as a cart-wheel, green with age, was set upright into the salt at the floor, and filling the collar, flush with it, was the plug: a disc of lattice-grown salt, white, smooth as a millpond and hard as marble. Behind it, the survey said, a short adit ran six yards into the wall and turned straight up, six hundred feet through the dome to the surface, a quarter-mile east of the Lantern, to a lid with a chain on it.

"Six feet thick," said Sorle. "Per the survey."

"Per the survey," said Saskia, "the founders were honest men."

She swung the sack off her shoulder and opened it. In it were two crowbars, a mallet, her glazier's chisel and a copper pan with a lid. Packed in sacking were six of the Lumen kilns' heat-jars, salt kept round a summer afternoon at the furnace mouth, which were not supposed to leave the kilns. Saskia unstoppered the first into the copper pan with a dipper of live brine from Hester's flask, and the summer let go into the brine with a soft cough, and steam came up off the pan in the warm dark.

"Well?" she said. "It won't dissolve itself."

* * *

It would not dissolve at all.

Sorle's slates said they need not go through it: free the seam where nine centuries had grown the plug into its collar, all round the rim, and the plug would come out like a cork. He had allowed an hour for it. They poured the hot brine into the seam and it ran off. They poured it again, slower, a cupful at a time into a notch Hester cut with the point of the crowbar, and it hissed and ate in a little and stopped. Lattice-grown salt was not mined salt. It had been grown on purpose, crystal locked into crystal along a worker's template, and it was as hard as a thing is when somebody has meant it to be.

So they did it the slow way. Hot brine into the notch; the chisel into the soft; the bar behind the chisel. Lever, crack, prise, and a flake of white came away no bigger than a coin. Hot brine again. Saskia worked the pan and the jars and set the chisel for each cut with a glazier's tap of the mallet. Hester lay on her side in the wet and worked the bar, because she had the arms for it; she had raked pans at ten and carried crates for twenty years, and the old work came back into her shoulders as if it had been waiting. Sorle held the lantern and read the watch and, when he could not stop himself, took the second bar, useless and stubborn, until his breath was coming in long hoarse pulls and his hands were bleeding where the iron had taken the skin. Hester took the bar off him without asking.

"Sit down."

"I will not—"

"Sit *down*, Benedek, before I keep you sitting."

He sat on the wet floor with his back against the wall, and the lantern in his lap, and the watch in his bleeding hand, and laughed. It was a short cracked sound.

"Corvin would have enjoyed this," he said. "Corvin always said I'd never done an honest day's labour."

They worked the south side first, where the seam was widest. The notch became a groove, and the groove a slot that took the bar to half its length, and she was working with her cheek on the wet salt and the steam in her eyes. The brine ran back down her sleeves to the elbow, hot and then cold. Every so often she stopped to breathe and Saskia went in with the dipper, and every so often Sorle said the time. Eleven and three-quarters. Twelve, less ten. Less five.

"Hester," said Sorle.

She came out from the plug. He was holding the watch up to the lantern.

"Twelve, less two," he said. "If you want to be at the line when it goes."

She went to the stay-cock and found its tasting-cock, and drew a bead, and put it on her tongue.

Forty thornes. The mountain. Tib.

She stood with the bead on her tongue and her eyes shut, and waited. Behind her the bar had stopped. The wall beside her breathed in and out against her shoulder, and the sea went on breaking a long way off inside the salt. Six Fathoms above her, beyond hearing, the Halcyon was ringing the twelfth bell.

"Twelve," said Sorle.

The weight changed.

It did not stop. It thinned. A door had opened very far away and a draught had come down a long corridor; she felt it go out of the stillness before she knew what it was. Thirty-five thornes. Thirty. Then faster, all at once, as if a rope had been cut and a weight had dropped off the end of it. Twenty. Ten. The coins came off her eyelids one by one, and the mountain went, and the weight behind her eyes went light, lighter, gone. On her tongue was a bead of nothing but brine. Old brine. Flat. Tasting of nothing but the pipe it had come through.

Two miles away, under Coldwater Street, a young man who arrived early to his own funeral had put his saw to a brass rod, and Teo had put his to another.

"They've cut it," she said.

Her voice did not come out as she meant it to. She found she had both hands flat on the stay-cock, holding on.

"They've cut it. It's not coming any more." She turned round. "It's *his* again. It's staying with him."

Saskia's face across the lantern was wet, from the steam or not, and she did not wipe it.

"Good," she said. "Now get back on that bar."

* * *

The quickening knew before they did.

The wall beside the stay-cock gave a long shudder under Hester's hand. The great dry fan of salt round the joint, the hand over the mouth, went dark from the edges in, as a cloth goes dark when you drop it in water. Then it was wet. Then it was running. Then, with a sound like a cork coming out of a bottle, a jet of water as thick as her finger shot out of the wall a foot from her face and hit the brass and broke into spray.

Salt water. Cold as the sea at the bottom of the world. It ran down her cheek and into her mouth, and she tasted it before she could stop herself. Not brine. Not the sweet weight of grain. It was salt, and something green, and something alive, and there was not a grain of stillness left in it. It was water that was going somewhere.

"*Hester!*"

She went back to the plug. The noise was rising behind her, the whole east wall a drum. Water ran ankle-deep in the old channels, and the founders' brass in them rang faintly, like a struck glass. The hum of the Hush had risen too, and was in her teeth so hard that her eyes watered.

"Bar," she said.

Saskia put the bar into her hands. Hester lay down in the water and drove it into the slot. The brine ran down her arms. She struck and levered and struck. Saskia poured, and poured again, and swore, and opened the last of the heat-jars into the pan without measuring, and the summer coughed out into the steam. Sorle was up off the floor again. He stood with his back to the breathing wall and the lantern high and the watch open in his other hand, saying *steady, steady, steady*, to nobody, or to all of them.

The bar went in to its full length, and something gave: a long low groan inside the plug, like a ship's timber taking strain, and the whole south side of the disc shifted under the bar by a hair and settled. A thread of cold came out of the seam over Hester's face. Air. Cold, dry, smelling of frost: air from six hundred feet above, from the open White under the midwinter stars, coming down the Founders' Shaft through a hole she could not see.

"It's through," said Saskia. "Hester, it's *through*—"

"Not enough." The thread of cold was the width of a straw. Hester put her hand on the north rim, and the north rim might have been part of the wall. "Loose this side. Not that. It'll want the whole throat."

Behind them a second jet broke from the east wall, and a third, and the sea in the salt roared.

And far above them, faint, and then not faint, a bell began to ring.

It was not any of the Halcyon's hours. It was a bell Hester had heard only once before, in Cinder, in the middle of the Minute: hard, flat, fast, the alarm from the Warden's Gallery in the Fifth. It came down the stay-lines into the Hush and rang in the brass all round them. Under it, faint, she heard the skeleton watch's song in the west galleries break off in the middle of a note. A little later it started again, ragged, louder, more voices than eleven, as the alarm went up the Stair into the Feast and the Choir came down.

Saskia looked at Hester. Hester looked at Sorle.

"The stays," said Sorle. "With the draw cut, every stay in the Fifth is starving. They'll have felt it go in the Warden's Gallery the moment it went. They'll think it's the Hush failing. They'll be right." He closed his watch. "They'll be coming down."

"Not to us," said Saskia. "To the stays. They don't know we're—"

"One of them knows," said Hester.

She knelt by the plug with the bar in her hands and the brine running down her arms and the sea breaking at her back. Across the Hush, far off, in the open door under the lattice bird, a light had come.

A lantern. Then two. Then three. They came through the door into the Hush and stopped, and swung slowly as their bearers looked out across the floor of clear salt. Then they came east. The bearers did not hurry, and they did not need to look for anyone. They walked steadily across the Hush toward the one place where anyone could be.

The first lantern was carried low, and the hand under it was steady, and the face above it was in shadow. But Hester knew the walk. She had seen it on the Causeway on the first morning, coming from Ambry with the salt-mud on its boots. She had seen it come down the gallery at the Silent Supper with the water running round its ankles, leading the Choir.

"Hester," called Orsolya Thane across the Hush.

Her voice was low and Lowside and level. It carried over the water and the hum and the sea as if they were not there.

"Hester. Chick. What have you done?"
