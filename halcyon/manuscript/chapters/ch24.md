# Chapter 24

## The Hush

> What is kept is not lost.
>
> — *Motto of the Halcyon, cut above the Hush door*

The plug moved.

It moved a finger's width, no more, with a grinding sound that Hester felt in her knees before she heard it. A breath of air came out of the crack around it, cold, smelling of frost. Sorle, on his back on the salt floor with the long crowbar braced across his chest, said something in Harlish that she had not known he knew. Saskia poured the last of the hot brine from the copper into the crack. It steamed and sank and the crack drank it.

"Again," Hester said.

"I am sixty-four years old, Miss Vail."

"Again, Reader."

He set the bar. She put her shoulder under the other end of it. For nine hundred years the plug had sat in its bronze collar and grown into it, as a stone grows into a field. They had been at it for an hour with hot brine and two bars and Saskia's glazier's chisel, and they had got it loose on one side. On the other it held.

Behind them, the Hush roared.

It had only hummed when they came in. Now the quickening at the east end had felt the sleepers' stillness stop, and woken.

She could hear it over the grinding of the bar: surf. Not the faint roar in a grain on the tongue, the sea-in-a-shell that every Reader knew. Surf, loud, close, inside the wall, as if the wall were a cliff and the sea were throwing itself at the far side of it. The whole face of the wall over the pocket was running, and the warmth came off it in a breath.

"It's climbing," Saskia said. She had her watch out, a flat brass Hask watch on a ribbon, and her lantern on it. "Seventeen minutes since the cut. It's twice as loud as it was at ten."

"I can hear it."

"I'm telling you the rate, Vail. Not the volume."

The plug moved again. The crack opened wider, a dark half-moon along its lower edge. A gust came out of it, colder, the cold of the White at night, fallen six hundred feet down the shaft. Sorle let go of the bar and lay back on the salt and breathed like a broken bellows.

"Enough," he said. "That will have to do. The water will take the rest."

"Will it?"

"If it doesn't," said Sorle, with his eyes shut, "we shall none of us be in a position to complain."

* * *

The three lanterns came on across the Hush.

She had known Orsolya would come. She had planned for it as she would have planned for the weather. The cut would show in the stays above within a quarter of an hour; the Warden would feel it in her hands before any alarm was rung. She would come down the Stair, and through the Fifth, and find the lattice standing open. Hester had known all that. She had not known what it would be like to see her.

Orsolya covered the last yards with her lantern held high. Two Fathomers in iron-grey followed her, a young man and an older woman. Behind them came Dunmore, in his coat, holding his lantern too low, as if he thought he could avoid being seen by it. Orsolya's white hair was loose. She had been asleep, or trying to be. She stopped at the top of the founders' steps at the east end, three shallow treads where the brass trunk of the stay-lines came down out of the roof, and looked at it all: the plug, the bars, Sorle on his back, Saskia with her watch. Then she looked at Hester, standing at the east wall in brine to her ankles with her sleeves rolled to the elbow.

"Oh, chick," she said. "Oh, chick."

"We cut the draw," Hester said. "That's what I've done."

"I know you've cut the draw. I've two hundred feet of stay-line up there going soft as butter. I've the east gallery of the Fifth sweating like a cheese." She came down the steps. Her voice was quite level. Her hands, Hester saw, were shaking. They were the Choir's hands, white with cold, and they were shaking. "You've killed us all. You know that? You've killed us all, and the lads in the Cistern, and your brother's folk in Lowside. When this goes, it'll not stop at the Hush. It'll not stop at the Halcyon."

"It's not going to *go*. It's going up." Hester pointed at the shaft. "That's the Founders' Shaft. It comes out at the Well-Cap, on the open White. We're giving it a road."

Orsolya looked at the black half-moon under the plug. For a moment her face showed nothing at all. Then she laughed. It was a terrible laugh, short and dry, like salt underfoot.

"A road," she said. "You've been talking to Benedek."

"I've been talking to Corvin."

That stopped her.

"I read his notebook," Hester said. "*She is right that it will flood. She is wrong that it must be held. The pressure wants a road, not a lid.* He'd worked it out. He was coming to tell you. And you kept him."

Behind Orsolya, the young Fathomer turned his head sharply and looked at his Warden. Dunmore, by the door, did not look at anyone.

"Warden," said the older Fathomer. "Warden, the gallery."

As if it had heard her, the east wall spoke.

* * *

It was not a sound so much as a change in everything at once. The surf behind the salt stopped, as a man stops between one breath and the next. In the silence the lantern flames leaned toward the wall, all of them together, every flame in the Hush bowing east. Then the skin of salt over the pocket broke.

It did not crack. It *opened*, all along a seam the height of a man, the way a fish's belly opens under a knife. Out of the seam came the sea.

It was black: not grey and not green, black as ink in the lantern-light. It was cold, colder than any water she had ever put her hands in, and it came out of the wall in a solid curved sheet and hit the clear floor of the Hush and spread. It was round her ankles and then round her shins before she had taken a step. It smelled. Oh, it smelled: weed and salt and something rich and rotten-sweet underneath, the smell of a thing that had been alive in great quantities, all together, nine hundred years ago, on the last afternoon there was a sea.

And it was alive. She knew it in her mouth before she knew it any other way. The spray hit her lips and she tasted it, and it was not grain. It was not stillsalt, not sweet, not heavy on the eyes. It was quick. Everything in it was quick. It was the taste of the bead in the crystal in the customs house and the beads on Tib's casing and the warm grain from the Fifth, all of it at once, the startle and the bitten tongue. There was nothing still in it anywhere.

Saskia was shouting. Sorle was on his feet, somehow, with his slate in one hand. The water reached the open plug and found the half-moon crack and went into it with a sound like an enormous throat swallowing. For an instant all of it went into the shaft.

Then the seam in the wall opened wider, and more came, and the shaft could not drink fast enough.

"*Hold it!*" That was Orsolya. She had come down into the water, waist-deep in it now at the bottom of the steps, and she had her hands out in front of her. She was going into the hold. Hester could feel her going, as you feel cold coming off a window, a deep old practised stillness reaching out across the water toward the seam. "Hold it, all of you, hold it shut—"

"No!" Hester was at the wall. She did not remember crossing to it. She was standing in the black water to her knees with her hands flat on the salt on either side of the open seam, and the cold of it was in her wrists like knives. "No. Don't hold it. Give it the shaft. Saskia. *Saskia.* The count."

"I know the count!" Saskia had climbed onto the founders' brass housing beside the plug, out of the water, her lantern on her watch. "On your release. Hold three, open two. Hold three—*Vail, you have to open it.*"

She had to open it.

Hester stood with her hands on the salt and the sea coming past her knees. She went into the hold, because that was where you started, and the hold came to her as it always came: deep, square, immovable, a door shutting. It was the best thing she could do. The salt under her palms answered it. She felt the seam stiffen and the water slacken through it. The stillness went out of her into the wall, the way it went into the stay-lines in the Choir, the way it had gone into the brine over Lucan's falling body on the White. The seam began to close.

"*No!*" Saskia's voice cracked. "You're holding! Vail, you're *holding* it—"

She knew. She could feel herself doing it. It was like watching her own hand close on a coin. She told it to open. It did not open. The sea pressed on the far side of the salt, all of it, the weight of nine hundred years of waiting, and her hold met it and held. The pressure climbed. She felt it climb through her palms into her wrists and her elbows and her teeth. It was not the warmth of the quickening any more. It was the arrears, the whole debt of the moment piling up behind the seam like water behind a dam. She was the dam.

"Release it, girl," said Sorle, close beside her. He had come into the water. "Release it. If it builds behind you and you let go all at once, it'll take the floor out."

"I can't."

"You can."

"*I can't.*" She could not. That was the simple truth of it. She had never in her life been able to. She had kept a falling drop for Crisp and stood there with it kept until Crisp sighed. She had kept Lucan Ferrers in the mouth of a sink and stood over him while the casing dragged, until Orsolya talked her through it. She had stood in front of a moth in the Hall of Assay and— She had done the moth. She had opened her hand for the moth. But a moth was a moth, and this was the sea.

The salt under her hands was creaking. She could hear it now. It was a long, low, groaning sound, like a ship's timbers in a story, like the Lantern's mirrors turning on a cold night. Little fractures ran out from the seam across the clear floor of the Hush, white lines in the black water, spreading.

* * *

"Chick."

Orsolya was beside her. Hester had not seen her come. She was in the water up to her waist and her face was the colour of the salt and her eyes were on the shaft: on the half-moon crack, and the plug, and the water still going into it in a hard black rope, even past Hester's hold.

"It's taking it," Orsolya said. "It's taking it."

"Yes."

"Up to the White."

"Yes."

Orsolya looked at the shaft for perhaps three breaths. Hester did not know what she saw there. Perhaps nothing more than water going where water was let go. Perhaps a woman of eleven years old in a Lowside street on the day they brought her father up out of Coldbank on a door, crushed and breathing, with a crowd round the pit-head saying *if they’d only shored it, if they’d only*. Perhaps only arithmetic. Orsolya had always had a gift for sums.

"Brannoc," she said, quite calmly, without turning. "Iley. Up the steps, both of you. Put your hands on the trunk at the stair-head and hold it. All of it. Every stay in the Fifth. You hold that gallery dry while this lass does what she's doing, and you don't let go till I tell you."

"Warden—"

"*Now*, Brannoc."

They went. The young man went first, splashing, and the older woman after him. At the top of the three steps, where the brass trunk of the stay-lines came down out of the dark from the Fifth and ran along the Hush wall, they knelt and put their hands on it. Hester felt them go into the hold together, a smaller, colder stillness, running up the line.

"Mr. Dunmore," Orsolya said. "Go with them."

There was no answer. Hester looked round. There was a lantern on the bottom step where Dunmore had stood, set down carefully, still burning. Far back across the Hush, in the dark, feet were running on salt toward the door. Then they were not. Dunmore was gone.

Orsolya made a small sound that might have been a laugh. Then she waded back through the water to the steps and climbed them and knelt at the trunk between her two Fathomers. She put her white shaking hands on the brass. Her stillness went out of her into the line, deep and old and enormous. Hester felt it pass along the wall above her head, a cold river going upward, and somewhere above them in the dark the stays of the Fifth Fathom took it and stiffened and held.

"There," Orsolya said, to no one, to the line. "There, now."

Then she looked down the length of the Hush, across the water, at Hester. Her face in the lantern-light was very tired and very clear.

"Open your hand, chick," she said.

* * *

*Open your hand.*

She had heard it on the White, with Lucan's casing dragging at the lip of the sink. She heard it now, and under it she heard her father in the steam of the Pans: *Lid's for when you're in a hurry.* She saw his hand on the fire-hook, drawing the coals out one shovelful at a time. Not all at once. A shovelful, and a pause, and the pan grumbling, and another. *Take her off the heat slow and let her talk.*

And under that, under everything, a boy's voice in a hot room above Tanner's Row. He had opened his eyes in the middle of the night and found her and said, as clearly as he had ever said anything: *Let me sleep, Hes. Just let me sleep.*

She had known what he meant. She had stood at the desk on Coldwater Street with his voice in her ears and known what he meant, and signed. She had told herself every month for eleven years that keeping was sleep and he had asked for it. She had kept him because she could not open her hand. Not for him. For her.

She was holding the sea shut for the same reason.

It was not a thought so much as a weight that came off her, as a sack comes off a porter's back at the end of a quay. She had not known how heavy it was until it was gone.

"Saskia," she said. "Count."

"Hold," said Saskia. Her voice was steady now. "Hold—hold—*now.*"

Hester opened her hand.

It was not like keeping. Keeping was a door shutting. This was a door opening onto a wind, and the wind was the sea. She let the stillness go out of her palms, not pushed but let go, and felt the salt soften under them, and the seam give, a little, a hand's breadth. The water came through it harder and went across the floor and into the shaft-mouth. The plug, under the weight of it, tore loose from its socket with a crack like a tree going over, and the sea took it into the adit and up the shaft ahead of itself like a cork. The whole round mouth of the Founders' Shaft stood open, black, roaring.

"Hold," said Saskia. "Hold—two—three. *Now.*"

She opened again. The sea went up.

She could hear it going: a long hollow rising roar inside the shaft, climbing, as the water climbed. It sounded like the Lantern's mirrors and like the night-chime of the White and like nothing she had a word for. She could feel the arrears go with it, the piled-up debt of the moment running out of the salt through her hands and away. It was not all at once. A shovelful, and a pause.

"Hold. Hold. Hold." Saskia, counting. "Now."

Sorle was beside her in the water with his slate held up out of it, a stub of chalk in his blue fingers, writing. "Longer," he said. "Hold longer this time. Four. The floor's taking strain on the north side. Give it four, Miss Orme."

"Hold four," said Saskia. "One. Two. Three. Four. Now."

Hester opened her hand. The sea went up.

It went on like that. Afterward she could not have said for how long. Sorle said, later, forty minutes; Saskia said fifty-two, and hers was the watch that had stayed dry. Hold and open. Hold and open. Each time she opened, the sea went into the shaft in a black pulse and rose, roaring, and each time the roar went up and up until she lost it, six hundred feet above her head, where the Well-Cap sat on the salt under the stars. Each time she held, the white fractures in the clear floor stopped spreading. Each time Saskia said *now* it was a little easier to open, and a little harder to come back.

Because it cost. She had known it would cost. The rush, the Primer called it, as if it were a thing that happened to other people. Every time she let go, the stillness she let go came back up her arms as heat, and the heat was in her chest, and her heart was going faster and faster. It was not the pounding of fear but something simpler and worse, a wheel spinning free. Sweat ran into her eyes and did not sting. The lanterns had got too bright. Saskia's voice came to her too fast, each word clipped off at the end, as if someone were winding the world on a key. The sound of the sea went up in pitch until it was nearly a whistle.

"Hold," said Saskia, from a great way off. "Hold. Vail? *Vail.* Now."

She opened her hand.

The whole Hush went white.

* * *

She was on her knees in the water. She did not remember kneeling. Her heart was going so fast it was no longer a beat but a hum, like the Hush's hum, like a bee in a jar. Her hands were still flat on the salt. She could not take them away. She could not see. Someone had her by the shoulders.

"Still runs to quick," said Sorle's voice in her ear, and it was the first time she had ever heard him sound frightened. "Still runs to quick, damn you, hold *on*—"

He had his hands over hers on the wall, in the water. The brine was over all four of their hands. She felt his hold go into it, an old man's hold, thin and dry and enormously patient, a stillness that had sat at a desk for thirty-two years with nothing to do but think. It ran out of him into the brine and through the brine into her, because that was the law, because still runs to quick, and nothing in the world had ever been quicker than Hester Vail at that moment. It went into her hands and up her arms. It found the spinning wheel in her chest and laid itself across it like a palm across a top.

The hum slowed. It became a beat. Fast, too fast, but a beat. She could hear it. She could hear Saskia sobbing somewhere and saying *now, now, now* to nobody, and the sea going up the shaft. The sea was going up the shaft without her, in a long steady roar, no longer in pulses. It was a river climbing, the pressure behind the seam running out evenly, of itself, the way a pan goes on talking quietly for an hour after the fire is drawn.

Then Sorle's hands went slack on hers. He sat down in the water.

"Reader," she said. Her voice was nothing. "Reader."

He did not answer. He was sitting up to his chest in black water with his eyes open and his chalk still in his fingers. He was not moving at all. She knew what it was. She had done it herself on the White, for four minutes, and come back with the sky changed. He had given her his stillness, and now he was lagging.

She held his hand and waited. She counted. At a hundred and eighty he blinked, once, slowly, like an owl. At two hundred he said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "—*on*, Miss Vail," finishing the sentence he had started three minutes before. Then he looked down at himself, at the water, and said, "Ah."

The roar in the shaft was dropping.

It dropped as a wind drops at evening on the White, not all at once but in long lowering breaths. The seam in the east wall still ran with water, but less, and less. The black flood across the clear floor of the Hush had stopped rising; it was going down, drawn back toward the seam in long slow currents, into the hollow behind the wall where the quickening had been. The lantern flames had stopped bowing east. They stood up straight.

The surf behind the wall was gone. Where it had been there was nothing. The hum of the Hush came back, the old pressure in the teeth. But it was lower, Hester thought. Lower than it had been. As if something very large and very far away had let out a breath and not yet drawn the next one.

Saskia came down off the brass housing into the water. She was holding her watch in both hands like something that might break. She looked at it, and at Hester, and at Sorle, and put the watch back in her pocket, carefully, and sat down on the housing with her face in her hands.

Nobody said anything for a while.

* * *

When Hester could stand, she stood. Sorle had to help her and she had to help him, so it took some time. The water was below her knees now and going down. Every step she took, the clear floor under it showed her the deep darkness underneath, the thing too large to be anything, and she did not look down.

At the top of the three steps, by the brass trunk of the stay-lines, the two Fathomers were sitting against the wall. The young man was awake and shaking, his teeth chattering, his arms wrapped round his knees. The older woman was lagging still. Her eyes were open on nothing.

Orsolya was kneeling between them, where she had knelt. Her hands were still on the brass.

No. Not on it. They had come off the brass. They lay in her lap, palms up, open, the white fingers a little curled, as a sleeper's hands are curled. She was sitting back on her heels with her head bent a little forward, as though looking down into her own palms for something written there. She did not move when Hester's lantern came up the steps. She did not blink.

"Warden," said the young Fathomer, Brannoc, through his chattering teeth. "She let go when it slowed. She said, *that's it, that's done.* And then she just—sat."

Hester knelt down in front of her. She did not touch her. She knew the look of it. She had seen it on the gallery floor of the Fourth Fathom on the night of the Silent Supper, when Ama Tessaly sat down in the middle of a stay and did not get up again. But Ama's lips had gone grey, and Orsolya's had not. Orsolya's chest was moving, slower than sleep, a breath to every ten of Hester's.

Settled. Not to death. For hours, perhaps. Perhaps a day. Thirty years in the Choir and one more hold than her heart had wanted to give.

Hester sat back on her heels opposite her, so that they were two women kneeling face to face on a step in the dark with the water going down below them. She found that she was crying, and that her tears were leaving salt on her cheeks, and that she did not care.

"It went up," she told Orsolya. "It went up the shaft. All of it. The Fifth held. You held it."

Orsolya's open hands lay in her lap.

Below them in the Hush the last of the black water slid back through the seam into the emptied dark behind the wall, and was gone, and left the clear floor shining, scored with white fractures, drying already at the edges into a thin new skin of salt.
