# Chapter 25

## The Gull

> And the grey birds cried over Ostrine water
> where no grey bird has cried since.
>
> — *"The Last Fleet," Rim ballad, traditional*

They carried her up the Stair in a blanket slung between two porters, because her legs would not do it. She remembered pieces. The chalk-names going past at the height of her eyes, Ama Tessaly's new and white among the old. Saskia's voice somewhere below saying *mind her head, mind her head, for pity's sake*. A Proctor's lantern. The cold. The cold most of all: her clothes were soaked to the chest with seawater, and the seawater had begun, in the long climb, to dry on her into a crust, and the crust was not grain. It kept nothing. It only itched.

At the First Fathom someone tried to take her to the Physic wards and she said no, so clearly that the porters stopped.

"The Well-Cap," she said. "I want to see."

"Miss, you're not fit—"

"Take her," said Sorle's voice, from somewhere behind. He was walking, with a stick he had got from somewhere, and a Proctor holding his other arm. "Take her up, man. She has earned the view. So, God help me, have I."

They came out of the Gatehouse into the dark before dawn.

It was the hard dark of midwinter on the White, the frost so deep that the air seemed to ring with it. The Lantern stood up black against a sky going grey at its eastern edge. The salt of the Shelf was stiff with rime and squealed under the porters' boots. And it was full of people: Proctors with lanterns, Readers with blankets over their gowns, aspirants still in their Assay Night finery, half-drunk and white-faced, porters and cooks, all standing in the frost on the eastern flank of the dome, in a long uneven line, looking east.

There was steam in the east.

The porters carried her round the foot of the Lantern and set her down on the salt at the edge of the crowd, and Saskia came and knelt and put a second blanket round her and held it closed at her throat. Hester looked.

A quarter of a mile out on the White, where the Well-Cap had sat for nine centuries on its square of founders' brick, there was no Well-Cap. There was a lake.

It lay on the white plain like ink spilled on paper, roughly round, four or five hundred yards across, perhaps more. It was steaming. The water was warmer than the midwinter air, and the steam hung a man's height above it and drifted south on the faint wind. Near the middle the water was still welling, slowly, from the mouth of the Founders' Shaft, in a low dome a few yards wide, like a spring in a field. At the edges it had frozen. All round the lake, where the black water met the white salt, a rim of ice was forming, grey and thin and creaking, a yard wide, two yards, and the frost was growing out across the water on it in long feathered spears.

The iron Well-Cap lay on the salt a hundred yards from the shore, upside down, where the sea had thrown it.

"Oh," said Saskia, beside her, on a long breath. That was all.

The whole long line of them stood on the frozen dome and watched the steam go up. Somewhere behind Hester a woman was crying, quietly, the way you cry at a wedding. The sound of the water came to them across the quarter-mile of salt. It was a soft lapping, a small slap and suck where the ice met it, and it was a sound that not one person standing on the Shelf had ever heard in their lives, except in a bathtub.

* * *

The light came up slowly. That was midwinter: the sun took its time. The sky went from grey to pearl to a thin cold rose, and the White went rose with it, and the steam on the lake went gold at its upper edge where the first light caught it. And as the light came, people began to walk out.

A few aspirants first, daring each other. Then Bassano, unmistakable in a fur hat. Then more, and Proctors half-heartedly trying to stop them, and then not trying. Hester got up. Saskia said "Vail—" and then saw her face, and got up too, and put an arm under her shoulder, and they walked out across the frozen salt together, very slowly. Sorle came behind them on his stick, saying nothing at all.

The shore of the lake, when they reached it, was a ridge of new ice standing up from the salt like the lip of a cup. Beyond it the grey rim of frozen sea ran out a few yards and thinned and went black. Hester stood at the ridge and looked down.

The water was clear. She had thought it was black; it was not. It was black the way deep water is black, and at the shallow edge, where the ice was thin, she could see down through it to the salt floor of the lake a yard below. On the salt floor, lying in the slow current, there was weed.

It was green: not the copper-green of the pans, but green as new grass is green, as Harl cabbages are green in a market in spring. Long ribbons of it, a dozen, more, lifting and lying down again in the welling current, and the light coming through the water lit them from inside. As she watched, one ribbon lifted and turned over and showed the paler underside of itself, and on it, clinging, there was something small and dark and many-legged that moved.

"Crab," said Saskia, in a strange voice. "That's a crab. Isn't it? Pip has a picture book. That's a crab."

It was a crab. It was the size of a sol, dark green and mottled, and it went sideways off the weed and down onto the salt floor and stopped there, waving one small claw at nothing.

And past it, in the deeper water beyond the ice, something flicked. A glint, silver, there and gone. Then another. Then, quite clearly, five or six of them together, small and quick and silver as new flecks, turning all at once in the current as if they were one thing: fish. Living fish, under the ice, in a lake on the White, going about the business of being fish as though nothing had happened to them at all. As though the afternoon on which the sea had last been a sea had simply gone on.

Hester knelt down on the ice. She put her bare hand flat on it, over the place where the crab was. She did not need the hold. The ice was cold and wet and tasted, when she put her fingers to her mouth, of salt and of nothing else. It was not sweet. There were no coins on her eyelids. It was only salt: the plain bright startled taste of something that was alive and moving and had no intention of being anything else.

Quick, she thought. All of it quick.

* * *

The gull came out of the steam a little after sunrise.

She knew it before she heard it properly. She had heard it once, in a single grain of warm white salt on a customs bench, on the first morning of autumn: a high falling cry, two notes and a third that broke. Teo had found it for her in a hymnal woodcut in the Brinestacks. *And the gulls, the birds of the sea, crying.* There were no gulls. There had been no gulls on the Rim for nine hundred years.

It came out of the steam near the middle of the lake, in the water, struggling: a lump of wet grey and white, low in the water, beating at the surface with something that might have been wings, and going under, and coming up. The crowd on the shore had seen it. A sound went along them, a long indrawn *oh*. The thing in the water beat at the surface again, and got its head up, and Hester saw a yellow beak and a round furious eye. It was swimming. It was swimming toward the ice on the northern side, where the frozen rim was widest, and it reached the ice and got its breast up onto it and hung there, its feet going, and then hauled itself out, all at once, and stood.

It was the size of a hen, perhaps, or a little bigger, grey on the back and white below, with black at the tips of its long wings. It stood on the ice with the black water behind it and the steam going up, and it shook itself. It shook itself from the head down, all over, the way a dog shakes, and the water flew off it in a spray that caught the low sun and went gold. It opened its wings and shook them too, and folded them, and opened them again.

Then it put its head back and cried.

Two notes and a third that cracked. A sound like a gate hinge. Like a child calling from a long way off. The sound went out across the White, over the frozen salt and the silent crowd and the black lake steaming, and came back off the dome of the Halcyon as an echo. It was the loudest thing in the world.

Beside her, Saskia had both hands over her mouth.

The gull cried again. Then it ran three steps along the ice and opened its wings and was in the air. It went up into the light, over the steam, beating hard. It circled once over the lake, low, as if to see what it had come out of. Then it turned west, toward the city, toward the rose-lit roofs of Ambry two miles off on the old shore, and flew. It was a grey speck and then nothing.

It was a long time before anyone on the shore moved.

* * *

When Hester turned round at last, looking for Sorle, she saw the Chancellor.

Perpetua Holm had been carried out of the Readers' Close in a chair. It stood on the salt at the foot of the Lantern, a carved chair with poles for bearers, and four porters stood beside it, stamping in the cold. The Chancellor sat in it as she had sat in the Hall of Assay the day before: upright, wrapped to the chin in black fur, her hands folded on her knees, her white face turned east. She had not moved, as far as Hester could tell, since the porters set her down. She was looking at the place in the sky where the gull had gone.

Hester did not decide to go to her. She found she was walking, with Saskia's arm under hers, to the foot of the Lantern. Up close, the Chancellor's face was very old and very still and quite composed. It was wet. There were lines of salt drying on it, from the corners of her eyes down past her mouth, as the tears of the Settled always dried. Hester had expected many things of that face. She had expected anger, or calculation, or the sharp blank fear that had been on Deputy Vey's face on the dais the day before. She had not expected grief.

There was grief in it, a great deal of grief, like a weight at the bottom of a scale, and no surprise at all.

"Chancellor," Hester said.

The old eyes moved and found her. It took some time. Holm's voice, when it came, was the voice of the Settled, a long slow whisper with pauses in it, as if each word had to be carried up from a long way down.

"Miss Vail," she said. "Aske's aspirant. Sorle's, now, I hear."

"Yes, Chancellor."

"Assayed with distinction." A pause. "Yesterday."

"Yes."

The Chancellor looked past her, east, at the lake. The steam was lifting off it now in the sun, thinning, so that you could see the whole black width of it. There was a line of ice all round its rim, like a ring of salt round the edge of a glass.

"It was always going to come back, Miss Vail," said Perpetua Holm. "We only hoped to be dead first."

Then she closed her eyes. One of the porters looked at another, and they bent and took up the poles, and the chair rose and turned and went away toward the Readers' Close, swaying, with the old woman in it upright and motionless and wet-faced. Hester stood in the frost and watched it go, and did not know what she had just been told, and knew that it was something.

* * *

The rest came fast, as the rest always does. The Halcyon was an institution, and an institution has a way of turning even a sea on the White into paper.

Orsolya Thane woke on the evening of the twenty-second, on a cot in the Physic wards, twenty hours after she had sat down at the top of the three steps. She asked for water, and for Chief Proctor Gage, and for Hester Vail.

Hester was four cots down under two blankets, with Rule's orders not to stand. She stood. A Physic's assistant opened his mouth and shut it again.

Gage came with a clerk and a lamp. He set a stool square to the cot and sat on it with his cap on his knee, and the clerk opened a ledger on the next bed. Orsolya was sitting up against the salt wall with a cup in her two hands. She could not quite close them round it. She looked at Hester a long moment, and then at Gage.

"I'd like her to hear it," she said. "Write down that she's here."

"Aspirant Vail, present." The clerk wrote. "Warden, before you say anything, you should know that—"

"It goes in that book. I know. That's why I asked for the book." Orsolya set the cup on the blanket. "Caution me, Chief Proctor. Then we'll start."

He cautioned her, in the old form, clipped and complete. She heard him out. Then she began, not at the Old Tap, but at the beginning.

"Seedmonth of 901," she said. "The Chancellor's rooms in the Readers' Close, at the Lantern Hour. I took up the east-trunk figures and the Choir's sick-roll and put them on her table. Perpetua Holm was in the chair. Deputy Chancellor Vey was standing at her shoulder. I asked for an inquiry. I asked for money for twice the Choir. I asked for the Delvers to be stopped on the Shelf." Her voice did not change. "The Deputy did the talking. The Chancellor sat and let him. His words were, *Manage it quietly, Warden. You've always managed.* Write that down, lad. Those words."

The clerk's pen had stopped. Gage looked at it, and it went on.

She gave the rest in order, with dates, the way a Choir roster is called. The sleeves under Coldwater Street and the brinewright who had fitted them, by name. The contract with Ferrers and Lisk, and the three letters she had signed it with, and where every sol of it had gone: stipends, widows' pensions, never a fleck to her. Bett Linnow, and the three others, by name and plinth. Ama Tessaly, and every shift she had let her sing. Then the sixteenth of Russet.

"Corvin went down to the Old Tap at the ninth bell to shut it," she said. "He meant to be back up for this lass at the tenth. I kept him there, with his hand on the wheel, in Hush grain. Edwin Dunmore and I brought him up the Stair on a cask-trolley, because the porters have the lifts after the eighth. We waited on the turn below his Cell while she stood at the door. We waited again while Benedek Sorle came and went. We had him in a little after the half, and I put a dish in his basin for you to write down."

Hester did not move. She watched the clerk's pen, and the lamp on the ledger, and Orsolya's open hands on the blanket.

It took an hour and a half. At the end of it Gage stood, and put his cap under his arm, and said the words of arrest, and Orsolya bowed her head to them as if to a hymn.

Only then did she look at Hester again.

"Did it all go up?"

"All of it."

Orsolya shut her eyes. "Good," she said. "Then that's done."

Jerome Pell was taken at his sister's house in the Middle Ward on the twenty-third, with the Cold House's Halcyon maintenance receipts in a tin box under her spare bed. Edwin Dunmore had bought passage on an eastbound skiff at the second bell of the twenty-second, paying in silver and not haggling. The skiff was overdue at Quell.

Lucan Ferrers walked into the Assize Chamber on the morning of the twenty-third, without an appointment, in the coat he had worn into the Cistern, which still had brick-dust on the cuffs. He laid on the clerk's table a fair copy, in his own hand, of the residual grain contract between the Office of the Warden of the Lower Fathoms and Messrs. Ferrers and Lisk, Exchequer Yard, with a sworn statement that he had found the original in his father's study. By noon it was in the *Courant*.

Teo told her about the Cistern that night, sitting on the end of her cot with a twist of saltcake he was not supposed to have brought in.

"You'd think the dark would be the thing," he said. "It wasn't. It was the noise. You know I hear it as voices. All the way down from the grate, the line was full of them. Three hundred, near enough, in the brass. Not words. Like a congregation told to hum that's forgotten the hymn. And the rods were cold enough to take your skin off. Lucan had his watch open on a brick and counted the minutes from the half out loud. I don't think he knew he was doing it.

"We couldn't hear the bell, of course. At twelve he just said 'Now,' very quietly, as if he were asking for the salt, and we put the saws in. Brass is stubborn. It took longer than you'd credit. He slipped and laid the back of his hand open and never said a word. He kept sawing with the blood running into the cut."

He turned the saltcake over and did not eat it.

"And when the rods parted, the voices stopped. Not all at once. They went *back*. Like people coming out of chapel and going home down different streets, one and then the next and the next. I knelt there with my hand on the pipe and listened to every one of them go home. One of them was Tib, I think. Don't ask me how I knew." He smiled, crookedly. "Then Agathe came down the cellar steps with a lamp and a bowl of water for Lucan's hand, and said, 'Well, they're dry, the lot of them,' and boxed both our ears for frightening her. I've never been so glad to be hit."

Hester said nothing. She took the saltcake out of his hand and broke it in two, and gave him half.

The Deputy Chancellor issued a statement on the twenty-fourth, read aloud at the Long Table at the Lantern Hour. It described the events of the night of the twenty-first of Rime as a natural quickening event of unusual magnitude, vented through a disused shaft by the timely action of members of the Halcyon, whom it did not name. It promised a Board of Inquiry. It did not mention a gull, or Seedmonth of 901. Somebody among the third-years laughed out loud at *natural*, and the clerk read on over him.

One pulse of the vent had backed up an old founders' adit into the east gallery of the Fourth, before Sorle lengthened the count, and the overflow stacks there had been under brine for an hour. Wynn Callow had himself carried down to look at them, and sat in the doorway most of an afternoon, and said nothing to anyone about what he had seen.

Agathe's letter came up on the twenty-fourth. *My dear. They are dry, every one. Your Tib is clear. Nell Corrie is crazed all over like a dropped plate but clear under it, and holding. They have sent us a Mrs. Arden from the Board who seems sensible. Come when you are able. Tide willing. A.L.*

And on the shore of the lake, through all of it, people came: across the Causeway by the hundred and then by the thousand, in carts and on foot and in landships that tacked up and down the black water as near as they dared. They brought children. They filled jars and carried them home. Lowside had already named it the Tear. On the second day the gull came back. It sat on the upturned Well-Cap for an hour in the afternoon sun with the crowd watching it in perfect silence, and then flew off again toward the city, crying.

* * *

Annis Rule had listened to Hester's chest on the morning of the twenty-second with a horn trumpet and her eyes shut, for a long time.

"Your heart keeps its own time now, Miss Vail," she said at last. She let Hester listen to it through the horn: small and quick and not quite even, a tick with a catch in it, like a pan with muck in the bottom. "That will not mend. You may release small things, slowly, with someone counting. But another rush like that will stop it."

Hester buttoned her shirt. "Ta," she said.

They vented Corvin Aske over six days.

Sorle would not let them do it faster. He had done the arithmetic on four slates, sitting up in a Physic bed, and done it again with Hester checking every line. A man kept in Hush grain for three months, he said, carried arrears enough to boil the blood in his veins if they came out at once. Spread over six days, in a warm bath, in stages, they might come out as a fever.

"Annealed," Saskia said.

"Annealed," said Sorle, with no trace of irony. "Thank you, Mrs. Orme. Yes."

They did it in the Physic wards on the Third Fathom, in the long tiled bath-room at the end, where the Physicians returned the Halcyon's own kept staff when a cure was found. Rule oversaw it. She had returned two hundred people in her life, and said on the first morning that she had never returned one who had been kept against his will and was glad to begin. When the sheet came off the casing, Hester stood and looked at it in the bath-room lamplight: the thin man standing in clear salt with one arm reaching and his mouth open on a word, and the faint widdershins spiral in the grain of it, turning round him like a shell.

Clear. Three months in Hush grain and still clear, without a breath of bloom. *The living keep clear.*

They lowered him into the warm brine. And every morning and every evening for six days, Hester went into the release with her hands on the casing under the water, and opened them, a little. Sorle sat on a stool at the bath's edge with his slate and his watch and told her when, his left hand in his lap in a wool mitten. It had not closed since the Hush, and Rule did not think it would. Saskia came between her lamp-work shifts and counted. Teo came and sat on the tiled floor with his back against the wall and his shawl round him and did not count anything, but stayed. The salt went down around Aske a finger's width a day, and the water grew hotter, and Rule's assistants drew it off and ran in more. On the third day the casing was thin enough that Hester could see his eyes clearly through it, open and short-sighted and fixed on something on the far side of the room that had not been there for three months. On the fourth day his skin above the waterline began to steam. On the fifth day the bath-room was hot as the Pans, and Hester came out of every release with her heart ticking and catching and her shirt soaked through, and Sorle made her sit with her hands in a basin of cold brine, his own good hand laid over them, until he could count her pulse without losing his place.

On the morning of the sixth day, the twenty-eighth of Rime, the last of the salt went.

It went as the last of the ice goes off a pond, all at once, the final thin sheet cracking and sliding off him into the water. And Corvin Aske's reaching arm finished its movement. His hand closed on the air where the Old Tap had been on the night of the sixteenth of Russet, and his mouth finished its word.

"—*you can't* hold it, Orsolya, it'll only grow—"

He stopped. He was standing waist-deep in a hot bath with steam rising off his shoulders, his spectacles crooked on his nose where they had sat for three months, and an assistant on either side holding his elbows. He blinked. He looked at the tiled walls, and at the steam, and at Reader Rule, and at Sorle on his stool, and then at Hester, standing in the water in front of him with her hands still open on nothing.

"Oh," he said. "You tasted it."

"I tasted it."

He took that in. Hester watched him take it in: the bath, the Physicians, Sorle, the heat. He had always been quick.

"How long?" he said.

"Three months."

"Three—" His eyebrows went up over the crooked spectacles. "Have I missed the Assay?"

"You have."

"Did you pass?"

"With distinction."

"Well, of course you did," said Corvin Aske, and began, absurdly, to laugh, and then to shake, and the assistants caught him as his knees went. They got him out of the bath and into a blanket and onto a bench. He sat there with the blanket round him and his teeth chattering and the steam coming off his hair, and held out his right hand to look at it.

It did not open properly. The fingers stayed curled, stiffly, as a man's fingers stay when he has held a pen too long in the cold. He tried to straighten them with his left hand and they went straight and then curled again, slowly, of their own accord. Reader Rule bent and looked at them and said nothing, and then said, in her grey voice, "That will be the arrears. Six days of heat through one hand. Some of it may come back. Some of it will not."

Aske looked at his hand for a long time. Then he looked up at Hester.

"Orsolya," he said.

"In the Proctory cells."

"Is she—"

"She held the Fifth," Hester said. "While I vented. She sent her people to the trunk and held it herself. She's Settled some. She's all right. She told Gage everything. I was there."

Aske closed his eyes behind the crooked spectacles. When he opened them they were wet.

"Of course she did," he said. "Of course she did, the daft woman." Then, more quietly: "I was going to ask her first. Before Gage. I owed her that. I thought—" He stopped. "Never mind what I thought. It's three months ago." He turned the stiff hand over in his lap and looked at its palm. "Is there tea? I'm told there's always tea."

Sorle was already pouring it, one-handed. He put the cup into Aske's left hand, since the right would not hold it, and put a pinch of salt in it without asking. Aske drank, and made a face, and drank again.

"Disgusting," he said. "Tell me everything. Start with what you tasted."
