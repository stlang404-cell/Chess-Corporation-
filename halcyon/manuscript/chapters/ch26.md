# Chapter 26

## What the Salt Keeps

> What is kept is not lost.
>
> — *Motto of the Halcyon*

The Proctory cells were on the First Fathom, behind the Chief Proctor's office. They were three small rooms cut square into the salt, each with a cot and a stool and a slop-bucket and a door of iron bars. They were not often used. Aspirants who fought at the Thirsty Society slept it off in them, Teo said, and once in a generation a Reader was put there for falsifying a grade. The walls were old and smooth and faintly pink in the lamplight. Somebody, a long time ago, had scratched a halcyon into the wall of the middle cell, a crude one, with a crooked wave under it.

Orsolya was in the middle cell. She was sitting on the stool, wrapped in an iron-grey blanket from the Choir stores, looking at the halcyon.

"Hester," she said, when the Proctor had unlocked the door and gone back to the end of the passage. She said it without turning her head, and she did not say *chick*. "Sit down. Before you fall down."

There was nowhere to sit but the cot. Hester sat on the edge of it. The blanket on it was folded square, Choir-fashion. She could see Orsolya's hands in her lap under the edge of the grey wool. They were still white. They lay open, palms up, as they had lain on the founders' step in the Hush, as if the Warden had forgotten how to close them and had stopped trying.

They sat. Somewhere far above, through all the Fathoms and the dome, Hester could feel the Lantern's mirrors turning in the noon sun, a faint tick in the stone that she had never noticed until she lived inside it.

"Corvin's up," Hester said.

"I know. Gage told me." Orsolya's face did not change. "His hand?"

"Stiff. Rule says some of it will come back."

"Some of it won't." Orsolya nodded slowly. "No. It wouldn't."

"He asked after you. First thing, nearly."

Orsolya closed her eyes, briefly. Then she opened them and looked at Hester for the first time, a long look, Lowside to Lowside, the look of one woman checking another for a short weight.

"I'll not say sorry to you," she said. "I'll say it to Corvin. I'll say it to Ruth Linnow, if she'll have it, and to the families of the other three, God help me, and to the Choir. Ama was sixty-six." She was quiet a moment. "But I'll not say it to you, Hester. You'd not believe it. And you'd be right not to, because I'd do half of it again. Not Corvin. Not the lads in the Cistern. But I'd go down to the Fifth again tomorrow and hold that line, and I'd hold it with whatever I had. You know that."

"I know."

"Good." Orsolya looked at the halcyon on the wall. "Brannoc came by. He's a good lad. He says the Choir's to be stood down a month while the Board looks at the stays. Says the Hush has gone quiet. Quiet as a church, he says. First time since I came into the Choir." She breathed out. "Your road."

"Corvin's road."

"Your hands, though."

Hester said nothing. When Orsolya spoke again her voice had gone lower and plainer, the voice she had used on the White with Lucan's casing dragging at the lip of the sink.

"Did it hurt?" she said. "Letting go?"

Hester thought about the wheel spinning free in her chest, and Saskia's voice going fast as a wound clock, and Sorle's old hands over hers in the black water. She thought about a weight coming off her like a sack off a porter's back.

"Yes," she said.

Orsolya nodded. For the first time since Hester had come in, something in her face eased.

"Good," she said. "Means you did it right."

* * *

On the afternoon of the twenty-ninth of Rime, Hester went to the Cold House, and her father came with her.

She had not asked him. She had gone down the Causeway in the morning and through Eastgate into Lowside, against a stream of people going the other way, out toward the Tear, with jars and children and baskets of food. She had gone to the Pans because she had told him she would. He had been at pan nineteen, raking, and he had looked up at her through the steam as he always did. Then, for the first time in her memory, he had set down his rake in the middle of a boil. He had told the boiler on pan eighteen to mind it, and gone to the pump and washed, and put on the clean coat from the Assay, and walked out of the boiling-house beside her into the cold without a word.

They did not talk as they went. They went up Tanner's Row and along Chandler's Lane, and at the top of the rise, where you could see the roofs and the sea-wall and the White, they both stopped. There was a black mark on the White now, two miles out, east of the Halcyon's dome: a long black oval with the low sun on it, like a coin dropped on a tablecloth. Aldous said, "Well," and started walking again.

Coldwater Street was quiet. The board over the door said THE LIVING KEEP CLEAR in its two hands, the old and the careful. Her father stood on the step and looked up at it, and Hester saw his jaw work.

"Da," she said. "You needn't."

"I know I needn't."

He went in.

There was a new clerk at the desk, and a new card beside the ledger: *Mrs. H. Arden, Director (acting)*. Hester did not pay. It was not the first of the month. She signed the visitors' book and held out the pen, and her father looked at it before he took it. He had stood at this desk once already this winter. Agathe had written to her of it: the nineteenth of Cinder, the release form turned round on the felt for him, the pen in his fist for the time it took a kettle to boil, and then the pen put down, and Aldous Vail gone out into the street without a word. He took it now and signed under her, slowly, in big careful capitals: A. VAIL. FATHER.

Then they went through the double doors, which he had not been through in nine years, and down the six worn steps into the cold sweet air.

The ward was as it always was. The light came in long bars through the high windows and lay across the eight rows, and in each bar the casings caught it and held it. The sleepers lay as they had been laid: the old man with his mouth open on a word, the girl with the red ribbon, the woman with the baby. Mother Agathe was at the far end of the second row with her brush and her oil can. She saw them and straightened, slowly, and did not come. She lifted one hand a little and let it fall, and went back to her brushing.

By the eastern wall there was an empty plinth where Bett Linnow had lain. Someone had put a sprig of Hask juniper on it.

Aldous stopped at the foot of the steps. Hester stopped beside him and looked down the fourth row to the third window, and waited. It took him a long time. She heard him breathing, heavily, through his nose, the way he breathed at the pans when a boil was going wrong. Then he went along the row, and she went with him, and they came to two hundred and eleven.

Tib lay on his back with his head turned a little toward the window. His eyes were half open. His hand was curled on his chest, holding its pencil. His casing was clear from end to end and perfectly dry. There was not a bead on it anywhere. The light came through it and lay on him, and you could tell where the salt ended and the air began only by a faint brightness along its edges.

Aldous looked at his son.

He stood beside the plinth with his big burned hands hanging at his sides and looked, for a long time. Then he put out his right hand, the good one, and laid it flat on the casing over Tib's chest, over the curled hand with its pencil. Do not touch the casings. He left his hand there. His shoulders moved once, and then were still.

Hester stood on the other side of the plinth. She had thought about what she would say on the Causeway, and in the Rookery at night with Teo asleep across the cell, and on the long climb up the Stair in a blanket. She had not got further than the first line. She found, standing there, that she did not need to. You did not push it out. You opened your hand.

"Tib," she said. "It's Hester. Da's here. He's come to see you."

The light lay across the rows.

"I've to tell you something," she said, "and I should have told you eleven years ago, and I'm telling you now with Da here so I'll not be able to pretend afterward that I didn't. You said something to me. The night before Lantern Night, in the room in Tanner's Row. You were burning up. You opened your eyes and you said, *Let me sleep, Hes. Just let me sleep.*" Her voice was steady. That surprised her. "And I knew what you meant. You didn't mean keep me. You meant let me go. You were fourteen and you'd had the bloom three weeks and you knew what it was, and you asked me, and I knew. And I came down here and I signed the paper anyway. I told myself you'd asked for sleep, and keeping's a kind of sleep, so I'd done what you asked. I told myself that every month for eleven years. It isn't true. I kept you because I couldn't stand to let you go. Not for you. For me."

Her father's hand on the casing had not moved. She did not look at his face.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That's the first thing. It was your sleep, and I took it off you, and I've been paying six sols a month so I'd not have to think about it.

"And the second thing is this. I'll not let you go today. I want you to know why, because you've a right to know. It's not because I can't. I think I could now. I've learned how." She looked at the clear salt over his face. "It's because I don't know enough yet. There's things in the salt that nobody knew were there, Tib. There's a lake out on the White, a real one, with fish in it. There's a gull. There's things that have been kept nine hundred years and come back alive and whole. And I don't know yet what that means for you. Nobody does. So I'm going to find out.

"And I promise you this." She put her own hand flat on the casing, beside her father's, over the curled fingers and the pencil that was not there. The salt was cold and dry. "When I know how to bring you back properly, or when I know how to let you go, I'll do it. Whichever's right. For you, not for me. And before I do it, whichever it is, I'll tell you first. Here. Out loud. The way I should have done."

She stopped. She had nothing else to say. The ward was quiet. Down at the end of the second row Agathe's brush had stopped.

Aldous took his hand off the casing. He looked at the print of it, a faint mist on the salt that was already fading, and then at his daughter across the plinth. His eyes were red and wet and small.

"Well," he said. His voice was thick. Then, after a long while: "He'd have told you that was a daft promise."

"He would."

"Made it anyway."

"Yes."

Her father nodded. He looked down at Tib a while longer. Then he did a thing she had not seen him do in eleven years, or in thirty-three. He bent over the plinth and put his lips to the salt over his son's forehead and kept them there. Then he straightened, and wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist, and turned and walked away up the row without looking back.

Hester stayed. Mother Agathe came up the row slowly with her can and her brush and stopped beside her and looked down at two hundred and eleven, as she looked at all of them.

"He's clear, my dear," she said. "Look at him. Clear as the day they did it."

"I know."

"The living keep clear," said Agathe, and patted Hester's arm once with her small dry hand, and went on down the row.

* * *

On the morning of the thirtieth Sorle sent for her.

His study was as it had been the first time she had seen it, in Fallow, after the sink: books on every flat surface and slates propped against the books, the kept moth on the desk in its globe of salt, the portrait of his wife above the cold grate. The slates had different sums on them now. One of them, propped against a pile of Horology quarterlies, bore in large chalk capitals the words *NATURAL QUICKENING EVENT*, underlined twice, with a row of exclamation marks after it that went off the edge.

"Sit," said Sorle.

She sat. He did not. He took down from a shelf a long narrow ledger bound in black cloth gone brown at the corners, and laid it open on the desk in front of her, on top of everything else, so that the kept moth had to be moved. It was the Bench register of the Black Bench of Horology. The names went back up the page, a few to a year, and then whole years with no name at all, and then, three years ago, two names in Sorle's own hand: his third-years, whom she had met twice and who were terrified of him.

Under them, in fresh ink, still a little shiny, he had written:

*Hester Vail. Turning Intake, 903. Assayed with distinction, 21 Rime 903. Admitted to this Bench, 30 Rime 903, by B. Sorle, Reader.*

"You've been on my Bench since Fallow," he said. "Informally. Provisionally. Under protest from the Registrar. This makes it law." He held out the pen. "You sign under it. There."

She signed. Her hand was steady. She blotted it.

"There," said Sorle. He closed the ledger with a soft clap and put it back on the shelf, and sat down at last on the other side of the desk, and looked at her over his steepled blue fingers.

"I should tell you," he said, "that you will be a very great trial to me. You have no theory. You have no manners. You hold like a miser and release like a drunkard. You think with your tongue. You took a matter of the gravest institutional importance into your own hands without the slightest reference to proper authority, and you took me with you, and I have not been so cold or so wet or so frightened since I was a child."

"Yes, Reader."

"And you were right," said Sorle. "Which I find, after thirty-two years, I had entirely forgotten the taste of." He looked at the slate with *NATURAL QUICKENING EVENT* on it. "It is not, I discover, a sweet taste. It is very like salt."

* * *

She found Lucan on the Choir Steps in the afternoon, which was not a place she had expected to find him.

In midwinter nobody came to the steps. Snow had drifted into the corners of the tiers in thin grey crusts. He was sitting near the top, alone, in his good coat, with his arms round his knees, looking east at the Tear. From up here you could see the whole of it: the black oval on the white, the ice-rim round its shore, the crowd along the near edge like a line of ants, the little sails of the landships tacking up and down the water.

She climbed up and sat on the tier below him. He did not seem surprised.

"My father has resigned the Exchequer," he said, without preamble. "This morning. *For reasons of health.* The Assize will hold a hearing in Thaw. Mr. Lisk is in Hask, it seems. Visiting relations." He looked at his gloved hands. "My father has not spoken to me since the twenty-third. My sister writes that he has had my rooms in the Upper Ward cleared and my books sent to the Halcyon in crates, addressed in his own hand. She thinks that's a hopeful sign."

"Is it?"

"I've no idea. I've never had a hopeful sign from my father before. I don't know what one looks like." He was quiet. "Sorle has written me into his register. Properly, this morning, under you. He said that if he must suffer one insufferable aspirant he may as well suffer two, since the insufferability appears to be cumulative rather than multiplicative, and he would like to test the hypothesis."

"God help us both."

"Yes." He turned his head and looked down at her. His face was thin in the cold light. There was a scab across the back of his right hand where the saw had slipped, in the Cistern, in the dark; Agathe had dressed it, and it was healing badly. "Vail. On the White, in Fallow. When you kept me. I said I'd repay it."

"You've repaid it. You cut the line."

"That wasn't repayment. That was—" He stopped. He looked out at the Tear. "That was what wanted doing. You taught me that. It's a Lowside phrase. I've caught it like a cold." He was quiet for a while. Then, in a different voice, shorter: "I've never owed anyone anything before. I didn't like it. I thought it would be like a bill. Something one paid, and was done with."

"And?"

"And I find I don't mind owing you," said Lucan Ferrers.

She looked up at him. He looked back, and did not look away, which was new.

"That's a start," Hester said.

They sat on the Choir Steps in the cold a long time after that, not talking, watching the landships on the black water. When the light went, he stood and offered her his hand to get up. She took it, though she did not need to.

* * *

The Ebbtide Vigil fell on the last night of the year.

Hester had seen it every year of her life from Ambry. The Tiders came out of the Abbey's town house and the Lowside chapels at dusk in their grey shawls, with their candles, and went in a long slow procession through Eastgate and out along the Causeway. A little way past its end they stepped down onto the White and walked out a few hundred yards onto the bare salt, singing, to meet the tide. Then they stood in the cold under the stars with their candles, and waited for the sea, and the sea did not come. At the twelfth bell they sang the last hymn and walked home. Lowside called it the Wet-Foot Walk and made gentle jokes about it, and gave the walkers hot cider at Eastgate coming home, because it was a cold thing to do every year for nine hundred years and be wrong.

This year the whole city came.

They came out of Eastgate at dusk like a river, and the river was lit. Every one of them had a candle, Tiders and not, Lowside and Upper Ward, delvers and glaziers and clerks and children. They came along the Causeway and down off it onto the salt and out across the White in the dark, not a procession any more but a great slow field of small lights a quarter-mile wide, going east, toward the black water. From the dome of the Halcyon, as Hester came down off it with the others, it looked as if the sky had been laid on the salt.

Teo had taken his grey Tider shawl out of his chest in the Rookery that afternoon and looked at it a long while and put it on. Hester, in the same hour, had taken down the two dishes that had sat on her shelf since Russet, with their two kept drops of brine, and put a finger to each, and opened her hand. They had gone to water before she had finished wanting them to.

They walked together. Saskia was beside Teo with Pip on her hip and Hennie Orme on her other arm. Pip held a candle in both fists with her mother's hand round them and asked every few yards whether there would be fish. Lucan walked a little apart, as he always did, and then not quite so far apart. Corvin Aske came as far as the Gatehouse on Sorle's arm, both of them wrapped to the eyes, and stood there to watch them go, and lifted his good hand. And at the foot of the Causeway, where it came down onto the salt, a short wide man in a clean coat was standing out of the stream of people with a candle that had gone out. He fell in beside Hester without a word, and walked with her a quarter of a mile, perhaps more. Then he stopped, and said, "That'll do me," and touched her arm, and turned back toward the city.

The shore of the Tear was a ridge of grey ice, and the candles came down to it and spread out along it, left and right, until the black water had a rim of light all round it. The Tiders began the hymn they always sang, the old one about the tide coming home over the sand. Hester had heard it all her life and never listened to the words, and she listened now. Then people who did not know the words took it up, humming, until the whole shore was singing. The sound went out over the water and did not come back, because there was nothing out there for it to come back off. There was only the dark, and the water, and the faint smell of weed.

Teo touched her arm.

"Come and read it with me," he said.

They went down off the ridge onto the ice-rim, where the frozen sea ran out a yard or two from the shore before it thinned. Teo knelt. Hester knelt beside him. Under their knees the ice was grey and rough and new, and in it, where it met the shore, there was a thin white crust: salt, coming out of the seawater as it froze, as salt comes out of any sea at its edges in the cold. Salt that had been sea on the twenty-first of Rime and had only begun, these last nine days, to be anything else.

Teo took off his gloves and laid both his bare hands flat on it and shut his eyes. Hester watched him go into the hold, his poor restless hold that had never learned to keep still. It was steady tonight, steadier than she had seen it. His breath slowed. His lips went pale. She took off her own right glove and touched one finger to the crust and put the finger to her tongue.

It tasted of the sea. That was all she could have said of it. Not sweet, not heavy; no coins on her eyelids. Salt and cold and something under the salt, bright and wet and startled, as when you bite your tongue. It tasted like tears, which she supposed it was. It tasted of something living.

Beside her, Teo had gone still. Not the hold's stillness. Something else. His eyes were open, but he was not looking at anything on the ice.

"Teo."

"Wait." His voice was low. "Wait. There's—"

She waited. Up on the shore the singing went on, the tide coming home over the sand. The candles moved along the ice-rim like a slow river.

"There's someone," Teo said.

He did not move his hands. He was listening, as she had seen him listen in the Brinestacks to a tablet nine hundred years old, for a sound inside the roar, and it was there.

"It's a voice," he said. "Hester. A person. Not the gull. Not the sea. A *person*." His breath was coming fast. "One word. Over and over." He swallowed, and shaped it slowly, carefully, as you shape a word in a language you do not know. "*Aelith?* Like that. Up at the end. Like they're asking."

"What does it mean?"

"I don't know." He opened his eyes and looked at her. In the candlelight his face was wet. "I don't know what language it is. I've never heard it." He laughed, once, a sound with no breath in it. "I left the Order because I wanted to know. And there's someone in the salt asking a question, and I don't know what it is."

Hester looked past him, out over the black water, east, toward the Deep, where there was nothing at all, three hundred miles of nothing, the White going on under the stars to the end of the world.

The wind was coming off the water. She had not noticed it start. It came across the Tear out of the east in a long, slow, steady breath, and it was not the Sere, which was dry and smelled of nothing. This was wet. It smelled of weed and salt and something under the salt she had no word for. It moved the candle-flames along the shore all together, every one of them bowing west, and lifted the edge of Teo's grey shawl, and went on past them over the ice toward the city.

She knelt on the new ice with the wind in her face. Her bare hand was on the crust, flat, the fingers open. She left it there.

Behind them on the shore, the whole city was singing a hymn about the tide. Out on the black water, very faint, very far off, something answered it. It might have been a gull.
